# The floor

A correct lesson and a great lesson are not the same thing, and most of the
internet is neither. These nine tests are what separate them. A lesson that
fails any of them is not finished, however accurate it is.

Each test is written with the failing version beside it, because the failing
version is always the one that feels like it is working.

---

## 1. It changes what the reader can do, not what they know

**Great.** The reader finishes able to perform an operation they could not
perform before: price a spread against a stop, count swing levels at two
settings, compute the trades a filter costs them.

**Merely correct.** The reader finishes knowing a definition. Vocabulary is
not capability, and a lesson that transfers only vocabulary has taught
nothing that survives the week.

*Test: can you state what the reader can now do, in one sentence, starting
with a verb?*

## 2. It makes one claim, and the claim could have come out otherwise

**Great.** "The same twenty bars contain eight structure events, or two, or
none." That is checkable, and it could have been false.

**Merely correct.** "Market structure is important." Nothing could refute it,
so nothing is being claimed.

*Test: what observation would have made this lesson wrong?*

## 3. It shows the thing instead of describing it

**Great.** The sixty closes are printed in full. The rule is stated. The
reader can run it and get the same answer.

**Merely correct.** A marked-up chart, an assertion about what it means, and
no way to check either. This is what every competitor does, and it is why
their lessons are interchangeable.

*Test: could a reader reproduce every number without asking us anything?*

## 4. It earns one moment of surprise

**Great.** At least once, arithmetic the reader has just watched overturns
what they expected. RSI falls fifteen points with nobody buying less. Adding
confirmation bars stops costing you the move. Four consecutive bars get
marked in both directions at once.

**Merely correct.** Everything in it confirms what the reader already
assumed. That is a summary, not a lesson.

*Test: name the sentence where the reader's expectation breaks.*

## 5. It concedes, before the reader thinks to

**Great.** A bounds section that names what the lesson did **not** establish,
in the lesson's own voice, including the objections a sharp reader would
raise. This is what makes the claims it does keep worth believing.

**Merely correct.** Confidence throughout, and a disclaimer at the bottom
about financial advice. The reader can feel the difference immediately.

*Test: does any bound cost us something we would rather have kept?*

## 6. Disagreeing with it is expensive

**Great.** Because the inputs and the rule are both printed, a reader who
disagrees has to recompute. That is the correct price of disagreement.

**Merely correct.** A reader can wave it away, because there is nothing to
check. Anything you can dismiss without work has not taught you anything.

## 7. It knows its neighbours

**Great.** It spends a named result from an earlier lesson, and sets up one
specific question the next lesson answers. Lessons 32 to 36 measure one
sixty-bar series, each adding a column, and the reader is told so.

**Merely correct.** It could be read in any order. That is a blog post with a
number on it.

*Test: what does this lesson spend, and what does it leave owed?*

## 8. Every sentence does work

**Great.** No sentence restates the heading, announces what the lesson will
cover, or exists to fill a paragraph. Cut anything that survives its own
deletion.

**Merely correct.** "In this lesson we will explore." "As we have seen."
"It is important to understand that." Every one of these is a sentence
apologising for the next one.

## 9. It ends where the reader has to act

**Great.** Three problems, each doable tonight, each on the reader's own
instrument, each producing exactly one number. The third collects a base
rate, because a number with nothing to compare it against is not a result.

**Merely correct.** A summary, a key-takeaways box, and a link to the next
lesson. Nothing the reader does differently tomorrow.

---

## The one-line version

**A lesson is finished when a reader who disagrees with it has to do
arithmetic, and a reader who agrees with it has something to do tonight.**
