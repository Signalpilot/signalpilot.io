Algorithmic Execution: Stop Giving Away Free Money

Reading time ~17 min • Module 2: The Cost of Trading •

You have a 70% win rate and a 3R average, and your account is up 10%.

The difference is execution: the gap between the price you chose and the price you got, charged on every trade whether it wins or loses. Whether that gap is a rounding error or the whole of your edge comes down to one division, which the box further down does for you.

What your entry method costs

How a trader enters matters as much as what a trader enters. Market orders buy you certainty of fill and charge you the spread for it. Limit orders cost you some fills and save you that spread. Over a year, on a high trade count, the difference between the two is not a rounding error — work out your own number before you assume it is.

Part 1: What Execution Cost Actually Is

The Gap Between the Price You Chose and the Price You Got

Every trade has two prices: the one you decided on and the one you got. The gap between them is slippage. It is not a fee, because nobody bills you for it and it never appears on a statement as a line item — which is exactly why it goes unexamined for years.

It comes from four places, and they are worth separating because each has a different fix:

  • The spread. A market order buys at the ask and sells at the bid, so you pay the spread on the way in and again on the way out. This is the only component you can remove outright: use limit orders and accept that some of them will not fill.
  • Depth. Your order is larger than the size resting at the touch, so it walks up the book. Smaller clips fix this. Better timing does not.
  • Timing. The first fifteen minutes carry the widest spreads and the thinnest resting size, because the overnight gap is still being priced. The last fifteen are the opposite: the closing auction concentrates enormous volume and spreads there are usually tight. What the close has is speed, so the risk is being run over rather than spread-gouged. Two different problems, two different fixes.
  • Urgency. A panic exit takes whatever is there. A stop placed in advance takes the same loss without the extra cost of deciding under pressure.

Here is why this matters more than it looks. Execution cost is charged on every trade, win or lose, and it scales with how often you trade. Your edge is also earned per trade, but only pays out on the ones that work. A strategy with a genuine edge and a high trade count can hand the entire edge back to the market through nothing but the way it is entered and exited. What follows is one trader auditing exactly that.

Part 2: One Trader's Slippage Audit

Sarah's $14,237 Execution Wake-Up Call

Trader: Sarah Martinez (composite example), 29, day trader from Miami, FL
Timeframe: Q1-Q4 2024 (12 months)
Account Size: $85,000
Trading Volume: ~380 trades/year
Problem: Profitable strategy, but in Q1 slippage was destroying 33% of potential returns

⚠️ The Before: Execution Ignorance (Q1 2024)

Sarah's journal said one thing and her statement said another, and the gap was $14,237 across a quarter of about 90 trades. Two warnings before you read the audit below. First, the strategy assumptions in the original version of this story — a 65% win rate at +2.4R average over 380 trades a year — work out to more than 900R in twelve months, which no discretionary trader achieves; treat the expected-P&L column as illustrative rather than as a target. Second, the audit table double-counts: the first-fifteen-minutes trades are a subset of the market-order entries, so their slippage appears in both rows. The total is therefore overstated. What the table does correctly is the part worth copying — it separates the cost by cause, which is what tells you which fix to apply.

Phase 1: The Slippage Audit (April 2024)

After a frustrating Q1 where her account didn't match her journal's expected performance, Sarah's trading mentor suggested conducting a slippage audit. She analyzed her last 90 trades in detail:

Sarah's Q1 2024 Slippage Audit: Hidden Execution Costs
Execution Method Trades Avg Slippage Cost Per Trade Q1 Total Cost Issue
Market Orders (Entry) 78 0.18% -$76 -$5,928 Instant gratification, paid the spread
Market Orders (Exit) 90 0.14% -$59 -$5,310 Panic exits, bad fills
First 15 Min Trading 22 0.31% -$131 -$2,882 Wide spreads, volatility
Commissions 90 — -$1.30 -$117 $0.65 per side
TOTAL EXECUTION COSTS (Q1): -$14,237 —
Expected P&L (based on journal): $43,417 —
Actual P&L (after execution): $29,180 —
Execution Destroyed: 32.8% of potential profit!

🚨 The Shocking Discovery

Sarah's execution costs in Q1: -$14,237

  • Market order entries: -$5,928 (avg 0.18% slippage)
  • Market order exits: -$5,310 (avg 0.14% slippage)
  • First 15-min trading: -$2,882 (avg 0.31% slippage from wide spreads)
  • Commissions: -$117

What the audit established, and what it did not: it established that a material amount of money was leaving through the fill price rather than through the strategy, and it established where — overwhelmingly through market orders, and worst of all in the opening minutes. It did not establish an annual figure. Extrapolating one quarter to a year assumes the next three look like the first, which is exactly the assumption the audit exists to stop you making about your own trading.

Phase 2: What She Changed — and Why No Results Follow

The usual version of this story reports the next three quarters in detail: slippage down to 0.03%, execution cost cut by 80%, tens of thousands added to the year, an account nearly tripled, and a closing line converting all of it into an hourly rate for learning execution basics. None of that follows here, and the reason is worth stating plainly.

Start with the premise. A 65% win rate at +2.4R average, over 380 trades, is +912R in a year. A very good discretionary year is a few dozen R. The strategy the case study assumes does not exist, so every figure computed downstream of it — expected P&L, execution cost as a share of it, the annual saving — is arithmetic on a fiction. There is also a double count inside the Q1 audit itself: the 22 first-fifteen-minutes trades are a subset of the 78 market-order entries, so their slippage is charged twice in the same total.

What survives, and it is the useful part, is the diagnosis rather than the recovery. Sarah went looking for a gap between her journal and her statement, found it in the fill prices, and could then attribute it to specific causes. That is a procedure any reader can run tonight, and unlike a performance record it does not depend on believing anything about her.

The changes worth copying

  • Limit orders instead of market orders, resting at the bid to buy and the offer to sell — and note carefully that this means joining the queue, not crossing it. A buy limit placed at the offer executes immediately at the offer, which is a market order wearing a different name and pays the full spread. If your limit fills instantly every time, you are not saving the spread; you are paying it with extra steps.
  • Stop-limit rather than stop-market, with the limit set at a price you would genuinely accept. Understand the trade you are making: a stop-limit protects you from a terrible fill in a cascade and, in exactly that cascade, may not fill at all and leave you holding the position. That is not automatically better. It is a different risk, and which one you want depends on whether the worse outcome for you is a bad price or an open position.
  • Splitting larger entries into two or three clips, which addresses depth — the only one of the four causes that clip size actually fixes.
  • Recording the mid-price at the moment of the decision alongside the fill, on every trade. Without that column there is nothing to audit.

One change from the original list that is a mistake

She also "switched to IBKR Lite for commission-free trading". For a trader whose entire diagnosis is execution quality, that is the wrong direction. Lite routes orders to wholesalers for payment for order flow; Pro routes them through the broker's own smart router and charges a commission for it. The commission line in her audit was $117 across a quarter. Giving up routing control to save $117 while trying to fix a five-figure execution problem trades the small visible cost for the large invisible one, which is the exact mistake this lesson exists to correct.

The line the lesson never drew, and it decides how much of this applies to you

Almost everything above matters in proportion to whether your order is larger than the size resting at the touch. If you are buying 200 shares of SPY, you are not walking the book, icebergs are irrelevant, and your entire execution cost is the spread — one cent — which a resting limit order removes and nothing else does. If your order is several times the displayed size, or you trade something that quotes in nickels rather than pennies, then depth, timing and clip size all start to bite. Work out which of those two traders you are before deciding how much of this lesson is about you. The audit in Part 1 answers that question directly, which is why it comes first.

🎯 One ratio decides whether this lesson is about you

The section above says execution matters in proportion to your order size against the resting book. There is a second and larger factor it does not name, and it is a single division you can do right now: the spread divided by your stop distance.

Crossing the spread on the way in and again on the way out costs you one full spread per round trip. In a penny-wide instrument that is one cent a share, whatever your size. Your risk on that trade is your stop distance, also per share. So the cost of crossing, expressed in the only unit that matters, is:

execution cost per trade, in R  =  round-trip spread  ÷  stop distance

Run it against a penny spread and a year of Sarah's trade count:

stop per share   cost as a share of 1R   drag at 380 trades a year
         $2.00                   0.5 %                       1.9 R
         $1.00                     1 %                       3.8 R
         $0.50                     2 %                       7.6 R
         $0.20                     5 %                        19 R
         $0.05                    20 %                        76 R

Read the top and the bottom rows next to each other, because they are two different occupations. A swing trader risking $2.00 a share gives up 1.9 R a year to crossing the spread — against a good discretionary year of perhaps forty, that is noise, and this lesson is a tidy-up. A scalper risking five cents a share gives up 76 R a year, which no edge survives. That trader does not have an execution problem to improve; the arithmetic says the strategy cannot clear its own costs, and no amount of order-type discipline changes it.

Two things follow. First, work out your own number before deciding how much of this lesson applies to you — the answer ranges from "barely" to "fatal" and nothing else on the page tells you which. Second, notice that the ratio has two levers and only one of them is about execution. Resting a limit order removes the numerator. Trading a wider stop, or a longer timeframe, shrinks the whole fraction faster than any routing decision ever will.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

🎮 Execution Quality Check

1. Your strategy shows a small positive edge in backtest, but live results are consistently worse. Execution costs are the suspect. Where should you look first?

A) Commission, since it is the only cost you are billed for
B) Slippage — at a fraction of a percent per trade it compounds across every trade you take
C) Exchange fees, which vary by venue
D) Nowhere — a live-versus-backtest gap is always regime change
Correct: B. Slippage is the cost you are not invoiced for, which is exactly why it goes unexamined: it is buried in the fill price rather than itemised on a statement. Across a hundred trades a fraction of a percent each way removes a large share of the return. A and C are real costs but they are visible and usually already in the backtest. D is possible, but you rule out the mechanical explanation before reaching for the market-structure one.

2. You need to buy a position larger than the visible size at the offer. What does the lesson recommend?

A) Send it as one market order and accept the fill
B) Split it into tranches, and use an iceberg so the book never shows your full size
C) Wait for the close, when volume is highest
D) Cross the spread aggressively so you are filled before anyone reacts
Correct: B. A large visible order is information, and the book reprices against it before you are filled. Splitting into tranches and hiding the remainder keeps the size out of view. A is the mistake this section exists to prevent. D misreads urgency as edge — paying up guarantees the worst price in the range. C is half-right about volume but the close is one of the widest-spread windows of the day.

3. Which window is worst for a size-sensitive entry?

A) The first and last fifteen minutes of the session
B) Mid-morning, once the opening range has resolved
C) The lunch period, when volume thins
D) It makes no difference if you use limit orders
Correct: A. The open and the close carry the widest spreads and the most aggressive participants, so an order placed there pays the most to transact even though volume is high. C is the tempting answer because thin volume sounds dangerous, and it does matter for very large size, but the spread cost is smaller than at the bell. D is wrong in a specific way: a limit order controls your price but not whether you get filled, and in a fast window an unfilled limit is its own cost.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Measure your slippage rather than assuming a figure: the 0.1-0.5% per trade quoted in most places describes illiquid instruments or oversized orders. In a penny-spread ETF at retail size it is a small fraction of that, and almost all of it is the spread on a market order
  • Market orders = instant gratification: Limit orders = better fills (patience saves money)
  • Scale into positions: Split large orders into 2-3 tranches for better average price
  • The opening and the close are both risky, for opposite reasons, and they are routinely conflated. The first fifteen minutes genuinely has the widest spreads: the overnight gap is being priced, and market makers quote defensively until they can see what the day is. The last fifteen does not — the closing auction concentrates enormous volume, and spreads are usually tight. What the close has is speed: price can travel a long way in a minute on imbalance flow, so a market order there is more likely to be run over than to be spread-gouged. Your own audit below will show you which of the two is actually costing you
  • Iceberg orders hide size, where your broker offers them: most retail platforms do not, and at retail size it rarely matters — if your order is smaller than what is resting at the touch, there is no hand to hide
  • Track effective spread cost: Measure (fill price - mid price) / mid price for every trade
  • Spread ÷ stop distance is the whole diagnosis: a penny spread against a $2.00 stop costs 0.5 % of one R and 1.9 R across a year of 380 trades; against a five-cent stop it costs 20 % of one R and 76 R a year. The first trader has a tidy-up, the second has a strategy that cannot clear its own costs. Compute yours before deciding how much of this lesson is about you

Implementation Goal: Implement these execution improvements over 30 days. Track slippage before/after. Compare the before and after in the units you measured, not in percentages someone else assumed. The honest arithmetic is simple: whatever you save per round trip, multiply by your trade count. If that number is small relative to your annual result, this lesson has told you something valuable — that your problem is elsewhere.

Execution cost is the one part of your results that is charged on every trade regardless of whether the idea was right. That is what makes it worth measuring, and it is also why the answer might be that yours is already small. Find your number first. Then decide how much of the rest of this applies.
