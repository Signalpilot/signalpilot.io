When Your Broker Acts Without You

Almost everything in this curriculum is about decisions you make. This lesson is about the handful of moments when someone else makes one for you, in your account, without asking.

Your broker can sell your position. The clearing house can hand you a hundred shares you never ordered. A rule you have never read can stop you trading at all. The exchange can halt the stock while your stop sits underneath it. None of these is rare, all of them are governed by published rules, and every one of them is arithmetic you can run in advance.

🚨 Why these rules exist

These rules are not out to get you. They exist because your broker lends you money and wants it back, and because an exchange with no brakes produces prices nobody can trade against. What ruins accounts is not the rules; it is meeting them for the first time at the worst possible moment, in a position sized as though they did not exist.

Part 1: The Price Your Broker Starts Selling At

When you buy on margin you put up part of the value and borrow the rest. Regulation T caps the initial borrow at half. After that you must keep your equity above a maintenance requirement — 25% is the regulatory floor, and most brokers set a house requirement of 30% or more, higher still on volatile names.

The call price falls straight out of that. Your loan does not shrink when the stock falls; only your equity does. So:

loan          =  position value  x  (1 - your equity share)
call value    =  loan  /  (1 - maintenance requirement)
fall to call  =  1  -  call value / position value

Worked once, slowly. You buy $20,000 of stock with $10,000 of your own money. Your loan is $10,000. Your broker’s maintenance requirement is 30%, so your equity must stay above 30% of whatever the position is worth. The position can fall to $10,000 ÷ 0.70 = $14,286 before that fails. That is a 28.6% fall. Below it, the broker asks for money, and if it does not arrive quickly they sell — at their choosing, not yours.

💡 Read the bottom two rows

At 2.86 times leverage against a 30% house requirement, a 7.1% fall brings the call. That is an ordinary week. And the bottom-right cell is not a rounding artefact: at three times leverage against a 35% requirement you are below the threshold the moment you open, which is why a broker with a 35% requirement will simply refuse the trade.

Notice also what the table does not contain: your entry, your thesis, or your stop. The call price is a property of your borrowing, not of your idea.

Why a stop does not save you here. A stop is an instruction to trade at a price. A maintenance call is an instruction to you, and if it goes unmet the liquidation is the broker’s, executed on their timetable, in whichever position is easiest to sell. In a gap the stop and the call arrive together and the stop is the slower of the two.

Part 2: Assignment — Shares You Did Not Order

If you are short an option, the person on the other side may exercise it, and American-style options can be exercised on any business day, not only at expiry. You find out overnight. The position simply appears.

Most early exercise is not random, and one case dominates: a short call on a stock about to pay a dividend. The holder of a call receives no dividend. If they exercise the day before the stock goes ex-dividend they own the shares in time to collect it. They will do that whenever the dividend is worth more than the time value they give up by exercising early.

📊 The one comparison that predicts it

For a short call going into an ex-dividend date, compare the dividend per share against the remaining extrinsic value of that call. If the dividend is larger, assume you will be assigned. This is not a rule of thumb; it is the arithmetic of the person on the other side, and they have every reason to run it.

The practical consequence for a covered call: you keep the premium, your shares are gone, and the dividend you were counting on goes to someone else.

Pin risk is the expiry version. A short option sitting almost exactly at the strike into the close may or may not be exercised, and you will not know until after the market has shut. Monday can open with a position you did not choose and cannot hedge. Lesson 41 covers what dealers do around those strikes; the defensive point here is narrower: an option within a few cents of its strike at Friday’s close is a coin flip you are holding over a weekend. Closing it costs a few cents of spread. That is the cheapest insurance in this lesson.

Part 3: The Pattern Day Trader Rule

In a US margin account, four or more day trades — buying and selling the same security on the same day — within five rolling business days makes you a pattern day trader. From that point you must hold $25,000 in the account. Fall below and day trading is restricted until you top up.

Three details that catch people, none of them obvious from the name:

  • It is rolling, not weekly. There is no Monday reset. The window is the last five business days, always.
  • It counts round trips, not orders. Scaling out of one position in three pieces is one day trade, not three. Scaling into two separate positions and closing both is two.
  • It applies to margin accounts. A cash account is not subject to it, but is subject to settlement — you cannot re-use unsettled proceeds, which limits you in a different way.

The strategic point is not how to dodge the rule. It is that a rule which caps you at three day trades a week is telling you something about position size. A trader with $8,000 who wants four day trades a week has picked a style their account cannot fund; Lesson 24 does the same arithmetic for futures, where the constraint is margin per contract rather than a regulation.

Part 4: Halts, Bands and Corporate Actions

Volatility halts. US markets run limit up-limit down bands around a rolling average price. Trade outside the band for fifteen seconds and the stock halts for five minutes. The band is percentage-based and widens for cheaper stocks and narrows for the most liquid ones; it also doubles in the last twenty-five minutes of the session.

What matters for you is what a halt does to your orders: nothing executes during it, and the reopening is an auction, not a continuation. A stop resting inside the halted range does not protect you, because there is no trading for it to trigger against. It becomes a market order into the reopening print, which is exactly the moment the spread is widest.

Corporate actions. A split multiplies your share count and divides the price; your resting orders are usually adjusted, but not always, and not always well. A special dividend, a spin-off or a reverse split can all move the printed price without anything happening to the company’s value. If you trade on price levels, a corporate action can trip every one of them at once for reasons that have nothing to do with supply and demand.

⚠️ The general shape

Every item in this lesson has the same structure: an external party acts on your position under a published rule, at a moment you did not choose. You cannot prevent any of them. You can compute all of them in advance, and being able to is the difference between an inconvenience and a catastrophe.

Part 5: Where Signal Pilot Fits

Honestly: nowhere in Parts 1 to 3. Margin requirements, assignment and the PDT rule are properties of your account and your broker’s agreement, not of the chart, and no indicator has any view on them. The arithmetic above is the whole tool.

Part 4 is the exception, because a halt and a reopening are order flow events. Volume Oracle shows what is actually trading into a reopening auction, which is the difference between a print you can lean on and one that is a single order clearing a thin book. That is worth seeing before you decide whether the reopening price means anything.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: You hold $30,000 of stock, $12,000 of it borrowed. Your broker's maintenance requirement is 30%. Roughly what fall brings a maintenance call?

A) 30% — the requirement is 30%, so a 30% fall breaches it
B) About 43% — the position can fall to $12,000 ÷ 0.70 = $17,143
C) 60% — your equity is $18,000, so you can lose that much
D) It depends on your entry price

Correct: B. The loan is fixed at $12,000; the requirement says equity must exceed 30% of current value, so the position can fall to $12,000 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = $17,143, which is 42.9% below $30,000. A is the most common wrong answer and it confuses the requirement with the move — the two are different numbers. D is worth naming because it feels right and is not: two traders with the same loan have the same call price, whether one is up on the position and the other down.

Q2: You are short a call. The stock goes ex-dividend tomorrow, the dividend is $0.80, and the call has $0.30 of extrinsic value left. What should you expect?

A) Nothing — American options are only exercised at expiry
B) Likely assignment tonight, because the dividend exceeds the extrinsic value being given up
C) Assignment only if the stock closes above the strike by more than the dividend
D) The exchange will adjust the strike by the dividend amount

Correct: B. Exercising early throws away the option's remaining extrinsic value but captures the dividend. At $0.80 against $0.30 that trade is worth making, so expect it. A is simply false for American-style options, which is most single-stock options. D describes what happens for a special dividend or a split, not an ordinary one — ordinary dividends are not adjusted for, which is precisely why this exercise decision exists at all.

Q3: A stock you are long halts limit-down while you have a stop resting below the market. What happens to your stop?

A) It fills at your stop price, since the price passed through it
B) It is cancelled automatically by the exchange
C) It does not execute during the halt and becomes a market order into the reopening auction
D) It converts to a limit order at the halt price

Correct: C. A stop is triggered by trades, and during a halt there are none. When trading resumes it does so through an auction, and your triggered stop joins it as a market order at the widest spread of the day. This is the concrete reason Lesson 10 insists a stop is a risk tool rather than a risk guarantee: it does what it says only while there is a two-sided market to say it into.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Your margin call price is computable and it is closer than you think. Loan ÷ (1 − maintenance requirement) gives the value at which it fires. At 2.86x leverage against a 30% requirement, a 7.1% fall is enough.
  • The call price depends on your loan, not on your entry. Being up on a position does not move it.
  • A short call going into an ex-dividend date will usually be assigned if the dividend exceeds the option's remaining extrinsic value. That single comparison predicts most early assignment.
  • An option within a few cents of its strike at Friday's close is a weekend coin flip. Closing it costs a spread and removes the whole problem.
  • The PDT rule is rolling, counts round trips, and switches off at $25,000. Being caught by it is information about your position size, not an obstacle to route around.
  • A stop does not work through a halt, because a stop needs trades to trigger against. It becomes a market order into the reopening auction.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Find Your Own Numbers

Objective: Replace four assumptions with four figures from your own account. None of this takes longer than an evening and none of it requires a trade.

Part 1: Your call price. Open your account and write down your total position value and your margin loan. Find your broker's maintenance requirement — it is in the margin agreement, and the house requirement for volatile names is often listed separately. Compute loan ÷ (1 − requirement). That is the value at which selling starts. Then express it as a percentage fall from where you are now, and ask whether you have seen a fall that size in the last two years.

Part 2: Your assignment exposure. If you hold any short options, find the next ex-dividend date for each underlying and the dividend amount. Compare it to the extrinsic value left in the option. Anything where the dividend is larger is a position you should expect to lose this week.

Part 3: Your day trade count. Count your round trips over the last five business days. If the number is three or more and your balance is near $25,000, you are one trade from a restriction you did not plan for.

Part 4: Write the sentence. In one line: "If my account falls to ____, my broker sells before I do." Put it where you will see it when you are deciding how much to buy. That sentence is the whole of Part 1, and you cannot write it without doing the arithmetic.
