Is Your Edge Gone, Or Is This Just Variance?

Reading time ~18 min • System Evaluation •
Signal Pilot
Professional Trading Education
0%

Keep reading to mark this lesson complete

You are fourteen trades into a losing run. Nothing about the system has changed — same rules, same markets, same sizing, same you. And the question in front of you is the only one that actually matters at this point in a trading career: is the edge gone, or is this what a working edge feels like from the inside?

Almost nobody answers that with arithmetic. They answer it with how the last three weeks felt.

💸 Two ways to get this wrong, and they do not cost the same

Quit a working system inside its normal range and you pay for the whole edge — not the drawdown, but every trade you would have taken for the rest of your career with a method that worked. That bill is unbounded and you never see it, because you never find out what the abandoned system went on to do.

Keep a dead system because you cannot tell the difference and you pay a bleed, and the bleed is capped by your risk limits if you set them properly in Lesson 9.

Traders spend almost all of their worry on the second failure and almost none on the first, which is backwards. Both come from the same hole: nobody wrote down what a normal bad run looks like for their own numbers, so every bad run arrives looking like evidence.

🚨 Real Talk

The drawdown you are in right now is almost certainly not the worst one your system will hand you. If you have taken two hundred trades, the arithmetic in Part 2 says your deepest drawdown to date is around nine times your risk per trade, and there is a one-in-twenty chance it reaches sixteen. If that sentence is a surprise, you have been sizing against a number you never calculated.

🎯 What You’ll Master

By the end of this lesson, you’ll understand:

  • How to compute, from your win rate alone, the losing streak you should expect to meet — before you meet it
  • Why the honest answer to “has my edge degraded?” is usually “that is not knowable yet”, and how many trades it would take to know
  • How to separate the statistical question (is this outside my own distribution?) from the structural one (does the thing my edge feeds on still exist?)
  • How to write a stop-rule before the drawdown rather than during it, which is the only time you are qualified to write one

Part 1: What a Normal Bad Run Looks Like

Two numbers describe your system: the fraction of trades that win, and what a winner pays relative to what a loser costs. Everything in this lesson comes out of those two. Throughout, one unit of risk is written R — the amount you lose when a trade hits its stop. A system that wins 45% of the time and makes 2R on a winner has an expectancy of +0.35R per trade: 0.45 × 2 − 0.55 × 1. It is a good system. Most traders would sign for it.

Here is what that good system does to you along the way. The table gives the probability of meeting at least one losing streak of a given length somewhere inside two hundred trades.

Your win rate 5 losses 6 losses 7 losses 8 losses 10 losses
40%100%99%91%75%38%
45%99%93%76%53%20%
50%97%80%54%32%9%
55%88%60%33%17%4%

Read the 45% row again. Eight losses in a row is a coin flip. Not a rare event, not a red flag, not a sign the market changed — a coin flip, in a system with a healthy positive expectancy, over a sample most traders reach inside a year. Six in a row happens to 93 traders out of 100 running that exact system.

📊 Where these numbers come from

This is not a simulation and there is nothing to take on trust. The probability of never seeing a run of k losses in n trades is an exact calculation: track the probability of being in each “current streak length” state, step forward one trade at a time, and a win resets the state to zero while a loss advances it. Sum the states that never reached a run of k. Twenty lines of any language will reproduce every cell above, and you should reproduce the row for your own win rate rather than borrowing the one nearest to it.

Notice what the table does not depend on: the payoff. Streak length is a property of the win rate alone. That is why a high-payoff, low-win-rate system — the kind most trend followers run — is psychologically brutal to hold even when it is working perfectly. At a 35% win rate, a streak of ten is routine. The system is fine. The person holding it is the fragile part.

Part 2: The Drawdown You Have Not Met Yet

Streaks are the visible part. The number that actually threatens your account is the peak-to-trough drawdown, because a drawdown does not need consecutive losses — it accumulates through a stretch of losers interrupted by winners too small to repair them.

For the same system (45% win rate, 2R winners, 1R losers, +0.35R expectancy), here is the distribution of the worst drawdown encountered in two hundred trades, from 100,000 simulated runs:

How unlucky Worst drawdown in 200 trades At 1% risk per trade
Typical (median)9R−9% of account
One year in four12R−12%
One year in twenty16R−16%
One year in a hundred21R−21%

Method: 100,000 independent runs of 200 trades each, outcomes drawn at the stated win rate, random seed 51. The exact figures shift by a few tenths of an R with a different seed; the shape does not.

💡 The sentence worth keeping

A 16R drawdown in this system is not evidence of anything. It is the one-in-twenty outcome of a method that makes money. If your risk limits are set so that 16R forces you to stop, you have built a system that shuts itself off during a normal year, and you will experience that shutdown as proof the edge died.

This is where the sizing lesson stops being theoretical. Risk 1% per trade and a one-in-twenty year costs you 16% — unpleasant, survivable, recoverable. Risk 3% and the same year, the same trades, the same edge, costs you 48% and you need to almost double to get back. Nothing about the edge changed between those two traders. One of them will conclude the system is broken.

Part 3: How Long Until You Could Actually Tell

Now the harder question, and the one this lesson exists for. Suppose the edge really is gone. How many trades would it take before your results could establish that, rather than just look like it?

The answer follows from the spread of individual trade outcomes. In our system a trade returns either +2R or −1R, giving a standard deviation of 1.49R per trade against a mean of just +0.35R. The noise is four times the signal on every single trade. To separate two hypotheses at conventional confidence, you need roughly 7.85 × variance ÷ difference² trades:

Question you want answered Trades required At 4 trades a week
Has the edge gone to zero?1439 months
Has the edge halved?571Nearly 3 years
Has the edge weakened by a quarter?2,284Over a decade

Sit with the middle row. An edge that has genuinely halved takes something like three years of live trading to prove statistically. Which means that for the overwhelming majority of working traders, the sentence “my edge has been degrading lately” is not a finding. It is a feeling with a number attached to make it sound like a finding.

⚠️ What this does not license

“You can never prove it, so ignore your results” is the wrong conclusion and an expensive one. Statistical undetectability cuts both ways: it also means a halved edge will keep paying you badly for years while you wait for significance that never arrives. The correct response is not to stare harder at the P&L. It is to stop asking the equity curve a question it cannot answer, and start asking the structural question in Part 5 instead — which often can be answered in an afternoon.

Part 4: The Trader Who Quit at the Bottom

Devin Osei (composite example) — four years of records, an opening-range system on index futures, 46% win rate and roughly 1.9R on winners across 380 logged trades.

In the spring of his fourth year Devin took a run of six losses, then two small winners, then five more losses. Thirteen trades, eleven of them red, a drawdown of 11R. He had never seen worse. He cut his size by two thirds after the first six, which meant the two winners in the middle paid a third of what they should have. Four trades later he stopped trading the system and spent two months rebuilding it.

📉 What the record actually said

Logged trades380
Worst run11 of 13 red
Drawdown11R
At a 46% win rate, a six-loss streak arrives with about 92% probability inside 200 trades, and Devin had taken 380. His worst drawdown was 11R against a median expectation near 9R for a sample that size. Nothing he experienced was outside his own distribution. He did not have a broken system; he had a system, and this is what having one costs.

The two decisions, separated. Cutting size after six losses was not the error people assume it is — it is defensible as risk management, and if he had written it down in advance it would have been a rule rather than a flinch. The error was that cutting size was unplanned, which meant the recovery trades were undersized precisely when the system was doing its job. He converted a normal 11R drawdown into a longer one by his own hand, and then read the longer one as confirmation.

What he could not have known, and did not need to. Devin never established that his edge had degraded, because with 380 trades and that spread of outcomes he could not have. What he could have established in one evening, at any point during the drawdown, is whether the thing his system fed on was still there. He never looked.

Part 5: The Two Questions, Kept Apart

Everything above answers the statistical question, and the answer is mostly “wait longer than you are willing to wait.” That is why the second question carries almost all of the practical weight.

The statistical question The structural question
Are my results outside the distribution my own win rate and payoff imply? Does the market behaviour my edge feeds on still happen?
Answered by arithmetic on your journal. Answered by looking at the market, not at your P&L.
Needs 143 trades to detect a total collapse; 571 for a halving. Often answerable in a single evening.
Usually says: not yet. Usually says something.

The structural question is specific to what your system actually exploits, and that is the point — you have to name the mechanism before you can check whether it survived. Some examples of naming it properly:

  • An opening-range system feeds on the first half hour producing a directional imbalance that persists. The check: has the distribution of first-30-minute range relative to daily range changed? That is measurable from free data in an evening, and Lesson 50 covers what it should look like.
  • A liquidity-sweep system feeds on stops clustering at obvious levels and getting taken. The check: are sweeps still reversing at the rate they did, or are they continuing through? Count them.
  • A mean-reversion system feeds on a regime that mean-reverts. When the regime turns trending, the edge has not degraded — it has been switched off, and it will switch back on. Lesson 49 is the whole treatment of this, and it is the most common reason a working system stops paying.
  • Anything with a capacity or crowding component feeds on other participants not doing what you are doing. Lesson 67 covers how that one dies, and it is the only failure mode on this list that does not come back.

✅ The reframe

“Is my edge gone?” is nearly unanswerable. “Is the thing my edge feeds on still happening?” is usually answerable this week. If the mechanism is intact and your results are inside your own distribution, you have a drawdown. If the mechanism is gone, you do not need statistical significance to act — you have a reason, and a reason beats a p-value you will never reach.

Part 6: The Rule You Write Before You Need It

You are not qualified to decide whether to abandon a system while you are down 11R in it. Nobody is. The only useful version of this decision is one made in advance, written down, and then obeyed.

A workable stop-rule has four parts, and the numbers in it come from Parts 1 to 3 rather than from how you feel:

  1. A drawdown threshold set beyond your own 95th percentile. For the system in this lesson that is past 16R, not at 8R. Below your own 95th percentile you are stopping on noise by construction.
  2. A structural trigger that does not need the threshold. “If the mechanism I named is measurably absent for two months, I stop regardless of P&L.” This is the clause that will actually fire, and it can fire while you are making money.
  3. A pre-committed size policy through the drawdown. Whatever you decide — hold size, or halve it at a named level — decide it now. Devin's real mistake was not the size cut; it was that the size cut was improvised.
  4. A restart condition. A rule that only stops is a rule for permanently shrinking your set of systems. Write what evidence brings it back, in the same sentence.
Part 7: Where Signal Pilot Fits

Being straight about this: no indicator tells you whether your edge is dead. That is a question about your record, and it is answered with the arithmetic above and nothing else. What the tools address is the structural half.

  • Harmonic Oscillator classifies the current regime as trending or mean-reverting. If your system feeds on mean reversion, this is the fastest read on whether the condition it needs is present — which is the difference between an edge that is switched off and one that is gone.
  • Janus Atlas marks sweeps and the structure around them. If your system feeds on sweeps reversing, the count of what happened after recent sweeps is the structural check from Part 5, done on the chart.
  • Volume Oracle shows the order flow context your setups form in. A setup that used to form on absorption and now forms on nothing is a structural change you can see before the statistics could ever confirm it.

None of these produces a verdict on your system, and any tool claiming to would be selling you certainty that the sample size does not support. They shorten the structural question from a research project to a chart read. The arithmetic stays your job.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: Your system wins 45% of trades at 2R. You have just taken eight losses in a row. What has this told you about your edge?

A) The edge is likely broken — eight consecutive losses is far outside normal for a 45% system
B) Nothing can ever be learned from results, so the streak is meaningless in every sense
C) Essentially nothing — a streak of eight arrives in roughly half of all 200-trade samples at this win rate
D) The edge is intact, because a losing streak is always just variance

Correct: C. At a 45% win rate the probability of meeting at least one eight-loss streak inside 200 trades is about 53%. It is the single most common surprising fact in this lesson, and it is exact rather than estimated. Note why D is also wrong: a losing streak is not always variance, it is just that this particular one carries no information. A streak of sixteen would be a different conversation. The skill is knowing which streak lengths are ordinary for your own win rate, which is the whole point of computing the table before you need it.

Q2: You suspect your edge has roughly halved. Trading four times a week, about how long would it take to establish that statistically?

A) About a month — a halved edge shows up quickly in the equity curve
B) About six months, the usual review period
C) Close to three years, which is why the equity curve is the wrong place to ask
D) It is detectable immediately if you use a rolling 20-trade average

Correct: C. Roughly 571 trades, which at four a week is close to three years. The reason is the ratio of noise to signal: individual trade outcomes have a standard deviation around 1.49R against an expectancy of 0.35R, and halving the expectancy leaves a difference that small samples cannot see. D is worth naming as a specific trap — a rolling 20-trade average of a 1.49R-standard-deviation process swings so wildly that it will show you an edge collapsing and recovering several times a year in a system where nothing has changed at all.

Q3: Your mean-reversion system has underperformed for four months. Which check is worth doing first?

A) Re-optimise the parameters on the last four months of data
B) Measure whether the market has been in a trending regime over those four months
C) Run a significance test on the last four months of trades
D) Halve your size and continue until the drawdown threshold is hit

Correct: B. The structural question is answerable and the statistical one is not, so do the answerable one first. Four months of trades is nowhere near 143, so C returns “inconclusive” by construction. A is the worst of the four: re-optimising on the period that hurt you is how a working system gets fitted to a regime that is about to end, which is the failure Lesson 32 is built around. D is not wrong so much as premature — it may well be the right action, but only if you wrote it down before the drawdown started rather than choosing it now.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Streak length depends only on your win rate, not your payoff. At 45%, eight straight losses inside 200 trades is a coin flip — compute the row for your own number and keep it somewhere you will see it during a drawdown.
  • Your deepest drawdown so far is probably not your deepest. For a 45% / 2R system over 200 trades the median worst drawdown is around 9R and the one-in-twenty case is 16R. Size so that the one-in-twenty year is survivable, because it is a normal year.
  • A halved edge takes years to prove. Around 571 trades, near three years at four a week. “My edge has been degrading” is almost never a finding at retail sample sizes, however strongly it is felt.
  • Ask the structural question instead. Name what your system feeds on and check whether it is still happening. That is usually answerable in an evening, and a reason is worth more than a significance test you will never reach.
  • Write the stop-rule before the drawdown, with a threshold past your own 95th percentile, a structural trigger that can fire independently, a pre-committed size policy, and a restart condition. During a drawdown you are the least qualified person to write it.
  • A regime that switched off is not an edge that died. Mean reversion stops paying in a trending market and comes back; crowding and capacity do not come back. Lessons 49 and 67 are the two sides of that.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Build Your Own Normal

Objective: Replace “this feels bad” with two numbers you calculated yourself. Everything here uses your existing journal and a spreadsheet.

Part 1: Your two inputs. From your last 100 closed trades, compute your win rate and your average winner divided by your average loser, both in R. If you have fewer than 100 trades, use what you have and write the count next to the figures — a win rate off 40 trades is a rough estimate and should be labelled as one.

Part 2: Your streak row. Compute the probability of at least one losing streak of 5, 6, 7, 8 and 10 in 200 trades at your win rate, using the state method described in Part 1. A spreadsheet with one column per streak-length state and one row per trade will do it. Write the row on a card.

Part 3: Your drawdown distribution. Simulate 10,000 runs of 200 trades at your win rate and payoff and record the worst drawdown in each. Take the median and the 95th percentile. Then check your actual worst drawdown to date against those two numbers.

Part 4: Name the mechanism. In one sentence, what market behaviour does your system feed on? Then write the specific measurement that would tell you whether it is still happening, and how long that measurement takes. If you cannot name the mechanism in one sentence, that is the most valuable thing this exercise will tell you, and it is worth more than the arithmetic.

Part 5: The rule. Write the four-part stop-rule from Part 6 with your own numbers in it. Date it. The date matters, because the whole value of the rule is that it was written when you were not in trouble.

You now have the arithmetic that separates a bad run from a broken system. The next lesson takes the other half: what volatility does to the size of that bad run, and why the same system in a higher-volatility regime hands you a drawdown that looks like a different system entirely.
