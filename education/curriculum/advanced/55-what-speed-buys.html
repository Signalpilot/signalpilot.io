They trade in microseconds. You trade in seconds.

By the time you click "buy," they've already canceled their quote, moved the price, and taken the other side of your trade at a better price.

You can't beat them at speed. But here's the thing: you don't have to.

Part 1: The Microsecond Arms Race

Welcome to the Speed Wars

Picture this: You're sitting at home, watching SPY trade at $520.00.

You see a setup. You click "buy market order."

50ms Timeline:

  • 0ms: You click buy @ $520.01 ask
  • 5ms: Order hits router (WiFi adds 2-5ms)
  • 20ms: Reaches broker (HFTs detected pressure 15ms ago)
  • 45ms: Reaches exchange—HFTs canceled $520.01, repriced to $520.03
  • 50ms: You fill @ $520.03. Slippage: $0.02/share

On 1,000 shares = $20. Multiply by billions = HFT business model.

💡 The Aha Moment

HFTs aren't predicting the future. They're just faster than you.

By the time your order arrives, they've already reacted to information you haven't seen yet.

HFT Latency vs. Retail

👤 You (Retail)

  • Home internet: 20-50ms
  • Broker routing: 10-30ms
  • Decision time: 500-5000ms
  • Total: 50-200ms

🤖 HFT

  • Co-located servers: 0.5-2ms
  • Direct fiber: microseconds
  • Algorithmic decisions: microseconds
  • Total: 0.5-2ms

Two orders of magnitude, give or take an order of magnitude. The gap is not worth measuring precisely, because nothing you do should depend on where in that range it falls.

Part 2: The $12,750 Lesson—Michael's Market Order Massacre

Michael's $12,750 Market Order Massacre

Michael Chen (composite example), Seattle, WA — $1,800,000 account, June 2023.

The Trade: Bought 15,000 NVDA shares at market @ 9:32 AM (2 min after open).

📉 The Instant $12,750 Loss

Expected Fill $410.50
Actual Fill $411.35
Slippage -$12,750

Where the $12,750 Actually Went

Two different things happened inside that fill, and it matters which is which, because only one of them is about high-frequency trading at all.

  • Most of it was his own size walking the book. The $410.50 on his screen was the best offer, and the best offer is a quantity as well as a price — typically a few hundred shares in a $400 stock, and thinner still two minutes after the open. A 15,000-share market order was never going to fill at the top of book. It consumes every offer in front of it and stops at whatever price clears the last share. The "expected fill" of $410.50 was never available to an order that size, and calling the difference slippage misattributes a cost he chose when he sized the order.
  • Some of it was reaction to the sweep. A 15,000-share order does not arrive everywhere at once. It reaches the nearest venue first, and the execution there is public. Faster participants read that print and reprice or pull their offers on the other venues before the rest of the order gets there. That part is genuinely a speed effect, and it is the mechanism the rest of this lesson is about — but it is a fraction of the total, not the whole story.

How to tell the two apart on your own fills. Ask your broker for the execution detail: every venue, every price, every timestamp. Then compare the volume-weighted price you actually paid against the volume-weighted price of the resting offers at the moment you sent the order — the depth you consumed. That difference is the part you caused. Anything worse than that is the part that reacted to you. Most retail traders who do this exercise for the first time find the split is not close: the depth term dominates.

Which points at the actual fix, and it is not about outrunning anyone. Michael's error was sending 15,000 shares as one instruction. Splitting it into pieces the book can absorb, using limit orders that rest rather than cross, and doing it at a time of day when the book is deeper are all cheap and available to anyone. What none of them do is make you fast.

Two things you will read about this kind of fill, both wrong. The first is a counterfactual: he "should have" worked it as a twelve-minute iceberg and would have filled at $410.62, saving $10,950. Nobody can know that. The alternative order never existed, and the book it would have traded against would have been a different book, because his own order is part of what made this one. What can be known in advance is narrower and more useful: the depth term is arithmetic, and an order sized to the visible book pays less of it.

The second is that faster participants saw his order before it reached the exchange. Nobody sees your order in flight. What they see is the print it makes when part of it executes somewhere, and how quickly they can act on that public information before the rest of it arrives. The distinction matters because it names the defence: not speed, but not announcing yourself.

💡 Michael's Lesson

An order larger than the book in front of it pays for the difference. That cost is yours, it is predictable, and it does not require anyone to be faster than you.

Part 3: How HFTs Actually Make Money

Strategy 1: Market Making (The "Fair" One)

Let's be honest: Not all HFT is evil.

Market makers provide liquidity. They quote both sides of the market and profit from the spread.

Here's how it works:

Example (100 shares): Bid $520.00, Ask $520.01. Both sides fill = $1.00 spread + $0.40 rebate = $1.40. Scale it across thousands of symbols: 1M round-turns/day = $1.4M/day. Tiny edge, massive scale.

🎯 Why This Matters to You

Market makers want balanced flow (50% buys, 50% sells).

When flow becomes toxic (informed traders), they widen spreads or pull quotes entirely.

Ever seen bid/ask spread suddenly jump from $0.01 to $0.10? That's HFTs stepping back.

Strategy 2: Latency Arbitrage (The Controversial One)

This is where it gets uncomfortable.

The popular version of this is that fast firms see your order coming before it reaches the exchange. They do not, and no mechanism exists that would let them: an order in transit is not public. What is public is the execution it makes when part of it lands. A large order routed to several venues arrives at the closest one first, prints there, and that print is information a faster participant can act on before the remainder of your order arrives at the others. Here is the sequence:

Scenario: You buy 10K SPY, sweeping several venues. 0ms: the first slice executes on Exchange A and prints publicly. 0.5ms: a faster participant reads the print and cancels its $520.01 offers on the other venues. 1ms: it buys ahead of the rest of your order on Exchange B. 2ms: the remainder of your order fills higher.

Two things about the label. This is usually called front-running, and legally it is not: front-running means trading ahead of a customer order you have a duty to, which is a broker's offence, not a stranger's. What this is, is latency arbitrage — acting on public information faster than the person who created it. Whether that should be permitted is a genuine argument; whether it is the same thing as front-running is not. Getting it right matters because the defence is different: nothing stops someone reading a public print, but plenty stops you from making one that announces the rest of your order.

Where to read more

Michael Lewis's Flash Boys is the popular account, and it is where most of what people believe about this comes from — including the front-running label the paragraph above just took apart. Read it for the mechanism and the history, and check the labels against the SEC's own filings.

Strategy 3: Quote Stuffing (The Shady One)

This is where HFT crosses into manipulation territory.

Quote stuffing: flooding a venue with orders and cancelling them, to slow competitors or to disguise intent. Two things to keep straight about it. It is a prosecuted offence rather than a routine practice, and the enforcement record is against individual proprietary traders and bank desks rather than against market making as an activity. And you cannot detect it from a retail screen: most orders in US equities are cancelled rather than filled, so a book that flickers and cancels heavily is the ordinary state of the market, not evidence of anything. Flickering prices are what a modern order book looks like.

Part 4: The Payment for Order Flow Scandal

Your "Free" Broker Is Selling You to HFTs

Robinhood. Webull. E*TRADE. Most zero-commission brokers do it, and they disclose it — the SEC requires quarterly reports naming who buys the flow and what they pay. Check your own broker's: the numbers there are real, unlike most of the numbers quoted about them.

Payment for Order Flow (PFOF): your broker routes your order to a wholesaler, which pays for the privilege. Be precise about the mechanism, because the usual description is wrong: the wholesaler executes the order against its own inventory. It is not shown your order and then allowed to trade ahead of it on an exchange, which would be front-running — a different act, and a prohibited one.

How PFOF Works (And Why You're the Product)

Example (100 shares SPY): the broker is paid on the order of $0.002 a share for routing it, so about $0.20 on this trade. The wholesaler fills you at or just inside the national best offer and hopes to unwind the other side better than that. What you give up is not a penny a share — the whole market is only a penny wide — it is the chance to have rested at the bid and been paid the spread rather than paying part of it. For scale on the industry rather than on your order: Citadel Securities booked about $7.5B of net trading revenue in 2022 across all its market-making businesses, and Robinhood took about $814M in transaction-based revenue that year.

🚨 The "Best Execution" Lie

Brokers claim they provide "best execution" and "price improvement", and on marketable retail orders both are usually real and are disclosed quarterly.

The narrower and truer complaint: you are filled, reliably and slightly better than the quote, and you are never the one resting at the bid collecting the spread. That is a genuine cost, and it is the one this lesson can put a number on. It is also much smaller than the versions that circulate, for the reason the next section gives.

What Payment for Order Flow Actually Costs You

The figure you will meet looks like this: a trader doing 140,000 shares a month measures 2.3 cents a share of slippage at a payment-for-order-flow broker against 0.8 cents at a direct-routing one, and saves $14,000 over ten months. Those numbers cannot be right, and it is worth seeing exactly why, because the error is the same one that makes most published slippage figures useless.

SPY's spread is a penny. One cent, from bid to offer, essentially all day. If you buy at the offer and sell at the bid you pay that penny, and that is the maximum a retail-size marketable order can lose to the spread. A claim of 2.3 cents per share of slippage is a claim of more than twice the entire width of the market. It is not a large number, it is an impossible one.

So what does PFOF cost? Less than the story implied, and in a different currency. Wholesalers who buy retail order flow generally fill small marketable orders at or inside the national best bid and offer — the price improvement is real, it is disclosed, and it is usually a fraction of a cent per share. The costs that are real look like this:

  • You cannot post. The routing model that pays your broker is built around filling you, not around letting you sit on the bid and be filled by someone else. Posting is where the spread stops being a cost and starts being income, and giving that up costs more over a year than the fill quality does.
  • You do not choose the venue. That matters little on 200 shares of SPY and a great deal on anything illiquid, on anything at the open, and on any order large enough that where it lands changes what it pays.
  • Price improvement is measured against a benchmark that moves. "Improved on the NBBO" is measured at the instant of execution, and a wholesaler chooses that instant. This does not make the statistic dishonest; it makes it narrower than it sounds.

Measure it yourself, because nobody else's number transfers to you. For your next fifty fills, record the mid-price at the moment you sent the order and the price you actually got. The average difference is your execution cost per share, in your instrument, at your size, at the times of day you trade. Do the same for a month at a direct-routing broker and you have the only comparison that means anything — and unlike the composite above, it is a comparison you can act on. On a penny-spread instrument at a few hundred shares, expect the answer to be small enough that the commission line decides it. On thinner names, or on size, expect the opposite.

PFOF vs. Direct Routing: The Real Comparison

The PFOF Model

Commission: $0

Order routing: Sold to HFT firms (Citadel, Virtu, Two Sigma)

Your fills:

  • Usually at or just inside the quoted price — the improvement is real and small, typically a fraction of a cent
  • No chance to provide liquidity (join the queue)
  • The cost is mostly not in the fill price: it is that you never post, so the spread is always something you pay and never something you earn

Who wins: Broker + HFT firm

Who loses: You (death by a thousand cuts)

Direct Market Access

Commission: $0.0035-0.005/share (IBKR)

Order routing: YOU choose exchange (NASDAQ, NYSE, IEX, etc.)

Your fills:

  • Can place limit orders at bid (provide liquidity, get rebate)
  • Control execution venue (avoid predatory HFT zones)
  • Hidden cost: Minimal (tight spreads, fair execution)

Who wins: You (actual market access)

Who loses: HFTs (less order flow to exploit)

🎯 Action Step

If you trade 50,000+ shares per month:

  • Switch to a direct-routing broker (IBKR, TradeStation, Lightspeed)
  • Enable "direct market access" (DMA) routing
  • Use IEX exchange (speed bump protects against HFT predation)

You will pay a commission you can see instead of a cost you cannot. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on your size and instrument, and the only way to know is the fifty-fill measurement described above. At a few hundred shares of a penny-spread ETF the commission usually wins and you should stay where you are; on size, or on anything with a wide spread, routing control starts to matter more than the commission line.

Part 5: How to Trade Around HFTs

You Can't Beat Them. But You Can Avoid Being Their Lunch.

Here's the good news: HFTs dominate the sub-second timeframe. You're trading minutes to days.

Different game. Different rules.

Tactic 1: Avoid Toxic Times

Spreads are widest when the day is being repriced, which is the first fifteen minutes, the last fifteen, and the minutes after a scheduled release. That is not a rule about high-frequency firms; it is a rule about how much anybody is willing to quote when nobody knows what the day is yet.

Widest: 9:30-9:45 AM, 3:45-4:00 PM, the minutes around news, and pre- and post-market. Tightest: 10:30 AM-12 PM and 2:00-3:30 PM, midweek, when SPY is turning over more than 5M shares an hour. If your method needs the open, trade the open with a limit order and a smaller size, rather than not trading it.

Tactic 2: Use Limit Orders (Not Market Orders)

A market order accepts whatever price clears it. A limit order names the price and accepts that it may not fill.

On a penny-wide name the whole saving is bounded by the penny: cross and you pay it, rest and you do not, and the arithmetic later in this lesson shows that half a cent is exactly what a typical commission costs you to recover. What a resting order actually buys you on size is that it does not print a sweep the rest of the market can read. What it costs you is the trade that runs without you.

Tactic 3: Hide Your Intent (Iceberg Orders)

A ten-thousand-share market order is larger than the book in front of it, so it pays the depth term whatever anyone else does, and it prints a sweep on the way through. An iceberg shows a few hundred at a time and refills, so it neither takes the depth in one gulp nor announces the size behind it. No figures are attached to either here: what an iceberg saves depends on the book it meets, and the version of this line that quoted $500-1,000 against $100-200 was describing a book nobody measured. Most brokers offer icebergs; the ones built on payment for order flow generally do not.

Tactic 4: Trade Longer Timeframes

HFT dominates 0-60 seconds. Your edge = hours to days where fundamentals, regime, and institutional flow matter. HFT noise = irrelevant at swing timeframes.

🎯 Signal Pilot Advantage

Janus sweeps work on 15min-4H timeframes. HFTs don't compete there.

You're identifying institutional positioning, not racing for microseconds.

Different game, and a slower one. That is the whole of the advantage: not that you see more, but that what you are looking at does not disappear in a millisecond.

Part 6: Flash Crashes—When HFT Liquidity Vanishes

May 6, 2010: The Flash Crash That Changed Everything

The Flash Crash: 2:32 PM, Waddell & Reed sells $4.1B E-Mini futures (pure market order). 2:40 PM: HFTs detect selling, start "hot potato" trading. 2:42 PM: HFTs withdraw liquidity (spreads widen from $0.01 → $5+). 2:45 PM: DOW drops 1,000 points in 5 minutes (-9%), E-mini S&P -5% in under 4 minutes, SPY $107 → $105.53 at the low print while individual names cratered far harder — Accenture $40 → $0.01. 2:47 PM: CME halts, then rebounds. Aftermath: more than 20,000 trades across 300+ securities — mostly ETFs — printed 60%+ away from pre-crash prices and were later canceled as "erroneous." Anything closer than 60% stood, so retail stopped out at merely terrible prices kept the loss.

💀 Composite Victim: John Parker

Long 5K shares of a broad-market ETF @ $105. Stop @ $102 (3% max loss = $15K). Actual fill: $88.50. Loss: $82,500 (15.7% instead of 3%). The ETF was back at $104 by 3 PM. John's position? Gone at $88.50. And because his fill was 15.7% away — not 60% — his trade was never busted. The traders filled at $0.01 got their trades canceled; he simply ate it.

Other Notable Flash Crashes

Aug 24, 2015 (ETF Flash Crash): HFTs withdrew at the open. Roughly a fifth of all US ETFs traded 20%+ below the value of the baskets they hold — XLE printed about 21% under fair value while the energy stocks inside it were down about 3%. Retail stop losses filled 20-30% below fair value.

Oct 15, 2014 (Treasury Flash Rally): 10Y yield 2.20% → 1.86% (biggest move in decades) in seconds, back to 2.15% by 9:45 AM. HFT algos created feedback loop.

March 2020 (COVID Chaos): Four circuit breakers in 2 weeks. HFT liquidity vanished during halts, spreads widened 10-50x. Key lesson: HFT provides liquidity when you don't need it, withdraws when you do.

🚨 Circuit Breakers Exist for This (But They're Not Enough)

After 2010, exchanges added circuit breakers:

  • Level 1: -7% drop = 15-minute halt
  • Level 2: -13% drop = 15-minute halt
  • Level 3: -20% drop = close market for the day

Single-stock circuit breakers: If a stock moves 5-10% in 5 minutes, trading halts for 5 minutes.

Problem: HFTs can still cause chaos within those thresholds. Stopping at -7% doesn't help if you got stopped out at -5%.

Part 7: IEX—The Exchange Fighting Back Against HFTs

The "Speed Bump" Exchange Designed to Level the Playing Field

In 2012, Brad Katsuyama (the protagonist of Flash Boys) left his bank trading desk to build a fix for something he had noticed years earlier:

Every time he tried to buy a large block, prices moved before his order filled across all exchanges.

What he found was latency arbitrage: his own first execution was printing on the nearest venue and being read there before the rest of the order arrived anywhere else. He called it front-running, the book called it front-running, and the label stuck — but the mechanism is the one described above, and the difference is what made an exchange the fix rather than a lawsuit.

So he built IEX (Investors Exchange)—an exchange with a built-in 350-microsecond delay (a "speed bump") that neutralizes HFT advantages.

How IEX's Speed Bump Works

Normal Exchange (NASDAQ, NYSE):
1. Your order arrives at exchange
2. HFT detects it instantly (co-located servers, 0.5ms away)
3. The first print is read; quotes elsewhere are cancelled and repriced
4. Your order fills at worse price

IEX Exchange:
1. Your order arrives at IEX
2. Goes through 350μs coil of fiber (the "speed bump")
3. During those 350μs:
   - HFTs cannot react faster (everyone delayed equally)
   - Quote changes from other exchanges propagate
   - Prevents latency arbitrage

4. Your order hits matching engine with fair pricing
5. A stale quote on IEX cannot be picked off with fresher information

💡 Why 350 Microseconds?

That's how long a signal takes to crawl through 38 miles of fiber coiled up in a box — long enough that a quote change on another New Jersey exchange reaches IEX before an HFT could act on it.

By adding 350μs delay, IEX ensures HFTs can't see quote changes on other exchanges and react before your order executes.

Everyone gets delayed equally = fair game.

What Routing to IEX Does, and What It Does Not

The routing case is usually made with a three-month experiment: default routing for one month, manual NASDAQ routing for the next, IEX for the third, average slippage falling from 1.4 cents a share to 0.6, and a headline saving of $2,500 a month or $30,000 a year. It does not establish what it appears to, for three separate reasons that are each worth knowing on their own.

  • The numbers exceed the spread. SPY and QQQ are penny-wide. An average of 1.4 cents a share of slippage is more than the whole market is wide; 0.6 cents is plausible only if half your fills are worse than the offer, which is not what happens on a liquid ETF at a few thousand shares. Any execution figure quoted for a penny-spread instrument that is larger than a penny should stop you reading.
  • Three consecutive months is not a controlled comparison. January, February and March were different markets. Changing the routing at the same time as the month changes means the routing and the market moved together, and no amount of arithmetic afterwards can separate them. If you want to test a routing change, alternate it order by order — odd tickets one way, even tickets the other — over the same weeks. That is the only version of this experiment that answers the question.
  • It attributes the whole difference to the speed bump. Even granting the numbers, the routing changed more than one thing at once: which venues were reachable, how much of the order rested versus crossed, and how often it filled at all.

What IEX genuinely does. The 350-microsecond delay applies to everyone equally, which means a quote change on another exchange has time to reach IEX before anybody can act on it there. That removes one specific arbitrage: picking off a stale quote on IEX using fresher information from elsewhere. If your order is resting on IEX, that protection is real and it is the reason the exchange exists.

What it does not do. It does not make a marketable order cheaper — if you are crossing the spread, you pay the spread wherever you cross it. It does not help an order that never fills, and IEX holds a modest share of US equity volume, so an order resting only there waits longer and sometimes waits forever. And it does nothing about the cost that dominated Michael's fill earlier in this lesson, which was depth, not latency. Fee schedules change; check the current one at the exchange rather than trusting a number in an article, this one included.

The honest summary: routing to IEX is a reasonable default for resting orders and a matter of indifference for orders you intend to cross with. It is not worth $30,000 a year to anybody trading a few thousand shares of SPY, and if a venue change ever appears to save you that much, the first thing to check is whether you also changed how your orders were working.

TradeStation:

Part 8: The Big Picture

Infamous HFT Manipulation Cases: When Speed Becomes Crime

Navinder Sarao: The Bedroom Trader the DOJ Blamed for the 2010 Flash Crash

Yes, one guy. From his parents' house in London. With $40 million in automated spoofing.

WHO: Navinder Singh Sarao, independent futures trader
WHEN: 2009-2015 (arrested April 2015)
WHAT: Spoofing E-Mini S&P 500 futures

THE STRATEGY:
1. Place massive sell orders (200-900 contracts) at levels above market
2. Spook other traders into thinking selling pressure building
3. Market moves down
4. Cancel fake sell orders before they fill
5. Buy at lower price, profit from the drop
6. Repeat thousands of times per day

Estimated profit: $40 million over 5 years
Role in Flash Crash: DOJ charged him with contributing to the May 6, 2010 crash
  (the 2010 SEC/CFTC report had blamed the Waddell & Reed sell program;

        Sarao ran this from a bedroom, and the charge was the intent

        cancelled order.

Tower Research: The $67.4 Million Spoofing Settlement

WHO: Tower Research Capital (HFT firm)
WHEN: March 2012 - December 2013
SETTLEMENT: $67.4 million (DOJ deferred prosecution agreement, 2019)

THE SCHEME:
- Three traders placed thousands of orders in E-mini futures
  they never intended to fill
- Used algos to fake liquidity, manipulate prices
- Canceled the fake side before execution
- Profited from price movements caused by fake orders

Illustrative pattern:
  9:30:00 - Place buy orders for 1,000 E-mini S&P contracts above the bid
  9:30:01 - Other algos detect "buying pressure"
  9:30:02 - Market ticks up 2 points

Virtu Financial: The Firm That Lost Money on One Day Out of 1,238

Not illegal. Just... statistically impossible for most traders. Yet they did it.

Virtu's 2014 IPO filing, covering 2009-2013, revealed:
- 1,238 trading days
- Profitable on 1,237 days (99.92%)
- Only ONE losing day (and it was tiny)

How? HFT market making at scale:
- Quote 10,000+ instruments simultaneously across 200+ venues
- Capture bid-ask spread on billions of shares
- Use speed to avoid adverse selection
- Withdraw liquidity during volatility (don't take losses)

Adjusted net trading income: about $620M (2013)
Spread across equities, FX, commodities and fixed income

Lesson: HFT isn't gambling. It's latency arbitrage at scale.

Why You Actually Have Advantages

You are at a disadvantage on speed, permanently. What follows is what that disadvantage does not cost you.

What HFTs Can't Do

  • Hold a view for longer than a position: a market-making book is flat by design and is measured on inventory risk, not on being right about a company
  • Multi-timeframe analysis: Don't consider HTF structure (only sub-second data)
  • Regime recognition: Don't adapt to trending vs. ranging markets (just react to flow)
  • Discretion: No "this feels wrong" gut check (pure math)
  • Overnight holds: Can't capture multi-day swings (too much overnight risk)
  • Narrative understanding: Can't read FOMC tone, geopolitical risk, sector rotation

The list is about mandate rather than capability. A firm quoting ten thousand instruments is not trying to do any of these things, which is why they are still available to you.

What You Can Do

  • Context: Read macro, understand regime shifts (risk-on vs risk-off)
  • Multi-timeframe: Daily trend + 4H setup + 15min entry + multi-day hold
  • Pattern recognition: Janus sweeps, BoS, CHoCH, liquidity hunts
  • Human judgment: "This setup is A-grade, that one's C-grade, skip that one"
  • Patience: Wait for A+ setups (HFTs must trade constantly to profit)
  • Adaptability: Change strategy when market regime shifts

You trade slower but smarter. That's your edge.

The Bottom Line: How to Profit Alongside (Not Against) HFTs

🎯 Your Anti-HFT Execution Playbook

  1. Use limit orders (never market orders)
    • Place limit at bid to buy, ask to sell (provide liquidity)
    • Wait for fills. On a penny-wide name the saving is bounded at half a cent a share, which the table below shows a commission usually cancels
  2. Avoid toxic times
    • Treat 9:30-9:45 AM and 3:45-4:00 PM as expensive rather than forbidden: same setup, smaller size, limit order
    • Avoid FOMC announcements, major news events
    • Trade during 10:30 AM-12 PM or 2-3:30 PM (calmer flow)
  3. Use iceberg orders for size (hide your intent)
    • Display 200-500 shares, hide the rest
    • The order does not print a sweep the rest of the market can read
  4. Route to IEX (speed bump exchange)
    • Neutralizes latency arbitrage
    • Protects orders you leave resting; does nothing for orders you cross with
  5. Switch to direct-routing broker (avoid PFOF)
    • IBKR, TradeStation, Lightspeed
    • Worth it when the spread is wider than twice your commission, and worth nothing on a penny-wide name, where the two cancel exactly
  6. Trade longer timeframes
    • 4H and daily charts, where a millisecond advantage buys nothing
    • Multi-day holds, where the execution cost is a rounding error against the move you are holding for
  7. Know what kind of stop you are using
    • A stop-market becomes a market order at the worst possible moment, which is exactly the John Parker outcome earlier in this lesson
    • A stop-limit protects the price and can leave you in the position if it gaps through — that is the trade you are making, and it is a real one
    • Place the stop where the structure says it belongs, not at the round number below it; if your exit level and an obvious round number coincide, move yours
    • Do not replace it with a mental stop. A mental stop fails in exactly the conditions that make stops matter: fast moves, and being away from the screen

Worked Example: Combining the Tactics

SCENARIO: You want to buy 8,000 shares of SPY (quoted $520.00 / $520.01)

The ceiling on what this can cost you:
- The spread is a penny. Crossing it on 8,000 shares costs $80.
- That is the whole spread cost. Not a range, not an estimate --
  8,000 x $0.01 = $80, and no execution tactic can save more than that.
- Anything worse than $80 is depth: your order is larger than the
  offers in front of it and has to reach up for the rest.

CROSSING (one marketable order):
- You pay the $80, plus whatever depth costs beyond the top of book.
- At the open, when the book is thin, the depth term is the larger one.
- At midday in SPY, a few thousand shares usually sits inside the top
  couple of price levels, so the depth term is small.

RESTING (limit at the bid, working the order):
- You give up the $80 and may collect a rebate instead of paying a fee,
  depending on the venue -- check the current schedule, it changes.
- What you take on is fill risk: some of the order does not fill, and
  the trade you wanted is smaller or does not happen.
- That risk is the price of the saving. It is not free money, and on a
  setup that runs without you it costs far more than $80.

WHAT TO ACTUALLY MEASURE (for your next 50 fills):
- Mid-price when you sent the order: ______
- Volume-weighted price you got:    ______
- Difference x shares = your real execution cost
- Split it: top-of-book depth you consumed vs. everything else

Do not annualise a single trade. One fill tells you nothing about a
year, and multiplying it by 104 produces a headline saving with no
evidence behind it -- which is how most of the figures in this
subject are made.

🎯 When routing control is worth paying for, and when the numbers cancel

The lesson's organising fact is that a penny-wide instrument can only cost you a penny. Push it one step further and it answers the question the whole PFOF argument is really about: is it worth paying a commission to get routing control?

What routing control buys you is the ability to rest at the bid rather than cross to the offer. If your limit fills, you save half the spread. What it costs you is a commission per share. So the comparison is one subtraction:

value of routing control  =  half the spread  −  commission per share

Take a typical direct-routing rate of half a cent a share:

quoted spread   half-spread saved by posting   commission   net per share
        $0.01                        $0.0050      $0.0050         $0.0000
        $0.02                        $0.0100      $0.0050        +$0.0050
        $0.05                        $0.0250      $0.0050        +$0.0200
        $0.10                        $0.0500      $0.0050        +$0.0450

Read the top row, because it is the one nearly every reader of this lesson is in. On a penny-wide ETF, half the spread is half a cent and the commission is half a cent. They cancel exactly. You pay a visible cost to recover an identical invisible one, and you additionally give up the price improvement the wholesaler was handing you. On SPY, at retail size, the entire controversy is worth approximately nothing to you.

Routing control starts to pay only when the spread is wider than twice your commission. That is not an ETF; it is a small-cap, an illiquid option, or a name quoting in nickels — where the second row is already worth its commission over again and the bottom row is worth nine times it.

One more term decides it for small orders. Most commission schedules carry a minimum of about a dollar per order, so below roughly 200 shares that minimum is the real rate: a hundred-share order on a penny-wide name pays a cent a share, twice the half-spread it was trying to save. Small orders on liquid names are strictly worse at a commission broker, before anyone argues about who sees what.

So the honest version of this lesson's conclusion is a threshold rather than a side to be on: let the flow be paid for on liquid penny-wide names, and pay for routing when the spread is wide enough to fund it. Run the subtraction on the instrument in front of you rather than on the industry.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • HFTs trade in microseconds—you can't compete on speed
    • The gap is large enough that the multiple does not matter. Depending on which numbers you pick it lands anywhere between twenty-five and four hundred times, which is why no single figure is quoted here. What matters is that nothing in your method should depend on being fast
    • They have co-located servers, you have home WiFi
    • Nothing in your method should require you to act inside a second
  • They make money via market making, latency arbitrage, and order anticipation
    • Market making: Profit from bid-ask spread ($0.01/share × billions)
    • Latency arbitrage: act on a price update on one venue before it has propagated to another. Note that this is not front-running — nobody is seeing your order and stepping in front of it; front-running means trading ahead of a customer order entrusted to you, which is a different act and is prohibited. No per-share figure is given, because on a penny-spread name the entire spread is one cent and any larger number describes your own order size rather than anyone else's profit
    • Quote stuffing is a prosecuted offence rather than a routine practice, and the enforcement record is against individual proprietary traders and bank desks. Note also that you cannot detect it: most orders in US equities are cancelled rather than filled, so heavy cancellation on your screen is the ordinary state of the book
  • Payment for order flow (PFOF) is selling you to HFTs
    • Brokers route retail orders to wholesalers and are paid for the flow. Nothing is shown to anyone before the market sees it — the order is executed by the wholesaler rather than displayed in advance, which is a different thing and worth keeping straight
    • What it costs you is the difference between your fill and the best price you could have had, which is usually a fraction of the spread and is often improved rather than worsened on a liquid name. Measure it on your own fills; any figure quoted here would be describing an instrument and an order size that may not be yours
    • Direct market access is worth its commission when the spread is wider than twice that commission, and not before
  • Treat the opening and the close as different problems (9:30-9:45 AM, 3:45-4:00 PM, news events)
    • Both windows are where the day is being repriced, which is why quoting is thin in them
    • Spreads widen 10-50x, slippage explodes
    • 10:30 AM-12 PM and 2-3:30 PM are the cheapest hours to cross a spread in. If your method needs the open, take it smaller and with a limit
  • Use limit orders, iceberg orders, and IEX routing
    • A market order accepts whatever price clears it, and on size that price is set by the depth of the book rather than by anyone else’s speed
    • A limit order at the bid or offer names the price and risks the fill. The saving is bounded by half the spread, which a commission usually cancels on a penny-wide name
    • Iceberg orders hide your size (display 500, hide 9,500)
    • IEX’s 350μs speed bump protects an order resting there and does nothing for one you cross with
  • Trade longer timeframes (hours-days) to escape HFT battleground
    • HFTs dominate 0-60 second timeframes
    • A market-making book is flat by design, so the multi-day move is not what it is there for
    • Your edge: Multi-timeframe analysis, context, discretion
  • Flash crashes reveal HFT's dark side
    • May 2010: DOW -1,000 pts in 5 minutes (HFTs withdrew liquidity)
    • Aug 2015: ETFs dislocated 20-30% from their baskets (HFT market makers pulled quotes)
    • The lesson is about stop type, not stop discipline: a stop-market becomes a market order in the worst conditions, a stop-limit may not fill at all
    • Choose deliberately and keep the stop. Replacing it with a mental one fails precisely when it matters
  • Know the ceiling on what execution can cost or save you
    • On a penny-spread instrument the spread cost is a penny a share. Any figure larger than that is depth — your own size — not the spread and not anyone front-running you
    • Which means the biggest lever is order size relative to the book, and it is available to you today, for free
    • Any published slippage figure bigger than the instrument's spread is wrong. Check that before you act on it, including here
    • Measure your own fills for a month. Nobody else's number transfers to your instrument, your size or your hours

  1. Setup (Before Market Open):

  4. Closing Session (3:30-4:00 PM):

🎮 Test Your Understanding (No Pressure)

Question 1: You want to buy 5,000 shares of SPY. What's the best execution method to minimize HFT impact?

A) Single market order for 5,000 shares
B) Market order during market open (9:30 AM)
C) Iceberg order (display 500, hide 4,500) at limit price
D) Urgent market order with "fill immediately" flag
Correct: C. An iceberg displays 500 and hides 4,500, so the book never advertises the full 5,000 and there is no size signal for anyone to react to. A does the opposite: one 5,000-share market order announces itself and walks the book, which is precisely the footprint faster participants watch for. B compounds it by choosing the open, when spreads are widest. D is A with the urgency stated out loud.

Question 2: Your zero-commission broker is paid for your order flow. A competitor charges $0.005 a share and lets you choose the venue. You buy 500 shares of SPY at a time, quoted $520.00 / $520.01. What does switching buy you?

A) About a penny a share — the width of the spread
B) Nothing, on this instrument at this size
C) $0.005 a share, which is what the commission costs
D) It depends on your connection speed
Correct: B. Routing control buys you the ability to rest at the bid instead of crossing to the offer, which saves half the spread: half a cent. The commission costs half a cent. They cancel exactly, and on top of that you give up the price improvement the wholesaler was handing you. A is the whole spread, which you only pay if you cross and only save if you post — and posting saves half of it, not all. C prices the commission without pricing what it buys. D is the question the lesson exists to get you to stop asking: on a penny-wide name at 500 shares, nobody’s latency is what decides your cost. Change the instrument and the answer changes with it: at a five-cent spread the same subtraction is worth two cents a share.

The speed race is real and you are not in it. What is worth taking from this lesson is narrower and more useful than that: most of what retail traders call slippage is their own order size meeting a thinner book than they assumed, and that part is arithmetic rather than conspiracy. It is also the part you can fix this week, by sizing orders to the depth in front of them and by resting instead of crossing when the setup lets you wait.
