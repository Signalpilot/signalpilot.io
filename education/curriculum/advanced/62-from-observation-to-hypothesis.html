What Would Have to Happen

Reading time ~12 min • Module 8: Building a System •

Nine observations on this course’s sixty closes either confirm an idea or settle nothing at all, and which one you get was decided by a sentence you wrote before you started counting. After two down closes the next close is up 6 times out of 9 here, which is 66.7 per cent against a coin’s 50, and phrased that way the idea is confirmed. But 32 of the 59 moves in this series close up regardless of anything, so the number to beat is 54.24 per cent, and the interval around 6 of 9 runs from 0.354 to 0.879 and swallows both. Nothing has been established. You would have needed 8 of those 9 before the count said anything whatever, and 656 bars before it could separate a ten-point lift from the drift.

Prerequisites: Lesson 19, for the arithmetic that turns a count into an interval and an interval into a number of trades, lesson 52, for the base rate as the only honest thing to measure a conditional count against, and lesson 18, for why a run of results that feels like evidence usually is not.

An observation is not a hypothesis until it forbids something

You are watching a chart and you notice something. Two red closes, then a green one. It happens again twenty minutes later. By the third time you are not noticing any more, you are expecting, and somewhere in there a sentence formed: the market bounces after two down closes. That sentence feels like a finding. It is not one yet, and the reason is narrow and fixable. It does not forbid anything. There is no count you could come back with tomorrow that the sentence would have to accept as a refusal.

Take the sixty closes this course has carried since lesson 38 and make the sentence do some work. Fifty-nine moves join those closes, of which 32 are up and 27 are down. Define the condition as two consecutive down closes and the outcome as the next close finishing higher. That is now a rule anyone can run, and running it gives 9 occurrences, of which 6 finished higher.

Six of nine is 66.7 per cent. Set beside the 50 that everyone carries around as the number a coin would give, it looks like a substantial edge, and this is the moment almost every idea in trading is adopted. The arithmetic that follows is what almost nobody you are trading against has done, and it costs an evening and a spreadsheet.

Start by making the condition sharper, which is what a person does when an idea feels promising. One down close, then two, then three. Same series, same outcome, same arithmetic.

Read the rate column and the idea is holding up: 0.654, then 0.667, then 0.667. Read the two interval columns and the evidence is evaporating. The interval is 0.344 wide after one down close, 0.525 wide after two, and 0.731 wide after three, so by the third row it covers three quarters of everything a probability is allowed to be. Sharpening the setup felt like precision and bought nothing, because the rate did not move and the sample fell from 26 to 3.

That is the first thing the sentence has to carry, then: the condition, stated tightly enough that two people counting separately get the same 9.

Fifty per cent is not the baseline

The second thing is the number the rate is being compared against, and it is not 50. Thirty-two of the 59 moves in this series close up without any condition attached, which is 54.24 per cent. This series drifts upward, as most equity series do over most windows, and any rule that ends up long is collecting that drift whether or not it has found anything.

So the claim is not worth 16.7 points over a coin. It is worth 12.4 points over the thing you would have got by doing nothing in particular, and that is the only version of it that could ever be wrong. The distinction is not pedantry: it is the difference between a number that has to beat something and a number that beats a straw man by construction.

Now the sample. To separate a rate from a baseline you need enough occurrences that the interval around your count clears the baseline, and lesson 19’s arithmetic gives the figure directly. Four times the baseline variance, divided by the square of the lift you are trying to detect.

The bars column is the occurrences column multiplied by how often the condition fires, which on this series is once every 6.6 bars. A ten-point lift, the smallest that would interest anybody, wants 656 daily bars. That is 31 months of watching before the count could tell you it was wrong, and it is why the idea you formed in twenty minutes is going to feel true for a very long time.

The four things the sentence has to carry

Put those together and a hypothesis is a sentence with four parts in it. The condition, tight enough to count. The baseline it has to beat, which is what happens without the condition. The size of the effect claimed, because without one no result is ever too small to count. And the sample that would decide it, computed in advance from the other three.

Drop any one and the idea acquires an escape route. Without the condition it can be recounted differently each time it fails. Without the baseline it beats a coin instead of beating the market’s own drift. Without a size it survives any result above zero. Without a sample it stays open forever, because there is no point at which you agreed to stop. The four together produce a single object, and it is worth naming, because the rest of this module spends it: the refuting observation, the specific count that you agreed in advance you would accept as a no.

This is also the first column of the sheet this module builds. One row per idea, and a column added by each lesson: the sentence here, what a backtest of it is worth in lesson 63, what searching for it costs in lesson 64, what surviving contact costs later. The module’s last lesson spends the whole row at once, which is the first point at which a row stops being a record and becomes a decision. The reason to start the sheet now is that this first column is the only one that cannot be filled in afterwards.

The same nine observations, judged twice

Nothing below changes the data. The 9 occurrences are the same 9, the 6 up closes are the same 6, and every figure comes off the same sixty closes. Only the sentence changes.

Judged loosely: the market bounces after two down closes, more often than not. Six of nine is 0.667, which is more than 0.500, so the observation agrees with the sentence and the idea is adopted. Notice that this verdict did not require the interval, the baseline or the sample size, which is exactly why it was available within twenty minutes of the idea arriving.

Judged strictly: after two consecutive down closes the next close finishes higher at least ten points more often than the 54.24 per cent baseline, and the 95 per cent interval around the observed rate excludes that baseline. Six of nine gives 0.667, and the interval runs from 0.354 to 0.879. It contains 0.5424. The verdict is undecided, and undecided is a result, because it tells you the next thing to do rather than what to think.

Here is the part that is genuinely worse than it looks. Hold the sample at nine and ask what the count would have had to be. Seven of nine is 0.778, and its interval still runs down to 0.453 and still contains the baseline. Eight of nine is 0.889, and its interval starts at 0.565, which finally clears it. So on nine occurrences there are exactly two counts that could have told you anything, 8 and 9, and every other outcome was going to be undecided before you started. Now price that. If the effect were real at the full ten points claimed, the chance of the afternoon landing on 8 or 9 was 0.112, or about one in nine. The test you ran had a one-in-nine chance of detecting the thing it was built to detect, and you would have called any of 5, 6 or 7 a confirmation.

An idea that cannot come out false has not been tested, it has been retold.

What you can do with this tonight is short: write the sentence with all four parts in it, compute the sample it needs, and find out whether you have that much data before you look at what the data says.

What this does not settle

That two down closes predict anything. Nine occurrences on one sixty-bar series cannot establish that, and the page has not tried to. The claim here is about the sentence rather than the setup, and a reader who runs the same condition on ten years of their own instrument may well find a real effect. What they will not find is a way of knowing that from nine.

That the interval used here is the only one available. These are Wilson intervals at 95 per cent, which is a convention, and a different convention moves the boundary. The normal approximation puts the third row’s upper bound at 1.200, which is not a probability; an exact binomial interval is wider than Wilson and puts the lower bound for 8 of 9 at 0.518, which fails to clear the baseline, so under that convention only 9 of 9 decides anything. The conclusion survives all three, but the specific figure 8 does not, and the page prints it as though it were a fact about the world rather than a fact about a choice of interval.

That an up close is a bounce. It is not. A close finishing higher than the one before it says nothing about the path in between, and a trader who needs a tradeable bounce needs the low of the bar, the time it took, and whether the entry was fillable. Every figure on this page measures a coarser event than the one the original observation was about, and the coarser event is the easy one to count, which is why it is the one that got counted.

That the baseline is known. This is the one that costs most, and it is the same error the page is complaining about, one level up. The 54.24 per cent comes from 32 of 59 moves, and the interval around that is 0.417 to 0.663. The number the hypothesis has to beat is itself uncertain across a range wider than the effect being claimed, and nothing on this page accounts for it. Doing so properly makes the sample requirements larger than the second table says, and the page has left the table as it is because the correction needs machinery the course has not built yet.

That occurrences are independent. The bars column assumes they are, and they are not: overlapping stretches of down closes share bars, so 100 occurrences carry less information than 100 independent draws would. The true requirement is therefore worse than 656 bars rather than better, which at least means the error runs in the safe direction, but a reader who wants the honest figure will need a block bootstrap and this page does not supply one.

And that stating an idea properly makes it a good idea. It does not. It makes it a testable one, and most testable ideas are false, which is the point of being able to find out cheaply. Nothing here helps you generate an idea worth testing, and the machinery for judging the ones you already have is worth much less than it looks if you have no way of producing candidates. That gap is real and this page does not close it. What comes next narrows it from the other side: lesson 63 takes an idea that has survived this sentence and runs it over history, and finds that a search across 253 ordinary configurations produces a winner beating buy-and-hold by 4.16 dollars on these same closes, while 52.5 per cent of series with no structure in them at all beat it by more.

Problems

  1. Compute your own baseline. Take two hundred bars of the instrument you trade and count how many closed higher than the bar before. Divide. That single number is what every conditional rate you ever quote has to beat, and almost nobody who trades your instrument knows it. Ten minutes, and you end holding one figure.
  2. Count one condition against it. On the same two hundred bars, pick the condition you actually watch for, count the occurrences and how many were followed by an up close, and compute the rate. Compare it with the baseline from the first problem, and note the gap in points. Half an hour, and you end holding the lift your idea is really claiming, which is smaller than the one you thought it was claiming.
  3. Write the sentence and price it. Take an idea you currently trade and write it with all four parts: condition, baseline, size, sample. Compute the occurrences needed from the lift you claimed, multiply by how often your condition fires, and convert to bars and then to months. Then count how many occurrences your record actually contains. An evening, and you end holding one number, the ratio of the sample you need to the sample you have, which for most ideas most people trade comes out well below one.
