Performance Attribution: Where Did Your Returns Really Come From?

You made +45% this year. Congratulations!

But here's the question nobody asks: Where did that +45% come from?

Was it your Janus strategy? Or did you just ride a bull market? Was it skill or luck?

🚨 Real Talk

Most traders can't answer this question. They see a green number and celebrate.

Professionals decompose every dollar of return. They know which strategies work, which don't, and why.

In this lesson, you'll learn:

  • How to separate alpha (skill) from beta (market exposure)
  • Strategy-by-strategy performance breakdown
  • Identifying your top 5 winners (they're probably most of your profit)
  • Eliminating strategies that don't work (and doubling down on what does)

Part 1: The Attribution Framework

Breaking Down the +45%

Let's say you made +45% this year. Time to decompose it.

Which Strategy Made the Money?

Total Return: +45%

By Strategy:
- Janus sweeps:      +25% (56% of return) ← Your edge!
- Breakouts:         +12% (27%)
- Mean reversion:    +13% (29%)
- Failed experiments: -5% (-11%)
                     ----
                      +45%

Insight: 56% came from ONE strategy
Action: Allocate more capital to Janus sweeps

One strategy dominated. That's your edge. Scale it.

Which Asset Made the Money?

Total Return: +45%

By Asset:
- SPY: +30% (67% of return)
- QQQ: +10% (22%)
- IWM: +5% (11%)

Insight: SPY trades worked best
Action: Focus on SPY setups (highest liquidity)

One caution about all three of these breakdowns. Total P&L by bucket measures allocation as much as it measures skill. If two thirds of your return came from SPY and you put two thirds of your trades in SPY, you have learned nothing about SPY — you have rediscovered where your capital was. The same trap catches the quarterly view: Q3 being your best quarter may mean your edge suits trending regimes, or it may simply mean you traded more in Q3.

The fix is one column. Alongside total P&L, compute expectancy per trade in each bucket, and record the trade count. A bucket with high total P&L and mediocre per-trade expectancy is telling you about your allocation. A bucket with high per-trade expectancy and few trades is telling you about your edge — and telling you, incidentally, that you are not taking enough of them.

Liquidity matters. SPY = best execution, least slippage.

Which Quarter Made the Money?

Total Return: +45%

By Quarter:
- Q1: +15%
- Q2: +8%
- Q3: +20% ← Best quarter (trending regime)
- Q4: +2%

Insight: Q3 = trending regime = Janus edge
Action: Trade larger in trending regimes

Know when your edge is strongest. Size accordingly.

💡 The Aha Moment

Performance attribution reveals where you ACTUALLY make money.

Most traders are surprised by how lopsided it is: a small minority of trades carries most of the profit.

Part 2: Alpha vs. Beta

Was It Skill or Just a Bull Market?

You made +40%. SPY made +20%. You're celebrating.

But wait—your portfolio has a beta of 1.5 (moves 1.5x the market).

Let's do the math:

Your return: +40%
SPY return: +20%
Your beta: 1.5

Expected return (based on beta): 1.5 × 20% = 30%

Alpha = Actual return - Expected return
      = 40% - 30%
      = +10%

Interpretation: +10% alpha = skill-based excess return
                +30% beta = just riding the market

(This is the shortcut. Full CAPM also subtracts the
 risk-free rate: 40% - [4% + 1.5 × (20% - 4%)] = +12%.
 Either version answers the only question that matters:
 is there anything left after the market is paid?)

Only +10% was skill. The rest was market exposure.

🚨 The Uncomfortable Truth

If the market had dropped -20%, your beta of 1.5 alone puts you at -30%. A +10% alpha still leaves you down 20%.

Alpha = skill. Beta = risk. Know the difference.

Sharpe, Sortino, Information Ratio

Sharpe Ratio (Return Per Unit of Risk) 
Sharpe = (Return - Risk-Free) / Std Dev

Your stats:
Return: 40%
Risk-Free: 4%
Std Dev: 18%

Sharpe = (40 - 4) / 18 = 2.0 (excellent!)

Benchmark:
< 1.0 = Poor
1.0-2.0 = Good
> 2.0 = Excellent

Those benchmark bands are the ones everybody quotes, and they are close to useless on one year of your own trading. Here is why, and it is the same problem that runs through this whole lesson.

A Sharpe ratio is an estimate, and the uncertainty on it depends on how much data went into it. From a single year of daily returns, the standard error on a measured Sharpe of 2.0 is roughly 1.0. That means a measured 2.0 is genuinely consistent with a true Sharpe anywhere from about 0 to about 4. You have not established that you are excellent; you have established that you had a good year, which you already knew. Three years of data roughly halves that error bar, and even then the bands above are wider than the differences between them.

Use Sharpe the way it is actually useful: to compare two things measured over the same period, on the same account, under the same conditions — your Janus trades against your breakout trades, this quarter against last. Comparisons within one sample cancel out most of what makes the absolute number unreliable. Reading a single Sharpe off a single year and calling it evidence of an edge is the same error as reading a win rate off twenty trades.

Sortino Ratio (Only Penalizes Downside) 
Sortino = (Return - Risk-Free) / Downside Dev

Advantage: Ignores upside volatility (good vol)

Your stats:
Return: 40%
Downside Dev: 12% (vs. total 18%)

Sortino = (40 - 4) / 12 = 3.0 (better than Sharpe!)

Takeaway: Your losses are controlled, gains are large

Do not read Sortino being higher than Sharpe as good news. It is higher almost always, by construction. Downside deviation only counts the returns below your target, so it is smaller than the full standard deviation for essentially any return series with any upside variance at all. For a perfectly symmetric distribution — no skew, nothing asymmetric about it — downside deviation comes out at roughly 0.7 of the total, which makes Sortino about 1.4 times Sharpe. Daniel's 3.0 against 2.0 is a ratio of 1.5. That is barely above what a symmetric, entirely unremarkable set of returns would produce.

What Sortino is genuinely for: comparing two strategies whose volatility is shaped differently. A strategy with occasional huge winners and consistent small losers will look worse under Sharpe than it deserves, because Sharpe penalises the big winners as volatility. Sortino does not. Use it as a second opinion on Sharpe, and read the gap between them as a description of your return shape rather than as a score.

Part 2.5: Real-World Case Study

The Strategy That Was Quietly Killing His Returns

Trader: Daniel Park (composite example), 38, discretionary day trader from San Diego, CA
Timeframe: Q1-Q4 2024 (12 months)
Account Size: $120,000
First half result: +$28,400 (+23.7%) over 252 trades
Problem: Made 23.7% in 6 months but had NO IDEA where the returns came from

⚠️ The Before: Flying Blind (Q1-Q2 2024)

Daniel traded 3 strategies for 6 months, made +$28,400, and felt great. But when he performed attribution analysis in July, he discovered a shocking truth: One strategy had contributed +$48,900 in profit, while another had DESTROYED -$23,800 of those gains. He was unknowingly sabotaging himself.

Phase 1: The Blind Spot (January-June 2024)

Daniel traded three strategies without tracking which made money:

  1. Janus Liquidity Sweeps (his main focus)
  2. Breakout Momentum Trades (looked exciting)
  3. VWAP Mean Reversion (seemed reliable)

Every month, he saw green P&L and assumed all strategies were working. He never decomposed the returns.

Daniel's H1 2024 Performance (Jan-Jun, 252 trades): Started $120K, ended $148.4K (+$28.4K, +23.7% return). Best months: March (+$5,920), May (+$7,120), January (+$6,840). Weakest: April (+$1,420). Daniel assumed all three strategies were working and felt great. Reality: He was about to get a wake-up call.

Phase 2: The Attribution Analysis (July 2024)

In early July, Daniel attended a trading workshop that emphasized performance attribution. He decided to analyze his H1 2024 results by strategy for the first time.

He exported all 252 trades, tagged each by strategy, and built a spreadsheet. What he discovered shocked him.

Daniel's H1 2024 Performance Attribution BY STRATEGY (The Revelation) — 1R = $500
Strategy Trades Win Rate Avg R/trade Total P&L % of Total Profit Insight
Janus Liquidity Sweeps 87 77.0% +1.12R +$48,900 172% ✓ MASSIVE EDGE!
Breakout Momentum 94 41.5% -0.51R -$23,800 -84% ❌ PROFIT DESTROYER!
VWAP Mean Reversion 71 59.2% +0.09R +$3,300 12% ⚠️ Marginal/neutral
TOTALS 252 58.7% +0.23R +$28,400 100% —

🚨 The Shocking Discovery

Janus Liquidity Sweeps: 87 trades, 77.0% win rate, +1.12R per trade → +$48,900 profit (172% of total!)

Breakout Momentum: 94 trades, 41.5% win rate, -0.51R per trade → -$23,800 loss (-84% of total!)

VWAP Mean Reversion: 71 trades, 59.2% win rate, +0.09R per trade → +$3,300 profit (12% of total)

Translation: Daniel had a MASSIVE edge in Janus sweeps (+$48,900), but he was unknowingly destroying 49% of those gains (-$23,800) with breakout trades. His "good" +23.7% return would have been +43.5% ($52,200 on $120,000) if he had never taken a breakout.

Phase 3: What He Changed — and What the Numbers Could Support

Armed with the attribution, Daniel made four changes in July:

🔄 Daniel's July changes

  1. Stopped taking breakouts entirely. 94 trades at -0.51R is a large enough sample and a large enough negative to act on.
  2. Shifted risk budget toward Janus sweeps. The strategy carrying the profit gets more of the budget.
  3. Cut VWAP mean reversion back to occasional setups. 71 trades at +0.09R is indistinguishable from nothing.
  4. Added confluence filters to the Janus setups and concentrated the same total risk into fewer, higher-conviction trades.

Before you copy this, understand what you are doing when you pick the winner. Daniel ran three strategies, measured them over six months, and scaled up the one that scored best. That procedure has a bias built into it, and it is worth naming: you are selecting the maximum of three noisy estimates, and the maximum of several noisy estimates is systematically higher than the truth behind it. Some of that 77% is edge. Some of it is the luck that made this strategy rather than another one come first in the ranking, and that part will not repeat.

The size of the problem depends on the size of the finding. Breakouts at -0.51R over 94 trades is a big, clear negative and selection bias does not explain it away — that conclusion is safe. Janus at 77% over 87 trades is a real result too, but the standard error on a win rate at that sample is about four and a half percentage points, so the true rate could plausibly be anywhere from the high sixties to the mid eighties, and the "best of three" selection pushes your best guess toward the lower end of that. Expect the strategy you scale to perform somewhat worse after you scale it than it did in the sample that made you choose it. That is not pessimism; it is what the arithmetic says will happen on average.

The practical consequence: attribution is far more reliable at telling you what to stop than at telling you what to double. Killing a clearly negative strategy is nearly free — if you are wrong, you gave up something close to zero. Doubling a strategy on six months of data is an actual bet, and it should be sized like one.

What we are not going to give you is his second half. An earlier version of this page carried it: 153 trades, +$41,200, a month-by-month list, a full-year total of +$69,600, and a headline claiming the attribution exercise was worth $6,100. Every one of those numbers was invented, and the last of them was invented in a particularly misleading way — it compared what happened in the second half against the first half's rate of return, and credited the entire difference to the spreadsheet. The second half was a different six months of market. No arithmetic performed afterwards can separate the effect of dropping the breakouts from the effect of the market Daniel happened to trade into.

What the first half establishes on its own, without any sequel:

  • He was running a strategy with a clear negative expectancy and did not know it. 94 trades at 41.5% and -0.51R is not ambiguous, and it went undetected for six months because the account total was green every month. That is the finding, and it is fully contained in the data he already had.
  • The account total hid it completely. +23.7% over six months looks like three working strategies. It was one working strategy, one doing nothing, and one actively subtracting from the first — and no amount of staring at the equity curve would have shown that.
  • The arithmetic of what the breakouts cost is checkable and is the honest direction of the counterfactual. Those 94 trades happened and lost $23,800. Adding that back gives +$52,200 rather than +$28,400. That is not a claim about what a better Daniel would have earned; it is a subtraction over trades that are in the log.

Daniel's advice to other traders: "I thought I knew what was working. I was wrong. Spend two hours doing attribution analysis. Two hours of spreadsheet work found a strategy that had been quietly costing me money for six months, and I would not have found it any other way."

Part 3: Top Winners & Losers

Your Top 5 Trades Are Most of Your Profit

This is the part that hurts (and helps).

Where You Made Money

Top 5 Winners (out of 50 trades, 1R = $400):

1. SPY sweep reversal:  +$2,000 (5.0R)
2. QQQ breakout:        +$1,800 (4.5R)
3. IWM mean reversion:  +$1,200 (3.0R)
4. SPY VWAP bounce:     +$1,120 (2.8R)
5. QQQ Janus sweep:     +$1,000 (2.5R)
                        -------
Total from top 5:       +$7,120

Total profit across all 50 trades: +$12,700
Top 5 = 56% of it, from 10% of the trades.

Lesson: Let. Winners. Run.

5 trades out of 50 = 56% of profit. This is why you hold 3R+ targets.

Where You Lost Money

Bottom 5 Losers (1R = $400):

1. QQQ oversize:        -$1,200 (-3.0R, 3x normal size)
2. IWM false breakout:    -$400 (-1.0R, stop hit)
3. SPY FOMO entry:        -$400 (-1.0R, ignored HTF)
4. SPY news volatility:   -$400 (-1.0R, VIX spike)
5. IWM late chase:        -$400 (-1.0R, FOMO)
                          ------
Total from bottom 5:    -$2,800

Note the top row. One sizing mistake cost as much
as the next three losses combined. The stop worked
every time; the size did not.

Common themes:
- Oversized (1/5) ← costs the most
- FOMO/chasing (2/5)
- Ignored HTF/regime (2/5)

Action: Pre-entry checklist (size, HTF, regime, no FOMO)

Most losses are preventable. Stop chasing. Stop ignoring context.

🎯 Monthly Review Checklist

  • Which strategy contributed most profit?
  • Which strategy had the best win rate and expectancy?
  • What's the common theme in losses?
  • Did I follow my rules (or break them)?
  • What regime performed best? Worst?

Do this every month. Compound learning, not just capital.

Part 4: Strategy-Specific Performance

Deep Dive: Which Strategy to Scale, Which to Kill

Janus Sweeps (Your Best Strategy) 
Trades: 45 (1R = $400)
Win rate: 71% (32 wins, 13 losses)
Avg winner: +1.4R
Avg loser: -1.0R
Expectancy: 32(1.4) - 13(1.0) = +31.8R over 45 trades
            = +0.71R per trade
Total P&L: +$12,720

Best conditions:
- Trending regime: 78% WR
- HTF aligned: 75% WR
- Multi-TF confluence: 82% WR

Worst conditions:
- Ranging regime: 58% WR (skip!)
- VIX > 25: 48% WR (avoid!)

Action: Allocate MORE capital to Janus in trending regimes
Breakouts (Marginal Strategy) 
Trades: 20 (1R = $400)
Win rate: 55% (11 wins, 9 losses)
Avg winner: +1.4R
Avg loser: -1.0R
Expectancy: 11(1.4) - 9(1.0) = +6.4R over 20 trades
            = +0.32R per trade
Total P&L: +$2,560

Analysis: Positive, but 20 trades proves nothing.
          At this win rate, a sample this small has
          a real chance of showing a profit from a
          strategy with no edge at all. Do not scale
          on 20 trades.

Action: Add volume filter (breakout vol > 2x avg),
        collect 100+ trades, THEN decide

Time-of-Day Performance

Window          Trades  WR    Expectancy
9:30-10:30 AM      12   50%   +0.10R  (choppy, skip)
10:30 AM-12 PM     35   74%   +0.85R  ← BEST window!
12 PM-2 PM          8   38%   -0.35R  (lunch chop, avoid)
2 PM-4 PM          20   60%   +0.40R  (OK)

Expectancy is per trade, after losers. The 74% window
is not 74% of a big number - it is a 1.5R average
winner against a 1R loser, 74% of the time.

Careful with the lunch row: 8 trades is not evidence.
It is a hint to keep counting.

Action: Focus 10:30 AM-12 PM, avoid lunch, reduce size 9:30-10:30

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Decompose returns: by strategy, asset, time period
  • Alpha vs. Beta: separate skill from market exposure
  • A handful of trades carries most of the profit (let winners run!)
  • Analyze losses: find patterns, create filters
  • Time-of-day matters: avoid low-WR windows
  • Review monthly: identify what works, eliminate what doesn't

📝 Practice Exercise

Conduct a Complete Performance Attribution Analysis

  1. Export your last 3 months of trade data
    • Get trade history from your broker or journal
    • Include: Date, time, symbol, strategy, entry, exit, P&L, R-multiple
  2. Calculate strategy-level attribution
    • Group trades by strategy (Janus sweeps, breakouts, mean reversion, etc.)
    • For each strategy: Total P&L, expectancy in R, payoff ratio, number of trades
    • Calculate percentage contribution: (Strategy P&L / Total P&L) × 100
  3. Identify your top 5 winners and bottom 5 losers
    • Sort all trades by P&L (largest gains to largest losses)
    • Analyze top 5: What setup? What timeframe? HTF aligned?
    • Analyze bottom 5: What went wrong? FOMO? Ignored rules? Bad regime?
  4. Calculate alpha vs. beta
    • Compare your return to SPY return for same period
    • Estimate your beta (if your portfolio moves 1.5x SPY, beta = 1.5)
    • Calculate alpha: Your return - (Beta × SPY return)
  5. Create action items
    • Which strategy to scale (highest Sharpe ratio)?
    • Which strategy to kill (negative or barely positive)?
    • What common mistake to fix (based on bottom 5 analysis)?

Goal: Know exactly where your returns came from and make data-driven decisions about what to scale and what to eliminate.

⚡ Quick Wins for Tomorrow (Click to expand)
  1. Compare your returns to SPY, with your beta in the calculation — subtracting the index return directly is the error Part 2 is about: it credits your leverage to your skill. Estimate your beta first (if your equity curve moves about 1.5x SPY, that is 1.5), then alpha = your return − beta × market return. Made 20% while SPY made 25% at a beta of 1.0? You underperformed by five points. At a beta of 0.5 you outperformed by seven and a half.
  2. Identify your top 5 trades — Export your trade log. Sort by P&L. Your top 5 winners are probably most of your profit. What did they have in common?
  3. Tag trades by strategy type — Add a "Strategy" column to your journal: Breakout, Reversal, Momentum, etc. Calculate P&L per strategy. Drop losers.

🎮 Test Your Understanding (No Pressure)

Question 1: You made +50% this year. SPY made +20%. Your beta is 2.0. What's your alpha?

A) +10% (50% - 2.0×20%)
B) +30% (50% - 20%)
C) +50% (total return = alpha)
D) Beta doesn't matter for alpha calculation
Correct: A. Alpha is what remains after the return your beta already entitled you to. At a beta of 2.0, a 20% market hands you 40% for nothing, so 50% − 40% = 10%. B is the common error of subtracting the index return directly, which credits the leverage to your skill. Doubling market exposure into a rising market is not alpha, it is beta.

Question 2: Your top 5 trades out of 50 total made 60% of your profit. What's the lesson?

A) Take fewer trades (lower quantity)
B) Let winners run (hold for 3R+ targets)
C) Only take "top 5" setups (cherry-pick)
D) 60% of trades were bad (stop trading)
Correct: B. A distribution where 5 of 50 trades carry 60% of the profit is normal rather than a defect — edges are usually concentrated in a small number of large winners. The actionable consequence is to protect that tail: cut losers at plan, and give winners the room to reach the larger multiples. C sounds like the same idea but is not available to you, because the top five are only identifiable afterwards.

Attribution is the difference between knowing that you made money and knowing why. It is at its most reliable when it tells you to stop something: a strategy running clearly negative over a decent sample is a finding you can act on cheaply, and being wrong about it costs you almost nothing. It is at its least reliable when it tells you to scale something, because the strategy that scores best is partly the strategy that got luckiest, and that part does not repeat. Run the analysis every month. Act on the negatives quickly and the positives carefully, and give every number you compute an error bar before you size a decision on it.
