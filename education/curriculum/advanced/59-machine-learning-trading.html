🎯 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • ML for trading: Feature engineering (what inputs), model selection (random forest, neural nets), overfitting prevention
  • Overfitting = model memorizes history instead of learning patterns
  • Compute the accuracy a filter has to beat before you build one: PF ÷ (PF + 1), where PF is the profit factor you already have

"Feed price data into a neural network → profit."

If only. Here's reality: most retail ML trading strategies fail live. Not because ML doesn't work—because traders misuse it.

Markets are non-stationary. Low signal-to-noise. ML overfits spectacularly if you're not careful.

🚨 Real Talk

ML isn't a magic money printer. It's pattern recognition on steroids. Use it wrong (data leakage, overfitting, insufficient data) and you'll backtest a 90% probability that goes 40% live. Use it right? It's a powerful filter for high-probability setups.

Part 1: What Machine Learning Can and Cannot Do Here

A Filter, Not a Forecaster

The useful way to think about machine learning in trading is not “what will price do” but “of the setups my method already produces, which ones should I skip”. As a forecaster a model is competing against every institution with better data and more compute. As a filter it is doing something much narrower and much more achievable: learning which of your own signals have historically been the weak ones.

Three properties of market data make this harder than the tutorials suggest, and all three have to be respected or the model will look brilliant and behave badly:

  • Markets are non-stationary. The relationship your model learned in 2021 may simply not exist in 2024, because the participants and the regime changed. A model trained once and left alone is decaying from the day it is deployed.
  • The signal-to-noise ratio is brutal. In most fields a model with more data does better. Here, most of the additional data is noise, and a flexible enough model will fit the noise perfectly and learn nothing.
  • Leakage is easy and invisible. Using the day’s high as a feature to predict the day’s direction, or splitting train and test at random rather than in time order, lets the model see the future. The backtest that results is not optimistic — it is meaningless.

And one thing that matters more than any of the above: accuracy is the wrong objective. A model that is right 90% of the time on trades that make 0.3R and wrong 10% of the time on trades that lose 3R has a negative expectancy and a beautiful accuracy score. What you want the model to raise is expectancy — the win rate and the payoff together — not the win rate alone. What follows is a trader who learned every one of these the expensive way.

⚠️ Before anything else: what an accuracy number can be

Nothing predicts next-day index direction at 95%. Or at 80%. Or at 70%. Daily equity index direction is close to a coin flip with a small upward drift, and honest published work — along with every practitioner willing to show their out-of-sample numbers — lands in the 52-57% band. A model at 56% is doing real work.

So when a backtest hands you 85% or 94%, you have not found an edge. You have found a bug, and it is almost always one of three: your features were computed across the whole series before you split it, so tomorrow's data is inside today's inputs; your test set leaked into training; or you are scoring the model on the same rows you fitted it to. Read a high accuracy number as a diagnostic, never as a result. The rest of this lesson is largely about the discipline that keeps that number honest.

Act 2: Learning the Hard Way (April-May 2024)

Nina's Rebuilding Process: Hired a prop trading mentor ($5K/month) who specialized in ML. Spent 6 weeks learning proper methodology.

Component V1 (Overfitted Disaster) V2 (Properly Validated)
Features 32 features incl. look-ahead bias (daily high/low, EOD volume) 12 features, zero look-ahead (RSI, VWAP distance, ATR, CVD at signal time)

Window 1 Q1-Q2 2023 Q3 2023 68% 1.4R Train: 71%, Test: 68% (3% gap = OK)

Key Insight: V2 measured 67% where V1 measured 87%, and the lower number is the more trustworthy one because it survived four windows the model was never fitted to. Be careful how much weight you put on it even so: four test windows drawn from a couple of hundred trades means each window holds a few dozen trades, and a few dozen trades measures a win rate to within roughly ten percentage points. What the four windows really rule out is the failure V1 had — a train score that collapses on unseen data. They do not pin down the true rate, and no amount of re-splitting the same trades will.

Act 3: What the Rebuild Establishes — and Where V2 Is Still Wrong

Nina deployed V2 the way you should: twenty paper signals first, then half size, then full, with monthly retraining and a rule for when a retrained model replaces the incumbent. The paper stage is worth reading correctly. Fourteen winners out of twenty is enough to catch a model that is broken — a model with a live bug tends to fail obviously, not subtly — and nowhere near enough to confirm a 67% rate. The standard error on twenty trades is about eleven percentage points.

What we are not going to give you is her live P&L. An earlier version of this page carried five months of it, a baseline comparison claiming the filter added $21,300, a "62.5% improvement" headline and a payback timeline. Every one of those numbers was invented, and worse, they were the wrong kind of evidence for the claim being made. Five months of one trader's filtered signals cannot separate "the filter works" from "the market suited these setups"; you would need the filter and the baseline running over the same trades in the same period, many times over, before the difference meant anything.

What the two versions genuinely establish:

  • V1's collapse is real evidence. 87% in backtest and 41% live on the same model is not bad luck. Each of the five mistakes has a known mechanism, and each one inflates a backtest in a direction you can predict in advance. That is the useful part of this story and it does not depend on anything that happened afterwards.
  • V2 removed those specific mechanisms. No look-ahead features, time-ordered splits, a smaller model, expectancy as the objective. Each change is defensible on its own logic before a single live trade.
  • The train-test gaps stayed small across four windows. That is the absence of a red flag rather than proof of an edge, and four windows on a few hundred trades is a small sample of a small sample.

Where V2 is still overfitting, by its own numbers. The comparison table sells the fix as "Random Forest, max_depth=4, 30 trees" against 240 trades. Thirty trees at depth four is on the order of four hundred split rules learned from 240 examples — still far more capacity than the data supports. It is a smaller mistake than a five-layer network on 180 trades, not a different kind of mistake. If you are working with a few hundred trades, the honest model is a logistic regression on three or four features, or a decision tree you can print on one page and read. If you cannot read the model, you cannot tell whether it learned your edge or your sample.

A second thing the table hides: a Random Forest's output is a fraction of trees voting, not a calibrated probability. A "0.65" does not mean a 65% chance of a winner. You can still use it as a ranking — take the top-scoring third of your signals — but treating the number as a probability, and especially inverting the low ones into short signals, is reading meaning into an arbitrary scale.

The honest summary of the whole episode: Nina replaced a process she could prove was broken with one whose failure modes she had at least tested for, and sized up slowly enough that being wrong a second time would have been survivable. That is the transferable lesson. Whether her filter still works today is a question only her own logged fills can answer, and the same is true of yours.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

🎮 Machine Learning Sanity Check

1. What role does the lesson give machine learning in a trading process?

A) It generates the trade ideas, replacing discretionary setups
B) It filters existing setups, predicting which ones carry higher expectancy
C) It sizes positions once a setup has triggered
D) It replaces backtesting, since the model validates itself
Correct: B. A model trained on your own setups can rank them; it has no way to invent an edge that was not in the data to begin with. A is the expectation that produces the overfitted disasters earlier in the lesson — asking a model to find a strategy rather than to grade one. D inverts the relationship: the model is the thing that needs validating, and it needs it more than a rule-based system does, not less.

2. You have a model at 80% accuracy and one at 55%. Which is the better trading model?

A) The 80% model, since accuracy is the point of a classifier
B) Whichever has more training data behind it
C) They are equivalent if both are statistically significant
D) Whichever produces the higher expectancy once reward-to-risk is included
Correct: D. Accuracy says how often you are right, not how much you make when you are — 80% at a poor payoff ratio loses money while 55% at 3R does not. A is the trap this lesson keeps returning to, and it is the same error the curriculum flags when a trader judges a strategy on win rate alone. C mistakes statistical significance for economic significance: a reliably measured bad edge is still a bad edge.

3. Why is a random train/test split invalid for a time-series trading model?

A) It leaks future information into training, so the model is graded on data it has effectively already seen
B) Random splits produce test sets that are too small
C) It is valid, provided the split is large enough
D) Because markets are non-stationary, so no validation works
Correct: A. Shuffling a time series puts tomorrow in the training set and yesterday in the test set, which is the one thing a live model never gets. The result looks excellent and does not survive contact with real data — the rolling-window approach exists precisely to prevent it. D is the fatalistic overcorrection: non-stationarity makes validation harder and makes walk-forward necessary, not pointless.

🎯 The accuracy a filter has to beat before it is worth having

This lesson says to judge a model on expectancy rather than accuracy, which is right and is where most treatments stop. But the two are connected by one line of algebra, and working it out gives you a threshold you can test any model against before you deploy it.

A filter does two things at once. It removes losers, which helps by one unit each. It also removes some winners, which hurts by your payoff ratio each. Take a strategy with win rate p, payoff b, and a balanced filter that correctly keeps a fraction a of winners and correctly rejects the same fraction a of losers. Filtering is worth doing only when

p × a × b  −  (1 − p)(1 − a)   ≥   p × b  −  (1 − p)

which rearranges to something short enough to remember. Writing PF for the profit factor you already have, p×b divided by (1 − p):

break-even accuracy  =  PF ÷ (PF + 1)

That is a strange and useful result, so read what it says before reading the table. The better your strategy already is, the more accurate a filter has to be before it helps at all. A good strategy has more to lose from every winner the model throws away.

profit factor without the filter   accuracy the filter must beat
                             1.0                            50 %
                             1.2                            55 %
                             1.5                            60 %
                             2.0                            67 %
                             3.0                            75 %
                             4.0                            80 %

Now set that against what a model on a few hundred trades honestly achieves out of sample. Not the 85 % or 94 % this lesson opens by calling a bug — the real number, on unseen data, is usually in the mid-fifties. Read the table again with that in hand. A filter at 55 % is worth having on a strategy with a profit factor of 1.2 and is destroying money on anything at 1.5 or better, because it is discarding winners faster than it is dodging losers.

So the honest use of ML here is narrower than the field suggests, and the lesson's own framing was right for a reason it did not give: a filter helps a marginal strategy and hurts a good one. If your profit factor is already 2, you need a genuinely 67 %-accurate out-of-sample classifier on financial data, and you almost certainly do not have one.

Two simplifications, stated so you can allow for them. The bound assumes a balanced model, and it assumes the trades the filter keeps have the same payoff ratio as the ones it drops. An unbalanced model — much better at rejecting losers than at retaining winners — can beat this threshold, and that is precisely the direction worth building toward. Tune for the losers you avoid, not for the accuracy score.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • ML is a filter, not a strategy: Use it to predict which setups have higher expectancy, not to generate trades
  • Feature choice matters more than model choice: and choosing fewer features matters most of all. Every feature you add is another way for the model to fit your sample rather than your edge.
  • A filter must beat PF ÷ (PF + 1) to be worth having: at a profit factor of 1.5 that is 60 % out-of-sample accuracy, at 3.0 it is 75 %. Since honest out-of-sample accuracy on a few hundred trades is usually in the mid-fifties, ML filtering helps a marginal strategy and destroys a good one — the better your edge, the more every discarded winner costs
  • Walk-forward validation is mandatory: Train/test split on time series = data leakage. Use rolling windows
  • Optimize for expectancy: 80% accuracy at a poor payoff loses money; 55% accuracy at 3R does not. Accuracy is the metric a classifier reports by default and almost never the one you are paid on.
  • Avoid look-ahead bias: Features must use ONLY data available at prediction time
  • Match model capacity to sample size, not to fashion: a forest beats a network on a few hundred trades because the data supports fewer parameters, not because forests are better. With 200 trades the honest model is a logistic regression on three or four features, or a tree you can read on one page.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Implement ML Feature Engineering for Trade Filtering

Objective: Build the smallest model that can rank your existing setups, and find out honestly whether it ranks them at all. Note what this exercise does not promise: an improvement figure. Whether a filter helps you is an empirical question about your own trades, and a target number stated in advance is the thing that tempts people to keep tuning until they hit it.

Part 1: Feature Engineering (The Most Important Step)

For each of your historical trades, calculate these features AT TIME OF SIGNAL (no look-ahead!). But first, the constraint that governs the whole exercise:

Pick three to six features. Not twenty. A rough floor is ten trades per feature before a model has any chance of learning a relationship rather than memorising your sample — and that is a floor, not a target. With sixty logged trades you can support six features at the very outside and you will get a more honest answer from three. This is the same arithmetic that killed V1: thirty-two features on 180 trades is under six trades per feature, which is not a model, it is a lookup table. The list below is a menu to choose from, not a set to use.

Choose the three or four you can state a reason for in one sentence — "my losers cluster when I enter far from VWAP", "my losers cluster in the first fifteen minutes" — and leave the rest out. If you cannot say why a feature should matter, the model will find a relationship in it anyway, and that relationship will be noise.

Part 5: Production Deployment with Confidence Thresholds

Don't trade ALL predictions. Only trade high-confidence ones:

Model Output = Probability (0.0 to 1.0)

Confidence Thresholds:
Probability > 0.65 = High confidence (take trade)
Probability 0.45-0.65 = Neutral (skip trade)
Probability < 0.45 = Low confidence (skip -- do NOT invert)

A note on what these numbers are. A Random Forest score is the fraction of
trees voting yes. It is not a calibrated probability: 0.65 does not mean a
65% chance of a winner, and the gap between 0.65 and 0.45 has no fixed
meaning in trades. Use the score as a ranking -- take the top third of your
signals -- rather than as a probability, and re-check the cut-off on each
retrain, because the scale drifts when the model does.

And never trade the low scores in reverse. A model that cannot find a reason
to take a trade has not thereby found a reason to short it; it has found
nothing. Inverting turns "no information" into a position that pays costs.

Backtest Results by Threshold:

Confidence > 0.60: ___% expectancy, ___R avg
Confidence > 0.65: ___% expectancy, ___R avg
Confidence > 0.70: ___% expectancy, ___R avg (fewer trades)

Optimal Threshold: 0.___ (maximize expectancy, not accuracy)

YOUR RESULTS:

Part 6: Monitoring and Retraining Protocol

ML models degrade over time. Monitor and retrain:

Monitoring Schedule:

Week 1-4: Track live performance vs test predictions
Expected: 60% accuracy
Actual: ___% accuracy
Drift: ___% (< 5% OK)

Week 5-8: Continue tracking
Actual: ___% accuracy
Drift: ___% (if > 10%, retrain)

Retraining Trigger:
1. Accuracy drops > 10% below test baseline
2. Market regime shifts (VIX spikes, new cycle)
3. Every 3 months minimum (refresh with recent data)

Retrain Process:
- Add last 3 months of new trade data
- Re-run walk-forward validation
- Compare new model to old model on same test set
- Deploy only if new model outperforms by 3%+

Implementation Goal: Build the filter over 4-6 weeks using your last 50-100 trades, then run it on 20 paper signals. The result you are looking for is not an improvement percentage — on this sample size you could not measure one reliably if it existed. It is an answer to a narrower question: does the model rank your trades at all? Sort your logged trades by model score and compare the top third against the bottom third. If the two groups have indistinguishable outcomes, the model has learned nothing and the correct action is to stop, not to add features. That negative result is worth the four weeks; it is the same finding V1 needed and did not get until it had cost real money.

The three failures in this lesson — look-ahead features, a shuffled split, and more model than data — share one property: each makes a backtest look better in a way that is invisible from inside the backtest. That is why the discipline has to be procedural rather than intuitive. You cannot tell by looking at a good result whether it came from an edge or from one of these, which is exactly why the process has to rule them out before the result arrives.
