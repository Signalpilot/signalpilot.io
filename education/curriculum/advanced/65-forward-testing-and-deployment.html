System Development: Your Edge, Codified & Automated

You found an edge. It works. But you can only trade it 8 hours a day.

What if your edge could trade 24/7? No emotions. No missed setups. Perfect execution.

That's systematic trading. And it's how professionals scale.

What a system actually buys you

Discretionary trading is limited by you: your attention, your hours, your patience. A system is limited by something else — the liquidity of what it trades and the capacity of the edge itself, both of which are real ceilings, not infinite ones. What a system buys you is not unlimited scale. It is the ability to run the same edge across more instruments and more hours than one person can watch, IF you can codify it.

Part 1: What a Trading System Is, and What Each Stage Is For

Every Stage Exists to Kill the Idea

A trading system is a set of rules complete enough that another person — or a machine — could execute it without asking you a single question. That is the whole definition, and it is a harder standard than it sounds: most discretionary edges dissolve the moment you try to write them down, because the part doing the work turns out to be a judgement you were making without noticing.

The development pipeline has five stages, and it is worth being clear about what each one is for. Every stage exists to kill the idea. If the idea survives all five, you have something; if you skip a stage, you have removed one of the ways it could have been killed, and you will find out which one in live trading, with money on it.

  • Codify. Write the rules until nothing is left to interpretation. This stage kills ideas that were never rules, only instincts.
  • Backtest. Run the rules over history with realistic costs. This stage kills ideas that never worked, and — if you are careless — convinces you of ideas that only worked because you fitted them to the sample.
  • Walk forward. Fit on one period, test on the next, roll forward, repeat. This stage kills the ideas that survived the backtest only by being fitted to it, which is the single most common way a system reaches live trading dead.
  • Paper trade. Run it live without money. This stage kills ideas whose rules cannot actually be executed in real time — the data arrives late, the fill was never available, the signal repaints.
  • Live, small. Real money, a fraction of your intended size. This stage kills ideas that work on paper and not against real costs, real fills and real slippage.

The stage most people skip is walk-forward, because a backtest that has already been fitted to its data looks wonderful and walking forward is what takes the shine off. What follows is a trader who skipped it.

Act 2: First Automation Attempt (Q1 2023) - The Overfitted Disaster

Tom's Approach: Spent 6 weeks coding Python backtest, tested on 2020-2022 data (same period he traded discretionary)

Tom's V1 System: Backtest vs. Reality (The Curve-Fitted Disaster)
Metric Backtest (2020-2022) Live Q1 2023 Gap
Win Rate 78.4% 48.2% -30.2% (MASSIVE!)
Avg R 2.1R -0.2R -2.3R gap!
Monthly Return +9.2% -3.8% -13% gap!!!
P&L (3 months) +$96,600 (projected) -$39,900 (actual) $136,500 disaster!

What Went Wrong? The 6 Fatal Mistakes (78% backtest → 48% live):

  1. In-Sample Only — Tested on same 2020-2022 data. System memorized patterns, no out-of-sample validation
  2. Parameter Overfitting — 8 parameters optimized (ADX>23.4, RSI<38.7). Curve-fitted to noise
  3. Ignored Transaction Costs — Zero slippage and commission in the backtest. Worth being precise about how much this actually cost him, because it is less than people assume: on SPY the round-trip cost at his size was single-digit dollars a trade, which is a few percent of the gross profit per trade, not a third of it. Costs were a real omission and a sign of a careless process, but they are not what turned 78% into 48%. Overfitting did that. Get the diagnosis right or you will fix the cheap problem and keep the expensive one.
  4. No Walk-Forward — Single backtest, no rolling windows. Didn't test degradation on new data
  5. Skipped Paper Trading — Straight to live, full size. Could have saved $40K
  6. Execution Assumptions — Assumed fills at the closing price of the signal bar. In reality the signal is only known once that bar has closed, so the earliest available fill is the next bar's open. That is not a slippage adjustment, it is a bar of look-ahead built into the backtest, and on a momentum system it flatters results in exactly the direction the system trades.

The Reckoning (March 31, 2023):

"I lost $39,900 in 3 months with my 'automated system.' Backtest showed 78% wins. Reality? 48%. I curve-fitted the shit out of it.

I optimized 8 parameters on historical data I ALREADY KNEW. Of course it looked perfect. Then live trading hit and it fell apart.

Transaction costs killed me. I ignored slippage, commissions, spreads. My backtest showed +$96K, but real costs ate -$23,900.

Back to the drawing board. This time, I'm doing it right."

— Tom Anderson, March 31, 2023 journal entry

Transaction Cost Reality Check — and the Rule That Actually Governs It:

V1 assumed zero costs. V2 modelled them, and this is where most write-ups of this subject go wrong. The standard claim is that costs take 29% of the edge, on an assumed 0.08% of slippage per trade. On SPY at $520 a share, 0.08% is 42 cents. The actual spread on SPY in regular hours is a penny — about 0.002% — so the assumed cost was roughly twenty times the real one, and the 29% that followed from it was an artefact of the assumption, not a finding.

Here is the arithmetic you can check. Say the system trades 200 shares of SPY per position, entering and exiting with limit orders that rest rather than cross:

  • Spread cost: a penny a share, paid on entry and exit if you cross, and close to nothing if you rest. Crossing both sides: 200 shares × $0.01 × 2 = $4.
  • Commission: $1 a side at a per-share broker, so $2 round trip.
  • Total: about $6 a round trip, which is what you should put in the backtest until your own fill records say otherwise.

Against an average gross profit of a few hundred dollars a trade, $6 is a rounding error. Against an average gross profit of $15, it is most of the edge. That is the rule, and it is not the one people usually state:

Cost drag is the ratio of cost per trade to gross edge per trade. Trade count does not enter it. Double the number of trades and you double the costs, but you also double the gross profit; the fraction is unchanged. What makes high-frequency systems cost-sensitive is not that they pay costs more often — it is that their edge per trade is small, so the same $6 is a large share of it. A system that scalps eight ticks and a system that holds for two days pay identical costs per round trip and are affected completely differently.

Which means the honest way to run this check on your own system is not to apply somebody else's percentage. It is to divide your modelled cost per round trip by your backtest's average gross profit per trade. If that number is under 5%, costs are not your problem and you should stop worrying about them. If it is over 30%, your system is a cost-arbitrage business and every remaining decision — broker, order type, routing — matters more than the signal does.

One thing costs do reliably change is the marginal trade. Trades whose expected value was slightly positive before costs are negative after them, so a properly costed backtest should show fewer trades taken, not just the same trades earning less. If your cost model only subtracts a fixed amount from every result and never removes a trade from the set, it is not modelling costs; it is applying a haircut.

Paper Trading Validation (August 2023, 50 trades):

V2 Paper Trading: Reality Check Before Live Money
Metric Walk-Forward Test Paper Trading Gap
Win Rate 59% 56% -3pp (inside the noise band)
Avg R 0.78R 0.71R -0.07R (acceptable)
Max Drawdown -14% -16% +2% (within tolerance)
WHAT THIS ESTABLISHES: ✓ The rules are executable in real time

Read that table for what it can support, not for what it looks like it supports. Fifty trades at a 59% expected win rate has a standard error of about seven percentage points, so anything from roughly 45% to 73% is an ordinary result for a system that genuinely wins 59% of the time. A 3-point gap is therefore not evidence that the system works; it is the absence of evidence that it is broken, which is a much weaker and much more useful thing. If the paper result had come in at 38%, that would have been informative — five standard errors below is not noise. The asymmetry is the point: a paper stage can refute a system, and cannot confirm one.

What the paper stage genuinely does confirm, and what nothing before it could, is that the rules can be executed as written. The data arrives in time. The signal does not repaint after the bar closes. The order type exists at the broker. The position sizing does not demand fractional shares the account cannot hold. Those are the failures paper trading is for, and every one of them is a yes-or-no answer that fifty trades is plenty to establish.

Act 4: What Happened Next — and What It Can and Cannot Show

Tom deployed the way the pipeline says to: paper first, then a quarter of intended size, then half, then full, with each step conditional on the previous one behaving. That ramp is the part of this story worth copying, and it is worth copying for a reason that has nothing to do with how it turned out. Size that follows evidence rather than confidence is correct whether the system goes on to work or not, because it is the only sizing rule that costs you little when you are wrong.

What we are deliberately not going to do here is give you his live results. Not because they were bad, and not because we are hiding them — but because a single trader's fourteen months of one system tells you nothing you could act on. Fourteen months is roughly one market regime. A system that made money through it might be sound, or might be a momentum system that happened to run during a trending stretch; the record cannot distinguish those two, and neither can Tom. If we printed a number here, the only thing it would reliably do is make you feel that this pipeline produces that number, which is exactly the inference the lesson has spent four stages telling you not to make.

What the two versions genuinely establish:

  • The V1 gap is real evidence, because it is a disaster. A backtest showing 78% and live trading showing 48% on the same rules is not ambiguous. Something in the process manufactured 30 points of win rate that did not exist. That inference runs in the direction that works: you can prove a method was broken from one bad outcome far more easily than you can prove one was sound from one good outcome.
  • The V2 process removed the specific mechanism that produced the gap. Eight parameters fitted to three years of data he had already traded became three parameters tested on data the fitting never saw. That is a defensible change on its own logic, before any results arrive.
  • The train-test gaps stayed small across five windows. Five windows is not many, and a 5–8% gap is not a guarantee of anything. But the alternative — a gap that widens window after window — would have been decisive evidence against, and it did not appear.

What no version of this story establishes: that a properly validated system earns a particular income, that systematic trading beats discretionary, or that Tom's rules will keep working. His discretionary year and his systematic year were different years in different markets; comparing their income is comparing two market regimes as much as two methods. The honest summary is that he replaced a process he could prove was broken with one whose failure mode he had at least tested for, and then sized up slowly enough that being wrong again would have been survivable.

The claim you will meet everywhere, and why it is wrong. The standard ending is "systematic trading scales infinitely — if you do it right", with a five-year projection of five systems at $50K each and a table listing scalability as "Unlimited". None of that is true, and the box at the top of this lesson already says why: a system is capped by the liquidity of what it trades and by how long the edge survives being traded. It is also capped by something subtler — your five systems are unlikely to be five independent edges. Most retail systems are momentum or mean reversion wearing different parameters, and running five correlated systems is running one system at five times the size, which is the opposite of what the projection assumed.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

🎮 System Validation Check

1. A backtest returns +40% annually. You then add realistic transaction costs. What should you expect?

A) Almost no change, since costs are small per trade
B) The result improves, because costs filter out marginal trades
C) It depends on the ratio of cost per trade to gross profit per trade, and on nothing else — you cannot answer without both numbers
D) The result becomes unmeasurable
Correct: C. Trade count cancels: double the trades and you double both the costs and the gross profit, leaving the fraction unchanged. What decides the damage is cost per round trip divided by gross profit per round trip. Six dollars against $400 of gross edge is nothing; six dollars against $20 is most of it. A is right for a system with a large edge per trade and badly wrong for a scalping system, which is why it is not a general answer. B has a grain of truth — costs do make marginal trades negative — but that shows up as fewer trades taken, not as a higher return on the same set. D is wrong because the calculation is entirely measurable once you have your own fill records.

2. You want to know whether a system will hold up on data it has never seen. Which method actually answers that?

A) In-sample optimisation across the full history
B) Walk-forward testing — train on one period, test on the next, repeatedly
C) Running the backtest on more tickers
D) Increasing the sample size by using a shorter timeframe
Correct: B. Walk-forward is the only method here that ever shows the system data it was not fitted to, and it does so repeatedly rather than once. A is the definition of the problem: optimising over everything guarantees the parameters fit that history. C adds breadth but not out-of-sample discipline. D increases the number of bars without increasing the number of independent market conditions, which is what actually matters.

3. Your walk-forward results are acceptable. What does the lesson say about going live?

A) Deploy at full size, since the validation is done
B) Deploy at full size but with a tighter stop
C) Skip paper trading, which only repeats the backtest
D) Step up incrementally: paper, then micro, then small, then full
Correct: D. Each stage tests something the previous one could not: paper trading catches implementation errors, micro size adds real fills and real slippage, and small size adds the psychology of money at risk. A treats validation as binary when the failure modes appear at different stages. C is the specific error — paper trading does not repeat the backtest, it tests whether your execution matches its assumptions.

🎯 How big a search does it take to manufacture a 30-point gap out of nothing?

This lesson says eight parameters is too many and calls it curve-fitting to noise. That is right, but it is a warning rather than a measurement, and the case study gives you enough to measure it. Tom's backtest showed a 78.4 % win rate and his live trading showed 48.2 %. The question worth answering is how much of that 30.2-point gap a search of his size produces even when the strategy has no edge at all.

Two ingredients. First, how noisy a win rate is on a finite sample: its standard error is the square root of p(1 − p) ÷ n. At a win rate near half and, say, 250 in-sample trades — generous for three years of a swing system — that is 3.2 points. Second, what happens when you keep the best of many attempts: the largest of N roughly independent draws sits about the square root of 2 × ln(N) standard errors above the mean. Multiply the two.

Now note what the lesson already told you about his grid. He reported thresholds of ADX above 23.4 and RSI below 38.7 — one decimal place, which means he searched in steps of 0.1. Over a plausible range that is a couple of hundred candidate values for each of eight parameters:

values tried per parameter   combinations   expected lift from luck alone
                         1              1                      0.0 points
                         2            2^8                     10.5 points
                         5            5^8                     16.0 points
                        10           10^8                     19.2 points
                       250          250^8                     29.7 points

The bottom row is his. A search that fine, on a sample that small, is expected to produce a 30-point in-sample lift from a strategy with no edge whatsoever. His entire gap fits inside what the search alone can generate. That does not prove his idea was worthless — it proves the backtest could not tell him either way, which is worse, because he sized as though it had.

One honest qualification, because the number is a ceiling rather than an estimate: grid-search results are heavily correlated with one another, not independent, so the real inflation is smaller than the table says. The order of magnitude survives, and so does the conclusion.

Now run it backwards, which is the part you can use tonight. Ask how much searching you can afford before luck exceeds some tolerance — say three points, small enough not to fool you. On 250 trades, with a standard error of 3.2 points, that allows a square root of 2 × ln(N) of under 1, which means fewer than two configurations. Not two per parameter. Two in total.

That is the real reason walk-forward is not a refinement. On the sample sizes any retail trader actually has, there is no budget for optimisation at all, so the only honest way to spend one is to buy it back afterwards by testing on data the fitting never touched. Every configuration you try in-sample is borrowed against that test.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • What systematizing actually buys you: the same edge running across more instruments and more hours than one person can watch. Not an unlimited ceiling — a system is still capped by the liquidity of what it trades and by how long the edge survives.
  • Walk-forward testing: the only stage that ever shows the rules data they were not fitted to, and it does so repeatedly rather than once. A gap that widens window after window is the finding; a small gap is only the absence of a red flag.
  • Monte Carlo tells you about sequence, not about the future: reshuffling your trades shows how bad the ordering could have been given the returns you observed. It cannot tell you about a regime that produces returns you have not observed, which is the risk that actually ends systems.
  • Cost the backtest from your own fills: the damage is cost per round trip divided by gross profit per round trip. Under 5% and it is not your problem; over 30% and the broker and the order type matter more than the signal.
  • Deploy incrementally: Paper → Micro size → Small → Full (validate at each stage)
  • Avoid overfitting, and know what it is worth in points: the expected in-sample lift from searching alone is the standard error of your win rate times the square root of 2 × ln(configurations tried). On 250 trades and eight parameters at a 0.1 grid, that is about 30 points — the whole of the case study's backtest-to-live gap, available to a strategy with no edge at all

🎯 Practice Exercise: Build and Test a Systematic Strategy

Objective: Take a discretionary edge, codify it into precise rules, backtest properly, and deploy with confidence.

6-Step System Development Process:

  1. Codify Rules — Convert discretionary setup to exact entry/exit conditions (5+ rules). Example: "Price sweeps 15-min low by $0.20+, reclaims within 5 candles, ADX>22"
  2. Backtest (200+ trades, 2+ years) — Track win rate, average R, maximum drawdown and Sharpe, but treat all four as estimates with error bars rather than thresholds to clear. At 200 trades a measured 55% win rate is consistent with anything from about 48% to 62%, and a Sharpe of 1.0 over two years is consistent with a true Sharpe anywhere from roughly 0 to 2. Write down the range, not the point.
  3. Walk-Forward Validation — Split into 6-month chunks, train→test rolling. If test performance 30%+ worse than train = overfitting
  4. Monte Carlo Stress Test — Reshuffle the trade sequence a thousand times and read the drawdown distribution. This answers one question well: given these returns, how unlucky could the ordering have been? It does not answer what happens if the returns themselves change, so treat the 95th-percentile drawdown as a floor on your worst case, never as a ceiling.
  5. Live Deployment (3 stages) — Paper (50 trades, win rate within 5% of backtest) → Micro size (30 trades, 25% size) → Full size (100%). If fails any stage, return to backtest
  6. Transaction Cost Reality — Model the spread from the instrument's actual quote and the commission from your actual schedule, then divide the round-trip cost by the gross profit per trade. Do not import a percentage from an article; on liquid instruments the numbers people quote are an order of magnitude too high, and on illiquid ones they are too low.

Goal: Complete steps 1-4 over 2-4 weeks, then paper trade at least fifty trades. Go live small unless paper trading gave you a reason not to — and be clear which kind of reason it was. An execution failure (late data, a repainting signal, an order type your broker will not accept) is disqualifying and fixable. A win rate a few points below the backtest is neither; fifty trades cannot resolve a gap that small. Most systems that fail here fail on execution, and finding that out with no money on it is the entire purpose of the stage.

The pipeline in this lesson is not a guarantee that a system works. It is a sequence of five chances to find out that one does not, arranged cheapest-first, so that the discovery costs you a weekend of coding rather than a quarter of returns. Tom's V1 skipped four of them and paid for the information at the last and most expensive stage. That is the whole argument for doing it in order.
