Market Impact Models: The Hidden Cost of Size

Market impact is the price movement caused by your order. For retail traders buying 100 shares of a megacap, it's negligible — though Part 3 works out how small a stock has to be before that stops being true, and the answer is closer to home than most retail traders expect. For institutions buying 500,000 shares, it's the difference between profit and loss. Understanding market impact models lets you predict how large orders move price—and exploit them.

💰 The $1 Million Mistake

A hedge fund decides to buy $100M of NVDA (approximately 800K shares @ $125). They submit a market order, thinking the spread ($0.01) is the only cost. Within minutes, their order exhausts the order book, pushing price from $125.00 to $127.50 before the last child order fills. The average fill comes in at $126.25 — $1.25 above the price they saw when they hit send.

Cost breakdown: Commission: $0. Spread: $8,000 (800K × $0.01). Market impact: $1,000,000 (800K × $1.25 average slippage).

Now notice what that number actually measures. 800K shares against NVDA's daily volume of roughly 300M is about a quarter of one percent of a day. The square-root law in Part 2, applied to that order at a 3% daily volatility, gives 0.16% — around 19 cents a share, or $155,000, if the same order were worked patiently across a session. The $1,000,000 is not what the size costs. It is what the impatience costs: the difference between working an order and sweeping the displayed book in one go.

Lesson: Impact is not a penalty for being large. It is a penalty for demanding liquidity faster than the book replenishes, and the gap between those two numbers — $155,000 and $1,000,000 — is what the rest of this lesson is about.

Part 1: What Is Market Impact?

Definition & Components

Market impact: Price change caused by executing a trade

Total cost = Commission + Spread + Market Impact

Component Description Typical Cost
Commission Broker fee $0 (zero-commission era)
Spread Bid-ask difference 0.01-0.1% (liquid stocks)
Market Impact Price movement from order 0.1-5% (depends on size/liquidity)

💡 Key Insight: For institutional traders, market impact often exceeds spread + commission by 10-100×. A $50M order might cause 0.5-2% slippage = $250K-$1M cost — but only in a stock where $50M is a meaningful share of the day's volume. The same $50M in a megacap is a rounding error on the tape and costs a few basis points. Impact is never a function of the dollar size of your order; it is a function of that size relative to what the stock trades.

Temporary vs Permanent Impact

Temporary impact: Price movement during execution that reverts after your order completes. Caused by temporary supply/demand imbalance. Liquidity providers step in to absorb the imbalance, returning price to fair value.

Permanent impact: Price movement that persists after execution. Market interprets your order as containing information (informed trading), so fair value reprices.

Worked Example (constructed): Large AAPL Purchase

Scenario: A large asset manager buys 500K shares of AAPL over 1 hour (average daily volume = 50M shares, so 1% of ADV). One percent of a day's volume compressed into one hour is roughly a 6-7% participation rate — aggressive, and the impact figures below reflect that.

Timeline:

Time Price Event
10:00 AM (Start) $150.00 Decision price (the manager commits to the trade)
10:15 AM $150.50 First 125K shares filled, price rising
10:30 AM $150.85 250K shares filled (halfway), temporary impact building
10:45 AM $151.05 375K shares filled, peak impact (exhausting nearby liquidity)
11:00 AM (Done) $151.00 All 500K shares filled; volume-weighted average fill across the hour = $150.70
11:30 AM (After) $150.40 Price settles (temporary impact reverts)

Impact Decomposition:

  • Total impact during execution: $150.70 (volume-weighted average fill, not the last print) - $150.00 (decision price) = $0.70 per share
  • Permanent impact: $150.40 (settlement price) - $150.00 (decision price) = $0.40 per share
  • Temporary impact (reverted): $0.70 - $0.40 = $0.30 per share

Total Cost:

  • 500K shares × $0.70 average impact = $350,000 total market impact cost
  • Of which $150,000 was temporary (reverted after), $200,000 was permanent

Why Permanent Impact Occurred: The market saw large sustained buying (1% of ADV in 1 hour is significant). Other participants inferred the buyer might have positive information (earnings, an upgrade), so fair value repriced upward.

Hold this number against Part 2. The square-root law, applied to the same 500K shares of the same stock, gives 0.15% — about $0.225 per share. This execution cost $0.70. The law is calibrated to orders worked across a full session; this one was crammed into an hour at roughly six times that participation rate, and the realized cost came in roughly three times the estimate. The formula gives you a floor for patient execution, not a forecast for impatient execution.

⚠️ The Information Leakage Problem

When you execute large orders, the market reverse-engineers your intent. If market thinks you're informed (hedge fund buying on insider info), permanent impact skyrockets. If market thinks you're uninformed (index fund rebalancing), permanent impact is minimal.

Defense: Disguise your orders using randomized timing, splitting across venues (dark pools + lit exchanges), and varying order sizes to look like noise rather than signal.

Part 1.5: Michael's $47,500 Market Order Disaster

💀 The Setup

Michael Rodriguez (composite example), $50M hedge fund PM, March 2024. Wanted $5M NVDA position (38,500 shares @ $130). NVDA ADV: 250M shares = 0.015% of daily volume. Thought: "tiny position = no impact."

Fatal mistake: Didn't account for INTRADAY volume. At 10:30 AM, volume was ~150K/min. His 38K share order = 5.1% of 5-minute volume (MASSIVE).

What Happened

First order (10:30 AM): Market order for 38,461 shares. Exhausted order book $130.01 → $130.70 in 60 seconds. Impact: $11K slippage.

The cascade: NVDA rallied to $131.80 (other algos detected institutional flow). Michael thought "going higher, buy more!" Added 15,174 more shares at $132.40 avg. Then NVDA reverted to $130.25 (temporary impact unwound).

Same day on AMD: Repeated the same mistake on a $3M AMD position (16,216 shares). AMD is thinner: at 10:45 AM it was printing about 40K shares a minute, so his order was roughly 40% of a minute's volume. The sweep ran $185.10 → $187.30, average fill $186.00 — $14,600 of impact. AMD settled back to $185.20, leaving him $13,000 underwater on the position.

Total damage: about $47,500 in one morning — $34,500 on NVDA (mostly the chase, not the first order) and $13,000 on AMD. On $10M deployed that is 0.47%, roughly ten times what the same two positions should have cost, and it came out of a $50M fund in under two hours.

Why the headline number is not bigger: market impact in megacap names is measured in tens of basis points, not percent. If you see an execution-cost story quoted in hundreds of thousands on a $10M ticket, the loss is coming from direction, not from impact. Michael's error was expensive because it was needless, not because it was enormous.

The 5 Fatal Mistakes

  1. Confused daily vs intraday volume — 0.015% daily = 5.1% of 5-minute window
  2. Market orders on large size — Should've used VWAP/TWAP
  3. No slippage estimation — Never calculated expected impact
  4. Chased his own impact — Bought more at peak of temporary move
  5. Repeated error on AMD — Less liquid stock = worse impact

Michael's Recovery: Professional Execution (6 Months Later)

The New Methodology

Example: $5M TSLA position (Sept 2024)

  • Pre-trade: Calculate intraday volume pattern, estimate impact using square-root law
  • Strategy: VWAP algo over 2.5 hours, participation capped at 3% (a $5M ticket in TSLA never comes close to the cap — the cap is there for the minutes when volume dries up)
  • Pause during lunch (low volume), resume at 2 PM
  • Expected cost, from the model rather than from a report: $5M of TSLA at $250 is 20,000 shares. Against a 100M-share day that is 0.02% of volume, so the square-root law at a 3% daily volatility gives 0.03 × √0.0002 = 0.042%, or about 10.6 cents a share — roughly $2,100 on the ticket.

Run that against his March morning and the lesson is arithmetic, not testimony. He paid 0.47% on $10M. The same two orders worked at a sane participation rate should have cost on the order of five basis points. The gap is a factor of ten, and every bit of it came from the rate at which he demanded liquidity rather than from anything about the positions themselves.

What is worth taking from the recovery is the method, not a performance figure: estimate before you send, size the schedule against intraday volume rather than daily volume, and never let your own impact be the reason you add. There is no honest way to report what disciplined execution "saved" him, because the counterfactual — what the same orders would have cost had he traded them badly — is not observable. The estimate above is checkable. A saving is not.

"I used to treat execution as a race. Now I treat it as a puzzle. The market tells you when it has liquidity. Listen to it, blend in, and you pay minimal impact." — Michael Rodriguez

Part 2: Market Impact Models

Model #1: Square-Root Law (Simplest)

Formula: Impact = σ × √(Q / V)

  • σ: Daily volatility (standard deviation)
  • Q: Order size (shares)
  • V: Daily volume (shares)
Square-Root Law Example

Stock: AAPL

  • Daily volatility (σ): 1.5% = 0.015
  • Average daily volume (V): 50M shares
  • Your order size (Q): 500K shares

Calculation:

Impact = 0.015 × √(500,000 / 50,000,000)

= 0.015 × √0.01

= 0.015 × 0.1

= 0.0015 = 0.15%

Interpretation: Buying 500K shares (1% of daily volume) will move price ~0.15%

Cost: If AAPL = $150, impact = $150 × 0.0015 = $0.225/share → total impact cost = 500K × $0.225 = $112,500

Model #2: Almgren-Chriss Model (Time-Dependent)

Concept: Impact depends on HOW FAST you trade. The model balances two competing costs:

  1. Market impact: Aggressive execution → high temporary impact
  2. Execution risk (price drift): Patient execution → price might move unfavorably

Formula (simplified):

Total Cost = Temporary Impact + Permanent Impact + Execution Risk

  • Temporary Impact: θ × (Q / T) where θ is impact coefficient, Q is order size, T is time horizon
  • Permanent Impact: η × Q (linear in order size)
  • Execution Risk: σ × √T × Q (volatility × the square root of time × size — risk grows with √T, not with T)

One caveat on the temporary-impact term: the original Almgren-Chriss paper makes it linear in the trading rate, as written above. The worked example below instead uses the square-root scaling from Model #1, which is what execution desks actually calibrate against. The shape of the trade-off — and where the minimum sits — is the same either way; only the numbers shift.

Trade-off (The Central Dilemma):

  • Trade fast (T small): High temporary impact (θ/T large), but low execution risk (less time for adverse price movement)
  • Trade slow (T large): Low temporary impact (θ/T small), but high execution risk (price might drift away during slow execution)

Almgren-Chriss Trade-off Example

Scenario: Buy 1M shares of TSLA. Daily volume = 100M shares. Current price = $200. Daily volatility = 3%.

Option 1: Aggressive (Execute in 30 minutes)

  • Temporary impact: High (1M shares / 30 min = 33K/min, market can't absorb that fast)
  • Estimated impact: 0.8% × $200 = $1.60/share → $1.6M impact cost
  • Execution risk: Low — 3% daily volatility over half an hour of a 6.5-hour session is 3% × √(0.5/6.5) = 0.83%
  • Estimated risk cost: $530K
  • Total cost: $2.13M

Option 2: Patient (Execute over 4 hours)

  • Temporary impact: Low (1M shares / 240 min = 4.2K/min, manageable rate)
  • Estimated impact: 0.25% × $200 = $0.50/share → $500K impact cost
  • Execution risk: High — 3% × √(4/6.5) = 2.35% over the four hours. Note the scaling: four hours is not four days, so the daily 3% shrinks by the square root of the fraction of the session used, it does not multiply by four
  • Estimated risk cost (adverse movement): $1.5M (if unlucky, the stock rallies during execution)
  • Total cost: $2.0M

Option 3: Optimal (Almgren-Chriss Solution: 90 minutes)

  • Temporary impact: Moderate (1M / 90 min = 11K/min)
  • Estimated impact: 0.45% × $200 = $0.90/share → $900K impact cost
  • Execution risk: Moderate — 3% × √(1.5/6.5) = 1.44% over the ninety minutes
  • Estimated risk cost: $920K
  • Total cost: $1.82M (BEST)

Check the shape yourself. Impact falls as 1/√T ($1.6M, $900K, $500K at 0.5, 1.5 and 4 hours — each is the previous one divided by the square root of the time ratio). Risk rises as √T ($530K, $920K, $1.5M on the same grid). One curve falls, the other rises, and the sum has a single minimum. Here it sits at ninety minutes.

What the model does not give you: the absolute level of the risk term depends on how much variance the manager is willing to carry — Almgren-Chriss has a risk-aversion parameter, and moving it slides the optimum earlier or later. The √T shape is model-determined. The dollar figures on the risk line are a choice, not a measurement.

Lesson: There's an optimal execution speed that minimizes total cost. Too fast = high impact. Too slow = high risk. Almgren-Chriss finds the balance.

Model #3: Kyle's Lambda (Information-Based)

Concept: Market infers information from order flow → permanent impact

Formula: Δp = λ × Q

  • λ (lambda): Market's sensitivity to order flow (price impact per share)
  • Q: Order size

Key insight: If market believes your order is informed (hedge fund buying on insider info), lambda is HIGH → price moves sharply

Defense (institutional): Split orders across time/venues to disguise intent (reduce perceived information content)

Part 3: Estimating Market Impact (Practical)

Rule of Thumb: Participation Rate

Participation rate: Your order size as % of recent volume

Impact guidelines:

  • < 1% of daily volume: Minimal impact (0.05-0.1%)
  • 1-5% of daily volume: Moderate impact (0.1-0.5%)
  • 5-10% of daily volume: High impact (0.5-2%)
  • > 10% of daily volume: Extreme impact — well above 1%, and no longer predictable. At this size the square-root law's assumption of a deep, replenishing book stops holding, and realized cost depends more on who else is in the name that week than on any formula.

These bands are realized costs from working orders across a full session. Compare them against Model #1 and they run high at the top end and low at the bottom: the square-root law with a 1.5% daily volatility puts 1% of ADV at 0.15% and 10% of ADV at 0.47%. The bands are wider because they absorb the things the formula leaves out — spread crossing, adverse selection, and the days when the book is thin. Use the formula for the estimate and the bands for the sanity check.

🎯 Invert the formula: how big does your order have to be before this matters?

Everything so far has run the square-root law forwards — here is my order, what does it cost. Run it backwards and it answers the question a reader actually has, which is whether any of this applies to them. Set the impact you are willing to pay and solve for the size:

order size  =  daily volume  ×  ( impact you accept  ÷  σ )²

The squaring is the whole story. Accepting twice the impact lets you trade four times the size, and halving the stock's volatility lets you trade four times the size at the same cost. Put dollars on both, at a tenth of a percent and a quarter of a percent:

stock                    traded a day   daily vol   order that costs 0.10%   order that costs 0.25%
NVDA                          $32.5 B          3%                  $36.1 M                 $225.7 M
TSLA                            $20 B          3%                  $22.2 M                 $138.9 M
AAPL                           $7.5 B        1.5%                  $33.3 M                 $208.3 M
AMD                            $4.6 B          3%                   $5.1 M                  $32.1 M
a $10M-a-day small cap          $10 M        2.5%                  $16,000                 $100,000
a $2M-a-day micro cap            $2 M          4%                   $1,250                   $7,812

Read the last two rows against the first four. In NVDA you can put thirty-six million dollars to work before impact reaches a tenth of a percent. In a stock that trades ten million dollars a day, the same threshold arrives at sixteen thousand — a position size that fits inside an ordinary retail account, on a name an ordinary retail trader might well own. In a two-million-a-day micro cap it arrives at twelve hundred dollars.

So the opening line of this lesson is true with a condition attached, and the condition is the stock rather than the account. Impact is not an institutional problem that retail traders are too small to have. It is a liquidity problem, and a retail trader in an illiquid name is further into it than a fund in a megacap. If you have ever wondered why your fills in a thin stock come in worse than the screen suggested, the answer is on this page and it is not your broker.

Run the two numbers you actually need before your next order in anything you would not call liquid: the stock's dollar volume on an average day, and its daily volatility. Both are on any quote page. Divide your intended position by the first, take the square root, multiply by the second, and you have your estimate in percent. If it comes out above about a tenth of a percent, you are the large trader in that name, and the whole of Part 4 is addressed to you.

⚠️ Professional Limit: Most institutions avoid exceeding 5-10% of daily volume in single stock to minimize footprint. Larger orders split across multiple days.

Volume Curve (Intraday Variation)

Observation: Volume is NOT constant throughout the day

Time (ET) % of Daily Volume Per hour Market Impact
9:30-10:00 AM ~15% ~30% Moderate (deepest liquidity of the day, but also the widest spreads and the fastest price moves)
10:00 AM-12:00 PM ~20% ~10% Low (steady institutional flow, calm prices)
12:00-2:00 PM ~15% ~7.5% High (thinnest liquidity per unit time in the session)
2:00-3:00 PM ~20% ~20% Low-Moderate (volume picking up, prices still orderly)
3:00-4:00 PM ~30% ~30% Moderate (closing auction absorbs flow, but everyone else is working orders too)

Read the third column, not the second. What sets your participation rate is volume per unit time, and by that measure the two deepest windows in the session are the first half hour and the last hour, both around 30% of the day's volume per hour. That is not the same as saying they are the cheapest windows to trade: the open carries the widest spreads and the sharpest repricing of the day, and the close is where every other institutional order is also trying to finish. You get liquidity and you pay for it in volatility and adverse selection.

Trading implication: 10:00 AM-12:00 PM and 2:00-3:00 PM are the practical windows — enough volume to absorb a working order, calm enough that you are not paying for it in price movement. 12:00-2:00 PM is the one window to avoid outright: at roughly 7.5% of the day's volume per hour it is the thinnest stretch of the session, so the same order size becomes a much larger share of what is trading.

Part 4: Execution Strategies to Minimize Impact

Strategy #1: TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price)

How it works: Split order evenly across time (e.g., 100K shares over 60 mins = 1,667 shares/min)

Pros: Simple, predictable, minimizes impact

Cons: Ignores volume patterns (trades same amount during lunch as during peak volume)

Strategy #2: VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price)

How it works: Trade in proportion to market volume (more during high-volume periods, less during low-volume)

Pros: Adapts to market conditions, minimizes impact better than TWAP

Cons: More complex, requires volume forecasting

VWAP vs TWAP Example

Goal: Buy 100K shares of AAPL from 9:30-11:30 AM (120 minutes)

TWAP approach:

  • 100K / 120 mins = 833 shares/minute (constant rate)
  • Total impact: Moderate (ignores volume patterns)

VWAP approach:

  • 9:30-10:00 AM (30% of volume): Buy 30K shares (1,000/min)
  • 10:00-11:00 AM (50% of volume): Buy 50K shares (833/min)
  • 11:00-11:30 AM (20% of volume): Buy 20K shares (667/min)
  • Total impact: Lower (trades more when liquidity is high)

Result: For large orders VWAP typically comes in 10-30% cheaper on impact than TWAP — that is 10-30% off the slippage, not off the price. On a 0.20% TWAP cost that means landing around 0.14-0.18% instead. Worth having; not the difference between a good trade and a bad one.

The 30/50/20 split above is this stock's profile for this particular morning. Real VWAP algos re-forecast the curve intraday rather than trusting a fixed template, because the day a stock reports or gets added to an index is exactly the day the template is wrong.

Strategy #3: Implementation Shortfall (IS)

Concept: Minimize difference between decision price and execution price

How it works: Trade aggressively at start (lock in decision price), then slow down to reduce impact

Use case: When you have strong conviction (don't want price to run away)

Strategy #4: Iceberg Orders

What: Display small order (e.g., 500 shares) while hiding large reserve (e.g., 50,000 shares)

Why: Prevents market from seeing full size (reduces front-running)

Risk: HFTs detect icebergs via pattern recognition (repeated small fills at same price)

Strategy #5: Dark Pool Routing (Advanced)

What are dark pools? Private exchanges where large orders trade without displaying quotes publicly

Major dark pools: UBS ATS, Goldman Sachs Sigma X2, JPMorgan JPM-X, Barclays LX, Instinet CBX. Treat any such list as perishable — Credit Suisse's CrossFinder was for years one of the largest, and it went away with the firm's 2023 absorption into UBS.

Dark Pool vs Lit Exchange Comparison

Lit Exchange (NYSE, NASDAQ, etc.):

  • Visibility: All quotes displayed publicly
  • Pros: Transparent, best execution guarantee
  • Cons: Large orders visible → HFTs front-run → higher impact

Dark Pool:

  • Visibility: Resting orders are hidden. Executions are not: off-exchange prints go to the tape through a FINRA trade reporting facility within seconds, so the market learns that size traded almost immediately — it just does not learn how much more of it is still sitting in the pool
  • Pros: Large orders can trade without tipping off market → lower impact
  • Cons: No guarantee of execution, potential for information leakage to pool operator

Example: $500M AAPL Purchase — 3.33M shares against a 50M-share ADV, so 6.7% of a day's volume. The numbers below only make sense at that scale; a $10M ticket in AAPL is 0.13% of ADV and costs single-digit basis points on any of these routes.

Method Impact Time Risk
Lit exchange (market order) 0.8-1.5% Seconds High impact, immediate fill
Lit exchange (VWAP algo) 0.2-0.4% 2-4 hours Moderate impact, good fill
Dark pool (block trade) 0.05-0.15% Variable (may not fill) Low impact IF filled, no guarantee
Hybrid (dark + lit VWAP) 0.1-0.25% 2-6 hours BEST: Low impact, high fill rate

Strategy #6: Delayed Execution (Multi-Day Strategies)

When to use: Building very large positions (>10% ADV) where single-day execution would cause excessive impact

Worked Example (constructed): Building a $50M Position Over 5 Days

Scenario: A fund wants $50M of a mid-cap at $250 a share — 200K shares. The stock's daily volume is 1.5M shares and its daily volatility is 3%. Note the pairing: this section is about orders above 10% of ADV, so the example has to be a stock where $50M actually is that. In TSLA, $50M is under two tenths of one percent of a day and none of this applies.

Single-day approach:

  • 200K / 1.5M = 13.3% of ADV
  • Expected impact (square-root): 3% × √0.133 = 1.1% → 1.1% × $250 = $2.75/share
  • Total cost: 200K × $2.75 = $550,000

5-day approach (40K shares/day):

  • Day 1: buy 40K shares — 2.7% of ADV, so 3% × √0.0267 = 0.49% = $1.23/share = $49,000
  • Days 2-5: same size, same estimate, $49,000 a day
  • Total impact: 5 × $49,000 = $245,000
  • Savings vs 1-day: $305,000 (55% reduction)
  • The 55% is not a coincidence and it is not specific to these numbers: splitting an order into n equal days cuts square-root-law impact by 1 - 1/√n. For five days that is 1 - 0.447 = 55%. For ten days it is 68%. The gains flatten fast, which is why nobody spreads a position over forty sessions.

Trade-off: Higher execution risk (price might rally during 5 days), but much lower market impact. Use when conviction is high and time horizon is long.

Part 4.5: Detecting and Avoiding HFT Predators

How HFTs Detect Large Orders

High-frequency trading firms use pattern recognition to identify institutional orders, then front-run them for profit.

HFT Detection Signals:

  1. Repeated Small Fills at Same Price: Iceberg order detected (e.g., 500 shares every 2 minutes at $150.00)
  2. VWAP Algo Signature: Order rate correlates with market volume (HFTs reverse-engineer your algo)
  3. Time-Based Patterns: TWAP = predictable (e.g., 1,000 shares every 5 minutes for 2 hours)
  4. Cross-Venue Correlation: If you're buying on NYSE and NASDAQ simultaneously, HFTs detect coordination

⚠️ HFT Front-Running Mechanics

Step 1: HFT detects your iceberg (500 share fills every 3 min @ $100.00)

Step 2: HFT buys 10,000 shares @ $100.01-$100.05 (front-runs you)

Step 3: Price rises to $100.15 due to HFT buying

Step 4: Your next fill: $100.15 (instead of $100.00)

Step 5: HFT sells 10,000 shares @ $100.15 to you. It paid an average of about $100.03 on the way in, so the round trip nets roughly $1,200 — twelve cents a share, taken out of your fill.

Keep the size of that number in mind. Twelve cents on a $100 stock is 12 basis points, and it is the reason this whole section exists — not because any single interaction is large, but because it happens on clip after clip for as long as your pattern stays readable.

Defense: Randomize timing, vary order sizes, split across multiple venues/algos, use dark pools for larger clips

Anti-Gaming Execution Tactics

Tactic #1: Randomized TWAP (Time Variation)

Standard TWAP: 1,000 shares every 5 minutes for 2 hours (predictable!)

Randomized TWAP:

  • Time interval: Randomize between 3-8 minutes (not fixed 5 min)
  • Order size: Randomize between 750-1,250 shares (not fixed 1,000)
  • Result: HFTs can't predict next order → harder to front-run
  • One practical catch: randomizing the interval around 5 minutes means you fire roughly 22 clips in two hours rather than 24, so the schedule has to true up against the target or you finish short. Every production randomizer carries a catch-up term for exactly this reason.

Example:

9:30 AM: 890 shares @ $100.00
9:34 AM: 1,120 shares @ $100.02 (4 min gap)
9:41 AM: 780 shares @ $100.01 (7 min gap)
9:47 AM: 1,050 shares @ $99.98 (6 min gap)
...
Impact comes in lower than a fixed-interval TWAP, because the pattern is no longer readable.
How much lower depends entirely on how much predatory flow was reading you in the first place,
which is not something you can measure from your own fills.
Tactic #2: Multi-Venue Fragmentation

Problem: Trading all 100K shares on NYSE → easy to detect

Solution: Split across 8-12 venues

  • NYSE: 15K shares (15%)
  • NASDAQ: 18K shares (18%)
  • Cboe BZX: 12K shares (12%)
  • Cboe EDGX: 10K shares (10%)
  • IEX: 8K shares (8%) — Speed bump protects from HFTs!
  • Dark pools (5 pools): 37K shares (37%)

Benefit: No single venue sees full size → harder to reverse-engineer intent

Risk: Complexity (need smart order router), possible adverse selection in dark pools

Tactic #3: IEX Speed Bump (Best Defense)

What is IEX? Investors Exchange with built-in 350-microsecond delay ("speed bump")

How it protects you:

  • Your order arrives at IEX with 350μs delay
  • HFT detects your order on other venues (NYSE, NASDAQ)
  • HFT tries to front-run on IEX
  • BUT: HFT's front-run order ALSO delayed 350μs
  • Result: HFT can't beat you to IEX → no front-running!

What the evidence actually shows:

  • The mechanism is real and easy to verify: the 350μs delay applies to everyone, so a latency advantage elsewhere cannot be converted into a queue advantage here.
  • The size of the benefit is much harder to pin down. Published venue-quality comparisons put the difference in fill quality somewhere between a fraction of a basis point and a few basis points, depending on order type, urgency and how the broker routed the control group. Claims of 5-15 basis points do circulate; they do not survive contact with the methodology.
  • Every study on this question is produced either by a venue with an interest in the answer or by a broker whose own routing decisions are part of what is being measured. Trust the direction of the effect more than any specific number attached to it, including this one.

Trade-off: Lower fill rates (IEX has ~2% market share), but fills are higher quality (no toxic flow)

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Question 1: You want to buy 200K shares of a stock with 10M daily volume. Using the square-root law (σ = 2%), what's the estimated impact?

Show Answer

Answer: Impact = 0.02 × √(200,000 / 10,000,000) = 0.02 × √0.02 = 0.02 × 0.141 = 0.00282 = 0.28%. For a $100 stock, that's $0.28/share impact, or $56,000 total cost on 200K shares.

Question 2: You see a 100K share buy print in TSLA (avg volume = 50M/day). What's your expectation for temporary impact?

Show Answer

Answer: Participation rate = 100K / 50M = 0.2% (very low). The square-root law with TSLA's roughly 3% daily volatility gives 0.03 × √0.002 = 0.13% — call it a tenth of a percent, and treat that as an upper bound for a print that was worked rather than swept. However, if the print is the first of many (an institution starting accumulation), permanent impact can develop as more orders arrive, and that is the part the formula does not see.

Question 3: When is market impact HIGHEST during the trading day?

Show Answer

Answer: 12:00-2:00 PM ET. Careful with how you state the reason: that window carries about 15% of the day's volume, the same share as the opening half hour. What makes it the thinnest stretch is that the 15% is spread over two hours — roughly 7.5% of the day's volume per hour, against 30% per hour at the open and at the close. Participation rate is a rate, so it is volume per unit time that decides your impact, not the share of the day a window happens to contain.

Practical Checklist

Before Executing Large Orders:

  • Check average daily volume (ADV) for stock
  • Calculate participation rate: Order size / ADV (target <5%)
  • Estimate impact using square-root law: σ × √(Q/V)
  • If participation >5%, split order across multiple days or use algo (VWAP/TWAP)
  • Avoid lunch hour (12-2 PM) for large orders (thinnest volume per hour in the session = highest participation rate)
  • Use iceberg orders to hide full size (but beware HFT detection)

While Executing:

  • Monitor temporary impact via Signal Pilot Volume Oracle (are subsequent prints moving price?)
  • If impact exceeds estimate by 2×, slow down execution (market sensing your order)
  • Check Pentarch Pilot Line for cumulative flow (are other institutions also buying?)

Post-Execution Analysis:

  • Calculate actual impact: (Avg fill price - Decision price) / Decision price
  • Compare to estimated impact (square-root model accurate?)
  • Track temporary vs permanent impact (did price revert after execution?)

Key Takeaways

  • Market impact = hidden cost of large orders (often exceeds spread 10-100×)
  • Square-root law: Impact ∝ σ × √(Q/V) (simple estimator)
  • Participation rate >5% of daily volume = high impact (split order)
  • VWAP > TWAP for minimizing impact (trades more during high-volume periods) — typically 10-30% off the slippage, not off the price
  • Avoid lunch hour (12-2 PM) for large orders (thinnest volume per hour = highest participation rate)

Market impact is invisible tax on your returns. Minimize it with limit orders, participation rates under 5%, and VWAP algorithms. Understanding impact models prevents costly execution mistakes.
