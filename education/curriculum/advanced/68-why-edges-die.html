Why Edges Die: Capacity, Crowding and Decay

Reading time ~20 min • Module 8: Building a System •

The previous lessons taught you to build an edge and to hold your nerve while it works. This one is about the cases where holding your nerve is the wrong answer — where the edge really is gone, and no amount of patience brings it back.

The tell is never in the equity curve. Lesson 55 established why: at retail sample sizes a halved edge takes years to prove. If you wait for your P&L to confirm that an edge has died, you will pay for the confirmation. The tells here are structural, and every one of them is visible in the market rather than in your account.

💸 The August 2007 Quant Quake

In the second week of August 2007, a cluster of well-funded quantitative funds — all running statistical models fitted on roughly fifteen years of data, all holding broadly the same factor exposures — lost billions of dollars in three trading days. Goldman Sachs' Global Equity Opportunities fund, one of the few whose numbers became public, fell about 30% in that week.

6-9 August 2007: Nothing about the companies in those portfolios changed. One large fund began liquidating, the liquidation pushed the shared factors against everyone holding them, the resulting losses forced the next fund to liquidate, and the loop ran for three days until it exhausted itself. Every model involved had been validated. What none of them had modelled was the behaviour of the other models.

Why it opens this lesson: every fund in that unwind had a real edge, correctly measured, and it did not save them. A backtest sees your positions. It cannot see the crowd standing in the trade beside you, and the crowd is one of the four things that kills an edge.

Three deaths and one pause

Three of the four deaths in this lesson are permanent. The fourth is not a death at all, and it accounts for most of the systems people abandon. Being able to tell them apart is worth more than any single strategy you will learn in this tier, because it decides whether you spend the next year waiting or rebuilding.

Part 1: Four Deaths, and One That Is Not a Death

An edge is a repeatable mispricing plus your ability to take it. Both halves can fail, and they fail in four distinct ways:

How it dies What actually changed Comes back?
CapacityNothing in the market. You got bigger, and your own order now moves the price you were trying to capture.Only if you shrink
CrowdingEnough others found the same trade that the mispricing is arbitraged in the good times and everyone exits through the same door in the bad ones.Rarely
Arms raceSomeone reached the opportunity before you can. The mispricing still exists; it is simply no longer available at your speed or your data.No
RegimeNothing about the edge changed. The market condition it needs is currently absent.Yes — this one is not a death

The fourth row is the one that matters most in practice, because it is by far the most common and it is the one people misdiagnose as the other three. A mean-reversion system in a trending quarter has not been arbitraged away and has not run out of capacity. It has been switched off, and it will switch back on. Lesson 53 is the full treatment; the mistake to avoid is rebuilding a system that was never broken.

Part 2: Capacity — The Death You Cause Yourself

Capacity is usually presented as a hedge fund problem. It is not. It binds wherever your order is large relative to what trades, and that ratio is about the market you chose at least as much as the money you have.

Lesson 69 established the shape of market impact: cost grows with the square root of the fraction of daily volume you take, not linearly. Written out:

impact  =  Y  x  daily volatility  x  sqrt( your size / daily volume )

Y is an empirical constant, close to 1 for equities
(published estimates land roughly between 0.5 and 1.5)

Your capacity is the size at which that impact equals your edge. Past it you are paying the market more than the mispricing is worth, and the strategy is arithmetically dead however good the signal is. Take a 20 basis point edge per trade in a name with 2% daily volatility — ordinary numbers for a short-horizon equity system:

Daily dollar volume Size that costs you a quarter of the edge Size that costs half Size that costs all of it
$5M (small cap)$3,000$13,000$50,000
$20M$13,000$50,000$200,000
$50M$31,000$125,000$500,000
$200M$125,000$500,000$2,000,000
$1B (large cap)$625,000$2,500,000$10,000,000

💡 Read the first row again

On a small cap trading five million dollars a day, a 20 basis point edge is entirely consumed at fifty thousand dollars a position, and a quarter of it is gone at three thousand. That is not an institutional number. Plenty of readers of this lesson trade above it already, in exactly those names, wondering why live results fall short of the backtest.

The uncomfortable version: if your backtest assumed fills at the mid and you are trading illiquid names, you may never have had the edge you measured. Capacity did not kill it later — it was never there at your size.

The diagnostic is the cheapest in this lesson. Take your average position size, divide by the average daily dollar volume of what you trade, and put that fraction through the formula. If the resulting impact is a meaningful share of your edge per trade, you have found your answer and you did not need a single further month of results to find it.

And note which way capacity fails. It degrades smoothly and it is reversible. Halve your size and you get most of the edge back, which makes it the friendliest of the four. It is also the only one you can fix in an afternoon.

Part 3: Crowding — The Death Your Backtest Cannot See

A backtest runs your strategy against history as it happened. It cannot show you what history would have looked like if a few billion dollars had been running the same rules, because that history did not occur. This is not a flaw in your methodology that better validation would catch. It is structural: the counterfactual does not exist in the data.

Crowding does two separate things, and it is worth keeping them apart because they arrive at different times:

  • In normal conditions it compresses the edge. More capital chasing the same mispricing closes it faster and closes it further. The signal still fires; it just pays less each year, and it pays less in a way that is statistically invisible on the timescales from Lesson 55.
  • In stressed conditions it inverts the correlation you relied on. This is the August 2007 mechanism, and it is the dangerous one. Positions held for unrelated fundamental reasons become correlated through their holders, because the thing they share is not an exposure but an owner under pressure. Your diversification was real right up to the moment it was needed.

⚠️ The proxies, and their honest limits

Crowding cannot be measured directly — nobody publishes who is in your trade. What exists are indirect readings, and each one is partial:

  • Correlation of your returns to a known factor. If your supposedly idiosyncratic system correlates 0.7 with a published momentum or value factor, you are in a crowded trade whether or not you designed one. This is the most useful of the four and takes an afternoon with free factor data.
  • Short interest and days-to-cover on the names you hold, for anything with a short leg. High days-to-cover is a crowded exit by definition.
  • Public popularity of the signal. Not rigorous, and worth something anyway. A rule that has been the subject of a bestselling book and a hundred videos is not a secret, and the era in which it was measured is not the era you will trade it in.
  • Behaviour on stress days. If your strategy has unusually bad days precisely when leveraged players are being forced out, you are standing in the crowd. Pull your ten worst days and ask what else was happening on them.

None of these is a verdict. Together they are far better than the equity curve, which will tell you nothing until the money is gone.

🎯 You cannot see the crowd, but you can count how many it can hold

The proxies above are the honest state of the art and they are all judgement calls. There is one thing about crowding that is not, and Part 2 already computed it without saying so. Capacity was presented as a limit on your own size. It is not. It is a limit on every dollar in the trade at once, because the mispricing does not care whose order eats it.

So the same line does double duty. A 20 basis point edge in a name with 2% daily volatility is exhausted at 1% of daily volume — by you, by you and one other, or by four hundred people between them. Divide that total by the size people actually trade and you get the population the trade supports:

daily volume   the trade holds in total   at $10K a head   at $100K   at $1M
         $5M                    $50,000              5.0        0.5      0.1
        $20M                   $200,000               20        2.0      0.2
        $50M                   $500,000               50        5.0      0.5
       $200M                        $2M              200         20      2.0
         $1B                       $10M            1,000        100       10

A small cap trading five million dollars a day holds five retail participants at ten thousand dollars each, and half of one at a hundred thousand. You do not need to know who else is in the trade to know that. If your position is a hundred thousand dollars in that name, the arithmetic says the edge was already gone before anyone else arrived — and if it is ten thousand, it says the fifth person to find your setup ends it for all of you.

Which reframes the whole of this section. Crowding is not a mysterious force that either has or has not found you; it is a capacity number divided by a typical position size, and the answer on illiquid names is a number small enough to count on one hand. The proxies tell you whether the crowd has arrived. This tells you how large it would have to be to matter, and on the instruments most readers trade, the answer is: not very.

It also explains the August 2007 shape without appealing to anything unobservable. Those funds were not in a trade that could hold a handful of people. They were in one that could hold several billion dollars, and several billion dollars had arrived. The bound was large, so the crowd took years to reach it — and then it was reached, and three days were enough.

Run it on your own book. Take the last column of the capacity table in Part 2, divide by your position size, and you have the number of people who can stand where you are standing. If that number is under about ten, treat crowding as a live risk rather than a hedge fund problem, and treat any public write-up of your setup as the thing that ends it.

Part 4: The Arms Race — The Death You Cannot Outrun

Some edges do not get crowded so much as taken. The mispricing is still there; it is claimed before your order can reach it. Lessons 44 and 56 cover the machinery, and the point here is narrower: this failure mode is permanent and one-directional. Nobody has ever got slower.

The practical test is whether your edge depends on being early to public information. A strategy that reacts to an economic release, an index reconstitution, or an opening imbalance is competing on latency, and that competition has been settled. A strategy that depends on holding a position through several days of a structural imbalance is not, because the advantage there is patience and balance-sheet, and neither is improved by a faster cable.

📊 The consolation, and it is real

The arms race kills fast edges and leaves slow ones alone. It is the one death on this list you can design around in advance, simply by choosing a horizon where the competition does not operate. Lesson 60 makes the same argument from the other side: the reason you cannot compete on speed is also the reason you do not have to.

Part 5: Which Death Is Yours?

Each death leaves a different fingerprint, and the fingerprints do not overlap much. Run this before you conclude anything from your results:

If you observe Suspect Check
Live results track the backtest on small trades and fall short on large ones CapacityImpact formula against your participation rate
Fine for years, then a violent stretch with no news to explain it CrowdingFactor correlation; what happened on your ten worst days
Signals still fire, but fills are consistently worse than the level that triggered them Arms raceSlippage between signal price and fill price over time
Underperformance lines up with a market condition you can name Regime — not a deathMeasure the condition directly (Lessons 49 and 64)

Two of those four checks are arithmetic on data you already have. The other two need an afternoon. All of them beat waiting for statistical significance, which Lesson 55 showed can take years to arrive.

Part 6: Where Signal Pilot Fits

Straight about the limits: no indicator knows the capacity of your strategy or who else is in your trade. Those are questions about your size and your correlations, and Parts 2 and 3 are the answer. What the tools address is the fourth row of the table — the one that is not a death.

  • Harmonic Oscillator classifies the current regime as trending or mean-reverting. That is the single fastest way to separate the recoverable case from the three permanent ones, and it is the case you are most likely to be in.
  • Volume Oracle shows the order flow your setups form in. A setup that used to form on visible absorption and now forms on nothing is a structural change in the mechanism, observable before any statistic could confirm it.
  • Janus Atlas marks the sweep structure a liquidity-based system depends on. If sweeps have stopped reversing at the rate they used to, that is the mechanism check from Part 5 done on a chart.

None of them returns a verdict on whether your edge is alive, and a tool that claimed to would be selling certainty the data does not support. What they do is make the regime question quick to answer, so you can spend your effort on the questions that are genuinely hard.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: Your strategy tracked its backtest closely for two years, then lost badly over three weeks with no company-specific news in any position. Which death does this fingerprint suggest?

A) Capacity — you must have outgrown the market you trade
B) Crowding — a long quiet period ended by a sharp correlated unwind is its signature
C) The arms race — someone got faster than you over those three weeks
D) Ordinary variance, which requires no explanation at all

Correct: B. This is the August 2007 shape: unrelated positions moving together because they share holders rather than exposures, with no news to explain it. A tells you nothing on its own — capacity degrades smoothly with size, it does not arrive as a three-week event. C is wrong on timescale; latency advantages erode edges gradually and show up as worsening fills, not as a sudden loss. D deserves care: three bad weeks genuinely can be variance, and Lesson 55 is about not over-reading them. What makes crowding the better read here is the correlated part — positions that should be independent moving as one is a structural observation, not a statistical one.

Q2: You trade a 20 basis point edge in names with 2% daily volatility and about $5M of daily dollar volume. At roughly what position size has your own impact consumed the entire edge?

A) Around $5 million — capacity only binds at institutional scale
B) Around $500,000
C) Around $50,000
D) It cannot be estimated without live execution data

Correct: C. Impact equals the edge when Y x 0.02 x sqrt(participation) = 0.0020, which puts participation at 1% of daily volume — and 1% of $5M is $50,000. The number is the point of the section: capacity is routinely described as a fund problem, and on illiquid names it binds at a size an individual can reach. D is the answer most people give and it is wrong in a costly way; the square-root law gives a usable estimate from three inputs you already know, and an estimate available today beats live data you would have to lose money to collect.

Q3: A mean-reversion system has underperformed for five months, and the market has been strongly trending throughout. What follows?

A) The edge has been arbitraged away; rebuild on a different premise
B) The condition the edge needs is absent, which is the one case on the list that reverses
C) Capacity has been reached and size should be cut permanently
D) Five months is conclusive evidence the system is dead

Correct: B. Regime is the fourth row and it is not a death. The mechanism is intact and currently unavailable, which is a completely different situation from a mispricing that has been competed away. D is worth naming explicitly: five months at a typical trading frequency is nowhere near the 143 trades Lesson 55 showed are needed to establish even a total collapse, so the duration is not evidence of anything by itself. The reason to reach a conclusion here is structural — the condition is measurably absent — and that conclusion is that you wait, at reduced size if you wrote that rule down in advance.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Four ways an edge dies, and only three are deaths. Capacity, crowding and the arms race are structural and largely permanent. Regime is not a death, is the most common diagnosis, and reverses on its own.
  • Capacity is computable from three numbers you already have. Impact grows with the square root of your participation rate; the size where impact equals your edge is your ceiling. On a $5M-a-day name a 20 basis point edge is gone by $50,000 a position.
  • A backtest cannot see crowding, and no amount of validation fixes that — the history in which everyone else ran your rules never happened. Factor correlation and the composition of your worst days are the practical substitutes, and the capacity number bounds the problem from the other side: a trade that holds $50,000 in total cannot be crowded by more than five people at $10,000 each.
  • Crowding compresses your edge quietly and inverts your correlations violently. The August 2007 unwind was not a modelling failure; every model was validated. What none of them modelled was each other.
  • The arms race only threatens edges that depend on being early to public information. Choosing a slower horizon is a legitimate and permanent defence, not a consolation prize.
  • Diagnose structurally, not statistically. Lesson 55 showed the equity curve takes years to answer this. Every check in Part 5 is faster than that, and two of them are arithmetic on records you already keep.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Your Own Capacity and Crowding Audit

Objective: Put a number on two of the four deaths for a system you actually run. Both parts use data you already have or can download free.

Part 1: Your capacity ceiling. For the five instruments you trade most, record the average daily dollar volume and the average daily volatility. Take your typical position size in each and compute the participation rate, then the impact from the square-root formula in Part 2. Express the result as a percentage of your edge per trade. Any instrument where impact exceeds a quarter of your edge belongs on a watch list; anywhere it exceeds half, you have found a real problem and the fix is to trade smaller or trade elsewhere.

Part 2: Your worst ten days. Pull the ten worst days in your trading record. For each one write down what the broad market did, what volatility did, and whether leveraged participants were under pressure. If the ten days have nothing in common, that is a genuinely good result and worth knowing. If they cluster on stress days, your positions are correlated through their holders and Part 3 applies to you.

Part 3: Name your dependency. In one sentence, what does your edge require the market to be doing? Then write the measurement that tells you whether it is doing it, and run that measurement once a month. This is the same instruction as the exercise in Lesson 55, deliberately, because it is the single check that answers the most questions for the least work.

Part 4: Rank your exposure. Score your system one to five on each of the four deaths. The highest score is where your attention belongs, and it is almost never the one you were worrying about when you started this exercise.

You now have both halves of the question the last two lessons were built around: whether your results are outside your own distribution, and whether the thing you depend on still exists. The next lesson turns to a market that is structurally different in almost every respect — one that never closes, has no single venue, and where the participants are visible in ways equities never allow.
