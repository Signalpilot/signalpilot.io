They're Faster Than You (And That's Not Even Their Edge)

Reading time ~13 min • Module 7: The Other Side •
Signal Pilot
Professional Trading Education
0%
You're making progress!

High-frequency and market-making firms are on one side of about half of all US equity volume.

They execute trades in microseconds. You blink, they've completed 10,000 transactions.

You can't beat them on speed. So stop trying.

One correction before any of this

Nobody sees your order before it fills. An order in transit is not public. What is public is the print it makes when part of it executes, and how fast somebody else can act on that print before the rest of your order arrives — which is latency arbitrage, not front-running. Front-running means trading ahead of a customer order you were entrusted with, and that is a broker’s offence rather than a stranger’s.

The distinction matters because it names the defence: not speed, which you cannot have, but not announcing yourself, which is free.

Part 1: The Market Maker Business Model

How They Make $12.5M/Year (And You Fund It)

Let's talk about the business you're unknowingly funding every time you trade.

Market makers make money three ways. Most traders only know about one.

Revenue Stream #1: The Spread (The Obvious One)

The setup:

Bid: $100.00

Ask: $100.05

Spread: $0.05

Market maker:

  • Buys at $100.00 (bid)
  • Sells at $100.05 (ask)
  • Profit: $0.05/share

The math: On 1M shares per day = $50,000/day = $12.5M/year

And that's just ONE revenue stream.

Revenue Stream #2: Exchange Rebates (The Hidden One)

Here's what they don't tell you: Exchanges PAY market makers to provide liquidity.

The Maker-Taker Model:

  • Maker (limit order): Receives rebate = $0.0015/share
  • Taker (market order): Pays fee = $0.0030/share

Who's the taker? You. Every market order you place.

The math: On 1M shares per day = $1,500/day = $375,000/year

That's on TOP of the spread. They're getting paid twice.

💡 The Aha Moment

Every time you use a market order, you're paying the exchange fee AND giving the market maker a rebate. You're literally paying them twice to take the other side of your trade.

Revenue Stream #3: Information Advantage (The Scary One)

Market makers see order flow in real-time.

They know what's coming before it hits the tape.

Example:

  • Large buy orders incoming → Widen ask, reduce inventory, let price run
  • Large sell orders incoming → Widen bid, short inventory, profit on drop

They front-run the market. Legally. (It's called "latency advantage," not "front-running.")

Your market order? They saw it 10 microseconds before it filled. They adjusted the price. You got the worst fill.

Part 2: HFT Strategies (Why You Keep Getting Burned)

The Tactics That Make You Exit Liquidity

Alright, let's talk about the dirty tricks. And yes, most of them are legal.

Strategy: Quote Stuffing

What it is: Flooding the order book with thousands of fake orders to confuse competitors and slow down their systems.

How it works:

11:00:00.001 → Post 10,000 orders

11:00:00.050 → Cancel 9,800 orders

11:00:00.100 → Post 10,000 NEW orders

Result: Competitor systems overloaded. HFT gains millisecond advantage.

Defense: Use limit orders. Trade during high liquidity hours. Don't react to noise.

Strategy: Latency Arbitrage

What it is: Exploiting speed differences between exchanges to capture spreads that are very short-lived, and never actually risk-free.

Example:

  • Price on Exchange A: $100.00
  • Price on Exchange B: $100.02
  • HFT: Buy A at $100.00, sell B at $100.02 instantly
  • Profit: $0.02/share — assuming both legs fill. If only one does, the HFT is left holding the position

Impact on you: Your market order on Exchange A fills at $100.01 (HFT bought first and pushed the price).

Defense: Use IEX (speed bump exchange) or limit orders only.

Strategy: Order Anticipation

What it is: Detecting large institutional orders and front-running them.

Pattern detection:

HFT detects:

  • 5,000 shares bought @ $100.00
  • 3,000 shares bought @ $100.01
  • 4,000 shares bought @ $100.02

Pattern identified: Large institutional buyer scaling in.

HFT action: Buy ahead, sell back at higher price.

Defense: Randomize timing. Use iceberg orders. Scale over days, not minutes.

Part 3: Why Your Stops Get Hunted

It's Not Personal. It's Profitable.

Stops cluster under obvious levels because everybody is reading the same chart, and a buyer with size will trade into that pocket because it is where the resting orders are. Nothing is targeting you; you are simply standing where the orders are.

Here's the setup:

You: Long AAPL at $150.00

Support: $149.55

Your stop: $149.50 (just below support)

Market maker sees:

• Cluster of stops at $149.50

• A pool of forced sell orders — not chosen, just triggered

• Cost to trigger: $10-50K in selling pressure

• Profit potential: Buy $10M+ at better price, ride reversal back up

Conclusion: It's profitable. So they do it.

Anatomy of a Stop Hunt

Let me show you exactly how this plays out:

12:00 PM: BTC at $45,200

12:05 PM: Support identified at $45,000. Stops clustered at $44,950.

12:15 PM: Price tests $45,050

12:16 PM: Aggressive selling → $44,980

12:17 PM: Stops triggered at $44,950. Volume spike.

12:18 PM: Price knifes to $44,900 (maximum carnage)

12:19 PM: REVERSES to $45,300

Retail trader: Stopped out at $44,950, fuming on Twitter

Market maker: Bought $10M at $44,950, sold at $45,200, profit banked

🎯 How to Trade This

Don't be the victim. Be the hunter.

Use Janus Atlas to detect these sweeps. When you see support broken briefly then reclaimed with volume:

  1. Wait for sweep confirmation
  2. Consider long on reclaim above support
  3. Stop area below swept low
  4. Target previous high

You just traded WITH the market makers instead of being their exit liquidity.

A Stop Hunt Minute by Minute (illustrative reconstruction)

Here is the same anatomy written out at the resolution you would actually watch it. The timestamps and prices below are a reconstruction of the pattern rather than a recording of one particular session — no tape is being quoted, and you should treat any minute-by-minute account of a sweep, here or anywhere else, as a diagram rather than as evidence. What is worth your attention is the order the pieces arrive in, because that order is what you can look for tomorrow:

Setup: a large-cap name trading around $242, three days into a rally

Pre-Market Context (9:15 AM ET):

  • TSLA had rallied from $238 → $242 over 3 days
  • Strong support established at $240.00 (round number, previous resistance turned support)
  • Market makers could see massive stop clusters at $239.50-$239.80 (just below the obvious $240 level)
  • Stop liquidity below the level: unknowable from outside, which is precisely why it is worth so much to whoever can see it

The Hunt Begins (10:30-10:45 AM ET):

  • 10:30 AM: TSLA testing $240.20, light volume, consolidation
  • 10:38 AM: Bid-ask spread widens from $0.02 to $0.08 (market maker warning signal)
  • 10:40 AM: Coordinated selling pressure appears—price drops to $240.00 on 180K shares (5× average 1-min volume)
  • 10:42 AM: BREAKAGE: Price knifes to $239.75 on another 220K shares
  • 10:43 AM: CASCADE: Stops trigger at $239.50-$239.80, volume spikes to 450K shares in 60 seconds
  • 10:44 AM: Low print: $239.25 (maximum pain achieved, all stops cleared)

The Reversal (10:45-11:15 AM ET):

  • 10:45 AM: Immediate buying appears at $239.25-$239.50 (market makers accumulating at better prices)
  • 10:50 AM: TSLA back above $240.00 on 380K buy volume
  • 11:00 AM: TSLA at $241.50 (above pre-hunt levels)
  • 11:15 AM: TSLA at $242.80, rally continues into close at $244.50

The Math:

  • Retail traders stopped out: ~$15-20M in forced selling at $239.50-$239.80 (average: $239.65)
  • Market makers accumulated: Bought at $239.25-$239.65, sold at $241.50-$244.50
  • Why anyone would bother: the buyer at $239.25-$239.65 is buying from people who are not choosing to sell. That is the entire economic point of the exercise — a pool of orders that must execute, at a price the seller does not get to pick. No profit figure is put on it here, because doing so would require knowing how many shares changed hands and at what average price, and neither is visible from outside.
  • Retail losses: Most stopped at $239.65, watched TSLA rally $5 without them—missed +2% move PLUS took -1% loss = -3% total opportunity cost

What the trade would look like: the sweep at $239.25 and the reclaim above $240.00 on volume are the two events; the entry is $240.20 with the stop at $238.80, below the swept low. Note what is being risked and where: $1.40 per share, at a level that has just proven it can be broken. No outcome is quoted, because the setup is a description of where to stand, not a promise about what happens next.

The pattern is common enough to be worth learning, and it is not a rule: plenty of breaks below support are simply breaks below support, and a level that is swept and reclaimed can be swept again an hour later. Janus Atlas marks the sweeps; what it cannot tell you is which ones will reverse. That is why the entry waits for the reclaim and why the stop sits below the low.

Carlos' $74,000 Stop Hunt Massacre: When HFTs Target Your Exact Stops

Carlos Martinez (composite example) — 5-year scalper with $155,000 account.

January 2024: Carlos traded SPY/QQQ breakouts with stops 5-10 ticks below support. "Tight stops, quick entries. Standard scalping."

By April 2024: 47 stop hunts in 16 weeks. Total loss: -$74,000 (-47.7% of account).

🚨 What Carlos Learned The Hard Way

"47 times in 16 weeks, price hit my EXACT stop within 1-3 ticks of support, then immediately reversed. This isn't bad luck—HFTs see clustered stops below support, sweep them, trigger cascades, then reverse. It's automated."

— Carlos Martinez, composite example

📉 Carlos' 16-Week Disaster: Jan-Apr 2024

Stop Hunts 47
Slippage Lost -$5,800
Total Loss -$74,000
The Pattern: Tight stops 5-10 ticks below obvious support + market orders during 9:30-9:45 AM (peak HFT hours) + $0.08/share avg slippage = predictable target for HFT algos.

The Stop Hunt Examples

Jan 12, 9:37 AM: SPY support $475.50. Carlos long $475.75, stop $475.40. Swept to $475.38 (2¢ below stop). Reversed to $476.40. Loss: -$700.

Feb 14, 9:42 AM: SPY support $510. Carlos long $510.20, stop $509.90. Swept to $509.88. Rallied to $511.50. Loss: -$600.

Mar 21, 9:38 AM: SPY support $520. Carlos long $520.15, stop $520.05. Swept to $520.03 (TWO TICKS below). Rallied $1.50. Loss: -$240.

These three are from the early weeks, when he was still trading 2,000 shares. As the losses mounted he sized up to make them back, so the later hunts cost far more — the 47 of them averaged about $1,450 each.

Two of the three multiply out cleanly at that size: Jan 12 risked $0.35 a share and cost $700; Feb 14 risked $0.30 and cost $600. Mar 21 is where the slippage line shows up. His stop was at $520.05 but the fill came at $520.03, the sweep low, so he paid $0.12 a share instead of $0.10, and 2,000 x $0.12 = $240. That two-cent difference, repeated across four months of market orders in the first fifteen minutes, is the $5,800.

📈 What a wider stop would have cost him

Take Jan 12 and move the stop off the obvious level. SPY’s five-minute ATR that morning was about $0.40, so a 1.5 x ATR stop sits at $475.15 instead of $475.40.

stop 10c under support    $475.40   risk $0.35   sweep low $475.38  -> stopped
1.5 x ATR from entry      $475.15   risk $0.60   sweep low $475.38  -> 23c of room

same $700 of risk:        $700 / $0.35  =  2,000 shares
                          $700 / $0.60  =  1,167 shares

the reversal he missed:   2,000 x $0.65  =  $1,300
                          1,167 x $0.65  =    $759

Hold the dollar risk constant and the wider stop is not more expensive. It is smaller. The loss is $700 either way; what the extra 25 cents of room buys is a position 58% of the size, and therefore 58% of the upside — the $0.65 reversal pays $759 rather than $1,300, a haircut of 42%.

That is the exchange rate, and it is the whole decision. You are not buying safety, because your worst case per trade has not moved. You are giving up 42% of the move in return for not being the most obvious resting order on the screen. Whether that is a good trade depends on how often your stop is the thing being swept, which is a number you have to count rather than assume.

And count it carefully, because Carlos’s 47 is not that number. He logged the sweeps that reversed, because those are the ones that feel like theft. The mornings when price took his stop and simply kept falling went into the journal as ordinary losses. So 47 counts how often he was hunted and the level then held, not how often he was hunted. Before you widen your own stops, count both columns.

📊 What the twelve weeks came to

Stop Hunt Losses -$68,200
Slippage Bleed -$5,800
Account $155K → $81K
The Reality: HFT systems react in microseconds, from servers co-located in the same building as the exchange's matching engine. They detect clustered retail stops below obvious support, sweep those levels, trigger cascades, then reverse. You can't beat speed. You must trade AROUND it.

The Rebuild: May-December 2024

May 2024: Carlos stopped fighting HFTs and started trading around them.

New Framework:

  1. Limit orders only — a limit order cannot fill worse than its limit, so on the fills you get, the slippage line goes to roughly nothing. The cost moves to the fills you don't get.
  2. Avoid 9:30-9:45 AM — Peak HFT. Trade 10 AM-2 PM instead
  3. ATR-based stops — 1.5× ATR from entry, NOT fixed ticks below levels
  4. Smaller size, wider stops — Reduced size 40%, increased stop distance 2×
  5. Trade WITH stop hunts — Wait for sweep + reclaim, then enter long

💡 Carlos' Lesson

You CANNOT beat HFT on speed. But you can stop being their exit liquidity.

  • Avoid 9:30-9:45 AM (peak HFT hunting hours)
  • Use limit orders and ATR-based stops (less predictable)
  • Trade WITH stop hunts (wait for sweep + reclaim, then enter)

Whether a given sweep crosses the line into manipulation is a question for a regulator with the order records, not for a chart. What matters to you is simpler: none of it is aimed at you personally, all of it is aimed at where predictable stops sit, and both facts point at the same fix. Don't fight speed. Trade around it.

Part 4: How to Defend Yourself

You Can't Outrun Them. Out-Think Them.

Here's the honest truth: You're not going to beat HFT on speed. Accept it.

But you CAN trade on timeframes and with strategies where speed doesn't matter.

❌ How Retail Gets Wrecked

  • Use market orders, so the fill is whatever the book happens to be
  • Place stops just below support (obvious clusters)
  • Trade first 15 minutes (highest HFT activity)
  • Trade illiquid hours (easy to manipulate)
  • Scalp with tight stops (spread > edge)

Result: Death by a thousand cuts. Account slowly bleeds.

✓ How Professionals Survive

  • Use limit orders (better fills or no fill)
  • Place stops with ATR buffer (avoid clusters)
  • Wait until 9:45 AM (volatility settles)
  • Trade 9:45 AM - 3:45 PM ET (liquid hours)
  • Trade higher timeframes (speed irrelevant)

Result: Better fills. Fewer stop-outs. Edge preserved.

The Complete Defense Playbook

Defense #1: Use Limit Orders (Always)

Why: a market order accepts whatever price clears it, and on size that price is set by the depth of the book. A limit order names the price and accepts that it may not fill. You are choosing which of the two risks to take rather than avoiding one.

Example:

  • Market order: You want to buy at $100.00. Get filled at $100.05 (slippage).
  • Limit order: You bid $100.02. Either fill at $100.02 or don't trade.

Result: Better fills. Or no trade (which is also fine—missed trade ≠ loss).

Defense #2: Avoid Obvious Stop Placement

Bad stop placement: Support at $100.00, stop at $99.95

Why it's bad: Everyone does this. Stops cluster. They get hunted.

Better stop placement: Support at $100.00, stop at $99.50

Why it's better: ATR buffer. Below obvious cluster. Based on structure, not round numbers.

Yes, wider stop = smaller position size. That's the trade-off. But you won't get swept as often.

Defense #3: Trade High Liquidity Hours

HFT works best in thin markets. When liquidity is low, they can move price with less capital.

Avoid:

  • First 15 minutes (9:30-9:45 AM ET)
  • Last 15 minutes (3:45-4:00 PM ET)
  • Overnight sessions (Asia/Europe for US stocks)

Trade: 9:45 AM - 3:45 PM ET (liquid hours, tight spreads, harder to manipulate)

Defense #4: Use IEX (Speed Bump Exchange)

What is IEX? An exchange with a 350-microsecond speed bump on ALL orders.

Why it helps: Neutralizes HFT latency advantage. Everyone gets the same speed.

Trade-off: Lower volume (less popular exchange). But fairer fills.

When to use: Large orders on liquid stocks.

Part 5: Trading WITH Market Makers

If You Can't Beat Them, Join Them

Here's the mindset shift: Stop trying to beat market makers. Start trading WITH them.

How? By reading their signals.

Signal #1: Spread Widening

When market makers widen spreads without news → They sense uncertainty. Expect a move.

Common approach: Tighten stops. Wait for confirmation. Avoid entries until direction clear.

Signal #2: Quote Pulling

Large bid wall disappears before price tests it → Fake wall. Price likely breaks down.

Common approach: If long, exit. If watching, short on breakdown confirmation.

Signal #3: Absorption Without Movement

Large volume trades, but price doesn't move → Market maker absorbing via iceberg. Strong level.

Common approach: This is REAL support/resistance. Trade the breakout or bounce.

Key Takeaways

  • Market makers profit three ways — spread, rebates, information advantage
  • HFT exploits speed — quote stuffing, latency arbitrage, order anticipation
  • Stop hunts are profitable — so they happen constantly
  • Use limit orders — better fills or no trade (both acceptable)
  • Trade liquid hours — 9:45 AM - 3:45 PM ET for best conditions
  • A wider stop is not a bigger risk, it is a smaller position — at the same $700, moving Carlos's stop from $0.35 to $0.60 a share takes him from 2,000 shares to 1,167 and cuts his share of the $0.65 reversal from $1,300 to $759. That 42% haircut is the price of not resting where everyone else rests
  • Trade WITH market makers — read their signals, don't fight them
Practice Exercise

🎯 Real-World Practice: Tracking HFT Behavior Around Key Levels

Objective: Learn to identify stop hunts and HFT activity at major support/resistance levels

Step-by-step exercise:

  1. Identify obvious levels — Mark 2-3 clear support/resistance levels on your chart (round numbers, previous day high/low, major pivot points)
  2. Place hypothetical stop clusters:
    • Where would most traders place stops? (typically 5-10 ticks below support)
    • Mark these "cluster zones" on your chart
  3. Monitor for 3 trading sessions: Watch what happens when price approaches these levels
  4. Log the pattern each time:
    • Did price knife through to stop cluster then reverse? (stop hunt)
    • Did spread widen before the move? (market maker signal)
    • How long did price stay below the level? (< 5 min = likely hunt)
    • Did volume spike at the sweep low? (stop triggering)
  5. Track effectiveness: If you had entered LONG after the sweep + reclaim, would it have been profitable?

Success metrics:

  • Document 5+ stop hunt patterns
  • Find 3+ instances where entering AFTER the hunt would have been profitable
  • Identify 2+ failed hunts (price continued through = real breakdown)

Pro insight: Stop hunts happen during illiquid hours (first/last 15 min, overnight) and before major economic data. Use Janus Atlas to mark sweeps automatically and combine with spread widening for highest-probability setups.

Test Your Knowledge

🎮 Knowledge Check (No Pressure)

Why do market makers widen spreads before major moves?

A) To make more money from retail
B) It's random—spreads always fluctuate
C) They sense uncertainty/incoming volatility and protect themselves from risk
D) They're manipulating the market
Correct! Market makers widen spreads when they detect uncertainty or incoming volatility. They're protecting themselves from taking on too much risk. This widening often happens BEFORE major moves, making it a useful leading indicator.

Your stop is at $99.50, just below support at $99.55. What's the problem?

A) Nothing—this is textbook stop placement
B) You should never use stops at all
C) The stop is too tight
D) It's an obvious cluster—everyone places stops there, making it a target for stop hunts
Exactly! Placing stops just below obvious support levels creates predictable clusters. Market makers can see these clusters and profit by triggering them. Better to use ATR-based stops below obvious levels, even if it means a wider stop and smaller position size.

You're watching ES futures at a key resistance level (5200.00). Suddenly, visible liquidity on the order book disappears—bid/ask sizes drop from 500 contracts to 50. What does this signal?

A) Adverse selection—market makers see large institutional order coming, price likely to break through 5200
B) Market makers are taking a break—wait for liquidity to return
C) Technical glitch—refresh your platform
D) Bullish signal—market makers clearing the way for a big buyer
Correct! When market makers pull quotes at a key level, it's adverse selection—they're protecting themselves from getting run over by large institutional flow. They have information you don't (order flow data, dark pool activity, internal fills). If they're pulling liquidity at resistance, it means they expect price to break THROUGH that level, not bounce. This is the mirror image of Carlos' -$74K disaster: he kept placing predictable stops where MMs were hunting. Lesson: When liquidity disappears at a level, trade the breakout, not the reversal.

You cannot be fast, and nothing in this lesson asks you to be. What it asks is that you stop resting where everyone else rests: at the same $700 of risk, moving Carlos’s stop from $0.35 to $0.60 a share takes him from 2,000 shares to 1,167, and that 42% haircut is the entire price of not being in the pocket.
