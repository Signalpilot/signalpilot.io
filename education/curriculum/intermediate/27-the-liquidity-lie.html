Part 1: The Reality Check
📖 Deep dive: Support and Resistance Explained — The Foundation of Every Trade →

You've been taught that support and resistance are "safe zones" where price bounces. Buy support, sell resistance, place your stops just beyond the level. It's trading 101, right?

Here's the reality: those obvious levels are not safe zones. They are the one place on the chart where a large number of sell orders can be relied upon to appear, and that makes them worth running through rather than defending. It is called a liquidity sweep, and the next two sections are why it happens — not as a conspiracy, but as the ordinary solution to an ordinary problem.

Why a stop cluster is worth running through

Start with what a stop-loss actually is, because the whole lesson rests on it. A stop is not a safety net sitting outside the market. It is an instruction held at your broker that does nothing at all until price touches your level — and then becomes a market sell order, filled at whatever the next available price is.

So a hundred traders with stops just below the same support are not a hundred people protected. They are a hundred sell orders that do not exist yet and will all exist at once if price gets there. The cluster is supply, held in reserve, at a price everybody can see.

Now take the other side of the screen. Suppose you need to buy ten thousand shares and the book shows eight hundred offered. Buying what is there lifts the price, so you buy the rest higher, and the more you buy the worse your average gets. You have moved the market against your own unfilled order. This is the basic problem of trading in size, and it has nothing to do with anyone's view on direction — a seller with ten thousand to unload has the identical problem in reverse.

The stop pocket solves it. Take price down through the obvious level and those hundred stops convert into supply, all at once, below where you started. You get filled in size without chasing your own order up the book. Nobody needs to know where any individual stop sits, and nobody is hunting you personally: the cluster is on the chart, in plain view, because everyone drew the same line from the same textbook.

Which is why the reclaim is the signal

A reclaim is price closing back above a level it just wicked below, or back below one it just wicked above. Mechanically that is all it is. What makes it worth waiting for is what it tells you about the supply.

If price runs through the level, takes the stops, and then closes back above it, the sell orders that were sitting under there have been consumed. There is nothing left to push against, which is exactly why price could come back. The reclaim is not a prediction. It is evidence that the supply which caused the move down has already been used up.

A sweep with no reclaim says the opposite. Price went through and stayed through, because the selling did not stop when the stops were taken — there was real supply behind them. That is not a sweep, it is a level failing, and there is no trade in it. This single distinction is what separates the method in this lesson from buying every dip below a support line.

Marcus's $8,200 Wake-Up Call: When "Perfect" Support Levels Failed 47 Times

Marcus Chen (composite example) — Software engineer turned trader with $45,000 saved for trading capital.

January 2024: Marcus was confident after 6 months of paper trading. His strategy? Textbook support/resistance trading on the 15-minute chart. Buy support, sell resistance, stops just beyond the levels.

By February 28th: 47 losing trades in 8 weeks. Win rate: 34%. Total loss: $8,200 (18.2% of capital).

The Breaking Point: February 14th, 2024

February 14, 9:25 AM: BTC at "perfect support" around $50,000. Marcus enters long at $50,080 with his stop at $49,785, half a coin.

9:43 AM: Price sweeps to $49,785 and stops him out. The stop sat $295 below his entry, so on half a coin that is $147.50.

9:46 AM: Price reclaims $50,120 and runs to $52,040. Entering on that reclaim instead pays $1,920 a coin, or $960 on the same half.

💡 Marcus's Realization

After reviewing all 47 losing trades, the pattern was undeniable:

  • 31 out of 47 trades (66%) — Directionally correct but stopped out, then price reversed in his favor
  • 16 out of 47 trades (34%) — Genuinely wrong directional calls
  • Average sweep depth: 0.6% below support (his stops were 0.5% below)
  • Average rally after sweep: 2.8% above entry level

The move he had called did arrive. He was simply not in it — that gap is entry timing, not direction, and nothing about his read had to change.

📈 What those three measurements come to

Three of the figures above are measured rather than assumed, and adding them answers both questions the story has raised: why it kept happening, and what it cost each time.

sweep clearance    =  average sweep depth  -  your stop distance
                   =  0.6%  -  0.5%   =  0.1%

entry-timing cost  =  your stop distance  +  average rally after the reclaim
                   =  0.5%  +  2.8%   =  3.3% of position value, per trade

The first line is why it was 31 trades and not three. His stop sat a tenth of a percent inside the average sweep, so an ordinary sweep reached it nearly every time. Nothing was hunting Marcus personally; his stop was simply sitting where sweeps routinely go.

The second line is what the timing was worth. The same call entered before the sweep returns −0.5%. Entered after the reclaim it returns +2.8%. The entry-timing decision is therefore worth 3.3% of position value on every one of those trades, and it is a swing from loss to gain rather than a smaller gain.

It is also where the 1.5-2% stop in the takeaways comes from, and it is worth being exact about what that is measured from. A stop 1.5% below the level sits 0.9% clear of a 0.6% average sweep, so an ordinary sweep stops reaching it — that is the figure for a position you are holding through a sweep. Once you are trading the method in Part 2 you enter after the reclaim instead, and the stop goes below the swept low, which is already past the sweep. Both rules say the same thing: put the stop outside where the sweep goes, not outside the level. A tighter stop is not less risk; it is a stop sitting further inside the zone sweeps reach. Run those two lines on your own instrument and you will have your own version of both numbers.

What He Changed

Marcus stopped trading for four weeks, then went back live having changed the entry rule and nothing else. Part 2 is that rule.

Part 2: The Rule, and the Tool

He changed one thing: when he entered.

Enter after the level has been run through and reclaimed, not at it. That is the whole substitution, and the rest of this lesson is how to see it happening.

What the wait actually looks like

Knowing to wait for a reclaim and knowing what you are waiting for are different things, and the gap between them is where the difficulty sits. Three points decide it, and none of them need a tool.

You are waiting on a close, not a wick. Price poking back above the level mid-bar is not a reclaim; it is price moving. What tells you the supply under the level has been consumed is a bar that finishes back above it, because that means the buying held for a whole period rather than for a moment. Which period is your choice, and it has to be the timeframe you are already trading, decided before the sweep rather than after — otherwise you will keep looking until you find a timeframe that says what you want.

A reclaim that fails is information, not a second chance. If price closes back above the level and then closes below it again, the reading was wrong: there was more supply than the sweep took out. That is the same situation as a sweep with no reclaim at all and it deserves the same response. The temptation is to treat the second break as a better version of the first setup. It is the opposite — it is the market saying the first reading was premature.

The swept low is the invalidation, and it is handed to you. This is the quiet advantage of the method. You are not picking a stop distance out of a percentage or an average: the sweep has just marked the exact price at which your reading is wrong. If price returns below the low the sweep made, the supply was never consumed and nothing is left of the reason for being in the trade.

Part 3: Sweep Patterns

Common Sweep Patterns

1. Obvious Support/Resistance Sweep

What: Price breaks clearly visible horizontal level

Why: Everyone sees it → Clustered stops → High liquidity

Example: the previous day’s low, the previous week’s high, or a round number like $100 or $50,000 — round numbers cluster hardest, because finding one needs no chart at all

2. Equal Lows/Highs Sweep

What: Multiple swing lows/highs at same price

Why: Retail sees "triple bottom = strong support" → More stops below

Example: Three swing lows at $44,500 = Massive stop cluster at $44,400

3. Trendline Sweep

What: Price breaks ascending/descending trendline

Why: Trendline traders place stops just beyond the line

Example: Uptrend support line → Stops clustered below it

Part 4: Common Mistakes

How To NOT Get Swept

Mistake #1: Ignoring Volume

Bad: "Price touched support and bounced, going long!"

Why it fails: Without volume spike, it's not a sweep (just a test)

Fix: Look for volume at least 50% higher than recent bars. Janus Atlas highlights this automatically with bright green bars.

Mistake #2: Trading Every Sweep

Bad: "Sweep detected, entering immediately!"

Why it fails: Not all sweeps reverse (some break through)

Fix: Require higher timeframe alignment and reversal confirmation

Practice Exercise

🎯 30-Day Sweep Recognition Challenge

Exercise: Build Pattern Recognition Through Systematic Tracking

Week one, three levels. Tonight, mark three obvious levels on the instruments you already watch — a support, a resistance, a round number. Tomorrow, watch what price does at each: does it wick 0.3-0.8% through before turning, and does it reclaim? Screenshot every sweep, and record four things each time — level swept, how deep in per cent, did it reclaim, how fast. Five days of that and you will recognise the shape without being told.

Then the thirty days. The same four fields, five levels a morning instead of three, and two things a week of data cannot give you:

  1. Pattern analysis: After 30 days, calculate your "sweep-to-reversal" success rate across different instruments and timeframes
  2. Entry refinement: Identify which sweep characteristics (depth, volume, speed of reclaim) came before the reclaims that held, on your instrument

Goal: After tracking 100-150 sweeps over 30 days, you'll have your own record of how often a sweep was followed by a reclaim on the instruments you actually trade, instead of an impression.

Then set your record beside Marcus’s: 66% of his losses were trades that bounced after stopping him out. If yours does not look like that, you have found something about your own instruments rather than failed the exercise.

Test Your Understanding

🎮 Quick Check (No Pressure)

Q: Why does price so often run through an obvious support level before turning?

A) Because retail stops cluster below them, so a run through the level meets a pocket of resting sell orders
B) Because a level that has held three times is more likely to hold a fourth, so it is the safest place to buy
C) Because support levels are where the most volume has traded, which is what makes them significant
D) Because the algorithms that set institutional prices are trained on the same chart patterns retail uses
Correct! Retail traders place stops below obvious support, and they place them in much the same place as each other because they are all reading the same chart. That clustering is what makes the level worth running through: a buyer with size meets a pocket of sell orders there and can fill into it without paying up.

Q: When should you enter a liquidity sweep trade?

A) Immediately when price touches support
B) After price sweeps below support AND reclaims with volume confirmation
C) Before the sweep happens to get a better entry
D) Only on round numbers like $100 or $1000
Correct! The key is patience—wait for the sweep to happen, then enter when price reclaims the level with strong volume. The reclaim is what tells you the run through the level is finished and the level still holds; without it, the level simply failed.

Q: What was Marcus's key mistake in his losing trades?

A) He sized too large for the stop he was using, so a normal adverse move became a large dollar loss
B) He was trading the right level in the wrong session, when the resting size was thinnest
C) He entered BEFORE the sweep instead of after, providing the liquidity
D) He held through the sweep instead of cutting, turning a small loss into a large one
Correct! Marcus was right about direction 66% of the time, but his timing was off. He entered at support and got stopped out before the bounce — his stop was the resting order a buyer with size filled into.

Q: Your stop sits 0.5% below support. On your instrument, sweeps run 0.9% below the level on average. Where does that leave your stop?

A) 0.4% inside the average sweep, so an ordinary sweep reaches it
B) 0.4% clear of the average sweep, so an ordinary sweep misses it
C) Level with the average sweep, so it is a coin flip
D) Sweep depth and stop distance are not comparable
Correct! Sweep clearance = average sweep depth − stop distance = 0.9% − 0.5% = −0.4%. The stop sits 0.4% inside the zone sweeps routinely reach, so an ordinary sweep takes it. Marcus's gap was 0.1%. To sit clear, the stop has to go beyond 0.9%, which is what the 1.5-2% rule is buying you.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Obvious levels get swept because retail stops cluster below them — the wick through the level typically runs 0.3-0.8% past it
  • Which is where the stop goes: 1.5-2% beyond the level, or below the swept low once you are entering on the reclaim. Outside where sweeps go, not outside the level
  • Wait for the sweep, then watch for the reclaim — a sweep with no reclaim means the level failed
  • Entry timing is worth 3.3% of position value: the same call returns −0.5% entered before the sweep and +2.8% entered after the reclaim
  • Volume + rapid reversal = Sweep confirmation
