SPY is trading at $450.20. There are 200,000 call contracts open at the $450 strike expiring Friday. If dealers are short most of them, they are holding SPY against that exposure — but note both qualifications, because most writing on this subject drops them. Open interest counts contracts, not sides: it does not tell you whether dealers are short those calls or long them. And at a delta near 0.50 the hedge is roughly $4.5 billion of stock, not the $9 billion of notional the strike represents. What happens to that hedge into Friday is what this lesson is about, and it is more interesting than the version you will usually hear.

This isn't theory. This is mechanical reality.

Options market makers don't just provide liquidity—their hedging activity creates massive, predictable price movements that most traders never see coming.

🚨 Why Most Traders Get Wrecked on OpEx

Friday 3:00 PM: SPY is rallying on good news. You buy calls. At 3:30 PM, SPY inexplicably dumps 1.5% in 15 minutes with NO news. Your calls expire worthless.

What happened? Something real, but not the thing you will usually be told. Dealer hedges do change fast in the last hour of an expiration, and the change can move price. What is NOT true — and this lesson used to say it in six places — is that dealers are obliged to liquidate their hedge into the close.

Part 1: Market Maker Delta Hedging Mechanics

Why Dealers MUST Hedge (And How It Moves Markets)

When you buy a call option, you're usually buying it from a market maker (Citadel, Susquehanna, Optiver, etc.). They don't want directional risk—they want to collect the spread without taking a directional view.

To stay neutral, they delta hedge by buying/selling the underlying stock.

Step-by-Step: How Delta Hedging Works

Example: Institution Buys 10,000 SPY $450 Calls

Trade: JPMorgan buys 10,000 SPY $450 calls from Citadel

Citadel's Position: Short 10,000 calls (naked short = exposed to unlimited upside)

Delta at time of sale: 0.50 (50% chance of expiring ITM)

Hedge Required: 10,000 contracts × 100 shares × 0.50 delta = 500,000 SPY shares

Action: Citadel buys 500,000 SPY shares to hedge

Result: Citadel is now delta-neutral for this instant. Note "for this instant": a delta hedge is only good until price moves. And the profit is not simply theta. A delta-hedged short call earns theta and pays gamma — every rehedge is a buy higher and a sell lower, which costs money. The position makes money only if realised volatility comes in below the implied volatility it was sold at. Describing it as "collecting theta decay" leaves out the half that can bankrupt you.

What Happens When Price Moves Up

SPY rallies from $448 → $452

New Delta: 0.75 (now 75% ITM probability)

New Hedge Required: 10,000 × 100 × 0.75 = 750,000 shares

Current Hedge: 500,000 shares

Action: Citadel must BUY another 250,000 shares

Impact: This buying pushes SPY higher → delta increases more → more buying → gamma squeeze!

What Happens When Price Moves Down

SPY drops from $452 → $448

New Delta: 0.30 (now only 30% ITM probability)

New Hedge Required: 10,000 × 100 × 0.30 = 300,000 shares

Current Hedge: 750,000 shares (over-hedged!)

Action: Citadel must SELL 450,000 shares

Impact: This selling pushes SPY lower → delta decreases more → more selling → crash amplification!

💡 The Key Insight

Market makers are mechanical hedgers. They:

  • Buy when price rises (to increase hedge)
  • Sell when price falls (to decrease hedge)

This is trend-amplifying behaviour — but only while the dealer is short the option. A dealer who is LONG gamma does the opposite: sells into strength, buys into weakness, and dampens the move. Both regimes exist, and Part 6 is about telling them apart. Get the sign wrong and every conclusion in between inverts.

⚠️ The "forced 4 PM unwind" is not a thing

You will read everywhere that dealers "MUST unwind billions of shares before the close on expiration Friday". It is worth knowing exactly why that is wrong, because a great deal of OpEx folklore is built on it.

Take the dealer short 250,000 SPY calls, hedged long stock. On Friday afternoon there are two cases. If the calls finish in the money, they are exercised and the dealer DELIVERS the hedge to the option holders. No shares hit the market at all. If the calls finish out of the money, delta goes to zero and the hedge does have to be sold — but that happens as delta decays, which is gradual and, in the last hour, concentrated in a narrow band around the strike, not as a $11 billion waterfall.

Two more corrections. The unwind is not one-directional: a dealer short puts that expire worthless has to BUY BACK short stock, which is upward pressure at the same moment. And the flows are pro-cyclical, not directional in advance — a hedge is unwound because price moved away from a strike, so it follows the move rather than predicting it. That is precisely why "buy puts at 3 PM on OpEx Friday" is not a strategy: you have to know which way price will leave the strike before the flow tells you anything, and knowing that is the whole problem.

The real effects of expiration are subtler and better documented: gamma concentrated at a strike pins price toward it when dealers are long gamma there, and repels price from it when they are short. Everything below is more useful once that replaces the waterfall story.

Part 1.5: The $67,200 Lesson—Kyle's OpEx Disaster

📉 CASE STUDY: Kyle's $67,200 OpEx Unwind Disaster (45 minutes)

Trader: Kyle Peterson (composite example), 28, options trader ($95K account), March 15, 2024 (Monthly OpEx)

Strategy: Bought 400 SPY $520 calls @ $2.15 at 3:00 PM on OpEx Friday (expiring 4 PM), betting on rally continuation

Fatal flaw: he bought a same-day option in the last hour, when the only thing that could save it was a move in his direction inside sixty minutes. The hedging story he later told himself about it was wrong in the details — dealers were under no obligation to liquidate anything before the close — but the trade was a bad trade before any of that, for a reason that needs no dealer at all: an option with an hour left is almost entirely gamma, its value is a step function around the strike, and there is no thesis you can hold for an hour that survives being wrong by fifty cents.

Result: Lost $67,200 (-78.1%) in 45 minutes as mechanical MM selling crushed SPY. Calls bought at $2.15, sold at $0.47

Setup (3:00 PM): SPY $521.80. Kyle buys 400 $520 calls @ $2.15 ($86K). Didn't know: 250K call contracts = MMs hedged with 21.25M shares ($11B) they MUST unwind before 4 PM.

Unwind (3:00-3:45 PM): MMs sold waves: 2M shares ($521.80 → $521.20), then 5M ($521.20 → $519.80, Kyle -20.9%), then 10M in waterfall ($519.80 → $518.20). Kyle panic-sold at $0.47. Loss: $86K → $18.8K = -$67.2K (-78.1%). By 4:05 PM: SPY bounced $520.50. Monday: $523. Purely mechanical, no fundamentals.

What changed: he stopped buying same-day options in the last hour. That is the whole change, and it is the only one supported by what happened to him. No recovery record is reported: a mirror-image winning trade and a six-month win rate would be describing a stretch that was chosen because it worked, and the symmetric version — "I bought puts at 2:45 and SPY closed higher" — is equally easy to construct and never gets written down.

What is actually worth taking from this: the phrase "market makers have obligations, not opinions" is true and useful. The conclusion Kyle drew from it — that the obligation runs one way, downward, at a predictable hour — is not, and it is how the same mistake gets made in the opposite direction. Buying puts at 3 PM on expiration Friday is the identical bet with the sign flipped: same hour, same step function, same requirement that price move your way inside sixty minutes. There is no version of "OpEx is an ATM". The usable rule from his $67,200 is narrow and dull, and dull rules are the ones that hold: do not buy a nearly worthless option and require the market to move a specific distance in a specific hour.

Case Study Quiz: Kyle bought 400 SPY $520 calls at 3:00 PM on OpEx Friday at $2.15 when SPY was trading $521.80. Max pain was $518. What was Kyle's critical mistake?

A) He bought calls that were too far out of the money
B) He used too much leverage (should have bought only 200 calls)
C) He bought calls in the final hour of OpEx when market makers had $11B in hedges they HAD to unwind regardless of price action
D) He should have set a tighter stop loss at $520.50
Correct: C. He bought an option with an hour of life left, at a strike price was sitting right on top of, and needed a move in his direction inside that hour. SPY went from $521.80 to $518.20 and his calls went from $2.15 to $0.47. Be careful with the explanation, though, because the popular one is wrong: dealers were NOT obliged to liquidate $11 billion of stock before the close. Calls that finish in the money are exercised and the hedge is delivered rather than sold; calls that finish out of the money see their hedge unwound as delta decays, which follows the move rather than causing it. What is true is that gamma near a strike on expiration day is enormous, so the last hour is where an at-the-money option is most violent in both directions — and Kyle happened to be on the wrong side of it. The proof that this is about position structure rather than dealer conspiracy is in the postscript: SPY bounced to $520.50 at 4:05 PM and traded $523 on Monday. He was not wrong about direction. He was wrong about the hour, and an hour was all he had bought.
Part 2: Gamma—The Accelerator of Market Movements

Why Gamma Matters More Than Delta

Delta tells you how much stock MMs are hedged with RIGHT NOW.
Gamma tells you how FAST that hedge changes as price moves.

Gamma = Rate of change of delta

High gamma environment:

  • Small price moves → large delta changes → massive rehedging flows
  • MMs constantly buying/selling → amplifies volatility
  • Perfect setup for gamma squeezes (GME, AMC)

Real Example: GameStop Gamma Squeeze (Jan 2021)

GME Gamma Squeeze (Jan 2021): the price path is public record. GME closed around $20 on 11 January and around $31 on the 13th, ran through the $40s and $60s over the following week, and printed an intraday high of $483 on 28 January. Call volume in the name was extraordinary throughout, the float was under 50 million shares, and short interest was famously enormous. The gamma-squeeze mechanic is the right explanation for the shape of the move: dealers short calls into a rising price have to buy more stock as delta climbs, in a name where there is not much stock to buy. What is NOT public record is how many shares dealers actually held — the "150M+, three times the float" figure this lesson used to print is invented, and impossible on its face, since it exceeds the shares outstanding. The share counts have been removed rather than guessed at. The mechanic survives without them; a made-up number does not make it more convincing, only less checkable.

Lesson:

  • High option volume + small float = explosive upside
  • MMs mechanically buy on the way up (no choice)
  • Feedback loop can push price to absurd levels

But They're Temporary:

  • The squeeze runs out of fuel when new call buying stops, because the mechanic needs a stream of fresh delta to keep the dealer buying
  • GME fell from $483 to the $40s within two weeks. Note what did not cause that: dealers being obliged to unwind at expiration. The boxed correction in Part 1 applies here too — there is no forced 4 PM liquidation. What happened is that several brokers restricted opening buy orders in the name on 28 January, the inflow stopped, and a price held up by continuous buying could not stay there without it
  • Millions of late buyers got wrecked, and the reason is worth being precise about: they were buying the part of the move that the buying itself had created

🚨 Trading Gamma Squeezes

Entry: Early when gamma is building (high OI, rising IV)

Exit: When fresh call buying slows. That is the fuel, and it is observable in daily option volume against open interest. Do not exit "before expiration because dealers will dump" — that is the forced-unwind story Part 1 takes apart, and it will have you exiting on a calendar date instead of on the thing that actually ends the move.

Risk: The whole structure is reflexive, so it unwinds faster than it built. You are not being paid for analysis here; you are being paid for being early, and there is no way to know in advance which day is late.

Part 3: Max Pain Theory & Pin Risk

Why Price Gravitates Toward Specific Strikes on Expiration

Max Pain is the strike at which the total intrinsic value that option writers would have to pay out is smallest. Not, as it is usually put, the strike at which the most contracts expire worthless — those are different numbers, because a thousand cheap contracts and one expensive one do not weigh the same. Note also who it is not about: market makers are not the option sellers here in any meaningful sense. They are hedged, roughly flat, and earn the spread; the writers who would actually pay out are whoever is net short each strike, which the data does not identify. Max pain is a property of the open interest table, not a party's profit target.

Pin Risk is the tendency for price to settle near a high-open-interest strike as expiration approaches. It is real and measurable, most clearly in single names with concentrated open interest and far less clearly in an index ETF traded in SPY's size. It also has a specific cause rather than magnetism: near a strike, at expiration, gamma is enormous, so dealers who are long gamma there have to sell every tick up and buy every tick down, and that is what holds price in place. Where dealers are short gamma at the strike the same mechanic runs backwards and price is pushed away from it. Which is why "price drifts to max pain" is a tendency you should check on your own instrument, not a law.

How to Calculate Max Pain

It is a small piece of arithmetic and worth doing by hand once, because doing it will tell you more about the shape of the open interest than any website that prints the answer.

  1. Pull the option chain for the expiration you care about: every strike, with call open interest and put open interest.
  2. Pick a candidate settlement price. Use each listed strike in turn.
  3. At that candidate price, work out the intrinsic value the writers owe. For every strike BELOW the candidate, calls are in the money: (candidate - strike) × call OI × 100. For every strike ABOVE the candidate, puts are in the money: (strike - candidate) × put OI × 100.
  4. Add the two together. That is the total payout at that candidate price.
  5. Repeat for every strike and take the candidate with the SMALLEST total. That strike is max pain.

A worked fragment, to make step 3 concrete. Suppose SPY has 40,000 calls and 10,000 puts at $448, and 20,000 calls and 60,000 puts at $452, and you are testing a candidate of $450. The $448 calls are $2 in the money: 2 × 40,000 × 100 = $8,000,000. The $452 puts are $2 in the money: 2 × 60,000 × 100 = $12,000,000. The $448 puts and $452 calls are worthless and contribute nothing. Total at $450 = $20,000,000. Do that for every strike and read off the minimum.

Two things the calculation cannot do, which is why it is worth having built it yourself. It weights by open interest, not by who holds what, so it cannot tell you whether dealers are long or short that gamma — and that sign decides whether price is pinned to the strike or pushed off it. And it moves during the session as open interest changes, so a max pain figure read at the open is a different number by 2 PM.

Trading Vanna: The Post-Earnings Fade

Strategy: Fade the vanna unwind when IV crush is severe

Setup Requirements:

  • High pre-earnings IV (40%+)
  • Massive call OI in near-the-money strikes
  • Earnings result mildly negative (not catastrophic)

Entry:

  • Watch the vanna unwind selling in the first 30 minutes. This part is observable
  • Note when it exhausts. What that tells you is that the next move will be driven by opinion rather than by hedging — it does not tell you which way
  • So there is no entry here, and the previous version of this box gave one anyway. If you take a long, take it for a reason that would have been a reason without the unwind, and treat the exhaustion as permission to stop waiting rather than as the signal itself

What the shape looks like (constructed, undated — the point is the sequence, not a session):

  • 9:30 AM: TSLA dumps to $233.80 (vanna selling)
  • 10:00 AM: Selling exhausts, TSLA stabilizes
  • 10:15 AM: Buy TSLA calls (betting on bounce)
  • By late morning: the stock recovers most of the opening drop. Note what this is and is not: it is a description of a shape that shows up after big events, not a claim about how often it completes. The unwind exhausting is observable; that it will be followed by a recovery is not.
  • What you are waiting for: the mechanical selling to run out, so that what moves price next is someone with an opinion rather than someone with a hedge. That is a real thing to watch for. It is not a signal that price goes up afterwards.

Charm: How Time Decay Affects Delta

Charm = Change in delta per day of time decay

As options approach expiration, their delta accelerates toward 0 (OTM) or 1 (ITM). This creates predictable dealer rehedging flows in the final days before expiration.

📖 Charm Mechanics on OpEx Week

Monday (5 days to expiration):

  • SPY $450 call (ATM): Delta = 0.50
  • Time decay impact: Minimal

Wednesday (3 days to expiration):

  • Same call: Delta = 0.52 (increased slightly as theta decays)
  • Charm accelerating

Friday (expiration day, 2 hours left):

  • SPY at $450.50 (slightly ITM)
  • Delta EXPLODES from 0.52 → 0.95 in final 2 hours
  • MMs must buy 4.3 million additional shares per 100K contracts
  • If the dealer is SHORT those calls, that buying pushes SPY higher, delta goes further toward 1.00, and they buy more. That is a squeeze away from the strike, not a pin — and this lesson previously drew the opposite conclusion from these same numbers, which is the single easiest error to make in this subject.
  • If the dealer is LONG those calls, the sign flips: they sell as delta rises and buy as it falls, and THAT is what holds price at the strike.
  • Result: the strike acts as a magnet or a trampoline depending on which side of that gamma the dealers are on. Charm tells you the flows will be large in the last hours. It does not, by itself, tell you their direction.
Part 4: Vanna—Volatility's Hidden Influence on Delta

What is Vanna?

Vanna measures how much delta changes when implied volatility (IV) changes.

📐 Vanna Formula (Simplified)

Vanna = Change in Delta ÷ Change in IV

Example:

  • SPY $450 call, IV = 15%, Delta = 0.40
  • VIX spikes +5 points → IV jumps to 20%
  • Vanna effect: Delta increases from 0.40 to 0.46 (+0.06)

Why it matters — and why the simple version is wrong: the sentence you will see everywhere is "when volatility spikes, dealers must buy more shares to hedge the same calls". That is true for an out-of-the-money call and only for that. Vanna changes sign across the strike, so an in-the-money call moves the other way, and more to the point, an index dealer book is not made of calls. Customers are net buyers of downside puts, so dealers are net short puts and hedged short stock; rising IV deepens that short delta and they sell more. The table below works through both directions. Get the sign from the book you are actually looking at, never from the greek in isolation.

✅ Trading Vanna Squeeze

Setup: VIX drops sharply (e.g., 28 → 18 in 3 days), typically once a known event has passed and the premium paid for it is released

What happens, in the direction it actually goes:

  1. Falling IV shrinks the delta of the short puts that dominate an index dealer book
  2. Dealers need less short stock against them, so they buy stock back
  3. That buying is a tailwind, not a drag — which is why the market so often drifts up in the sessions after an event resolves

What NOT to do with this: the previous version of this lesson said "short SPY when VIX drops below 20". That is backwards, and it is backwards for a reason worth internalising. A falling VIX in an index book releases dealers from short stock hedges, which is buying, and it is one of the more reliable tailwinds there is. Falling volatility has historically gone with rising equities, not falling ones. If you want to trade vanna at all, the honest version is: expect the mechanical bid AFTER an event resolves and IV collapses, size it small, and know that it is a flow argument with no edge over the direction the market was going anyway.

Part 5: Volmageddon—Volatility Regime Shifts

When Volatility Explodes

Most of the time VIX trades in the teens. Spikes above 30 are much rarer than the "2-3 times per year" you will often see quoted — in most calendar years since 1990 it does not happen at all, and the years it does are the ones you can name. No frequency is given here for that reason; count them on your own chart and the shape of the distribution will teach you more than a number would.

What triggers Volmageddon:

  • Fed surprises (unexpected rate hike, hawkish pivot)
  • Geopolitical shocks (wars, banking crisis)
  • Earnings disasters (mega-cap misses guidance)
  • Technical breaks (key support levels collapse)

The Volpocalypse Cascade

February 5, 2018 - The Original Volmageddon

  1. Background (Friday 2 February): a strong jobs report with an upside wage surprise revived rate-hike fears. Note the date — the report was the Friday, not the Monday, and this lesson previously placed it at 1 PM on the 5th.
  2. 2:00 PM: Volatility ETFs (XIV, SVXY) start unwinding → forced buying of VIX futures
  3. 3:00 PM: VIX hits 30 → triggers more unwinds → feedback loop begins
  4. Into the close: the S&P finished the day down 4.1%, having fallen far more than that at its worst point in the afternoon. The VIX move was a full-day move, not a 2.5-hour one; the lesson previously compressed it.
  5. Monday close and after: VIX closed 5 February at 37.32, up about 116% on the day from 17.31 the previous Friday. VIX futures kept going after the cash close, and VIX itself traded above 50 the following morning. Credit Suisse announced the acceleration of XIV on the 6th; its indicative value had fallen roughly 96%, from about $99 to about $6. Not literally a 100% loss, but close enough that the distinction only mattered to lawyers.

Result: the S&P fell about 10% from its 26 January high to its 8 February low, nine sessions. Traders who were short volatility without a defined loss lost most of what they had committed to it — which is the actual lesson, and it is about position structure rather than about the VIX. A short volatility position with no long option against it has no maximum loss, and the day you find out is the day it does not matter what your win rate was.

Trading Volatility Spikes

⚠️ Don't Fight Volatility Explosions

Common mistake: "VIX is at 35, that's too high, I'll sell puts (bet on calm)"

Reality: VIX can go from 35 to 60 in hours. Selling volatility during spikes = picking up pennies in front of a steamroller.

Better approach: the safe conclusion from this section is negative, not positive. Do not sell volatility without a long option capping the loss, and do not size a short volatility position off its win rate, because the win rate is high right up until the trade that ends the account. Buying protection once VIX is already above 30 is buying it at the price the panic set, which is a hedge you should have owned earlier and rarely a trade. No return figures are quoted for it here; a range like "300-500%" describes one path out of many and tells you nothing about how often you pay the premium and get nothing.

Part 6: Dealer Positioning & Hedging Flow

Dealer Long Gamma vs Short Gamma

Market makers (dealers) are either long gamma or short gamma depending on retail/institutional option positioning.

Short Gamma Regime (Volatile Markets)

🔴 Dealers Short Gamma = Amplified Volatility

What it means:

  • Retail/institutions bought lots of calls and puts
  • Dealers sold those options (now short gamma)
  • Dealers must hedge by buying when price rises, selling when price falls

Effect on price:

  • Price rises 1% → Dealers buy shares (chase rally) → price rises MORE
  • Price falls 1% → Dealers sell shares (accelerate decline) → price falls MORE
  • Result: Exaggerated moves, increased volatility

Trading implication: In short gamma regimes (VIX > 20), momentum trades work. Buy breakouts, short breakdowns. Dealers amplify the move.

Long Gamma Regime (Calm Markets)

🟢 Dealers Long Gamma = Dampened Volatility

What it means:

  • Retail/institutions sold lots of options (collecting premium)
  • Dealers bought those options (now long gamma)
  • Dealers hedge by selling when price rises, buying when price falls

Effect on price:

  • Price rises 1% → Dealers sell shares (fade rally) → price mean-reverts
  • Price falls 1% → Dealers buy shares (support dip) → price mean-reverts
  • Result: Range-bound, low volatility

Trading implication: In long gamma regimes (VIX < 15), mean-reversion works. Sell rallies, buy dips. Dealers provide liquidity and dampen moves.

Part 7: OpEx + Earnings Combo Events (The Perfect Storm)

📉 CASE STUDY: Sarah's $96,000 OpEx + Earnings Disaster (6 hours)

Trader: Sarah Chen (composite example), 32, options trader ($180K account), November 17, 2023 (Monthly OpEx + NVDA Earnings)

Strategy: Bought 300 NVDA $510 calls @ $4.80 at 9:35 AM on OpEx Friday after earnings beat, betting momentum continues

Fatal flaw: she bought the direction she already believed, on the one day when the option would expire before the direction had time to arrive. The dealer-hedging story that gets told about this trade is overstated — nobody was obliged to sell 35 million shares by 4 PM — but she did not need one to lose: she paid for a thesis with a deadline, and the deadline came first.

Result: Lost $96,000 (-66.7%) in 6 hours as mechanical MM selling crushed NVDA. Calls bought at $4.80, sold at $1.60. NVDA rallied to $512 by Monday (but calls expired worthless Friday)

The setup (Nov 15-17): NVDA earnings Wed after close: beat by 7%, strong guidance, stock jumped 8% to $505. Thursday: rallied to $508, call OI exploded ($510: 180K contracts, $515: 120K, $520: 90K). MMs hedged with 35M shares ($17.6B). Friday = monthly OpEx = MMs MUST unwind ALL shares by 4 PM. Sarah's fatal assumption: "Earnings beat + rally = more upside." What she missed: Dealer mechanics trump fundamentals short-term.

The disaster (Friday 9:30 AM-3:45 PM): (1) 9:30-10:00 AM: FOMO phase, retail piles in, NVDA peaks $506.50, Sarah's calls +12.5% to $5.40, she thinks "I'll hold for $520." (2) 10:00 AM-12:00 PM: MMs start unwind, sell 3M shares first wave, NVDA stalls then bleeds to $503, Sarah's calls -16.7% to $4.00, "Just a dip, earnings were great." (3) 12:00-2:00 PM: Reality sets in, NVDA $500.50, calls -41.7% to $2.80, "This isn't bouncing." (4) 2:00-3:30 PM: Waterfall, final MM unwind wave (10M shares in 90 min), NVDA crashes to $498.80 (just under max pain at $500), calls -66.7% to $1.60. (5) 3:45 PM: Sarah capitulates, sells 300 calls @ $1.60. Exit: $48K. Loss: $144K → $48K = -$96K.

Post-mortem: 4:00 PM: NVDA closed $499.20 (dealer unwind complete). Monday Nov 20: NVDA rallied to $512 (earnings momentum resumed). Sarah's $510 calls would've been worth $8.50 next week—but they expired worthless Friday. Sarah's mistake: Bought same-day calls on OpEx Friday with massive dealer hedge overhang. Earnings were bullish, mechanics were bearish. Mechanics always win short-term.

What changed, and a warning about the obvious next move: she stopped buying same-day options into an expiration. No recovery record is given, and in this case the omission matters more than usual, because the trade a reader will reach for next is selling those calls instead of buying them — and that is a much more dangerous position than the one that lost her $96,000. Two hundred naked calls sold at $3.80 collects $76,000 and carries no defined maximum loss. If the stock gaps through the strike, the loss is whatever the gap is, times 20,000 shares. Selling premium into expiration works most of the time and that is precisely the problem: the win rate is high, the losses are unbounded, and the arithmetic of that combination is in Lesson 36. If you want the short-premium version of this idea, buy the further-out option against it and turn it into a spread. The capped profit is what you pay for having a worst case at all.

The half of her conclusion that survives: "when earnings land on or near expiration Friday, do not buy same-day calls" is sound, and it is sound for the plain reason rather than the dealer reason — you are paying for a directional view and giving it hours to be right. "Fade the unwind instead" is where it goes wrong. That instruction assumes a flow whose direction you cannot know in advance, and it invites a naked short option as the way to express it. Being right about a mistake does not make its mirror image correct; sometimes the honest conclusion is that the day is simply not tradeable and the position to hold is none.

Case Study Quiz: Sarah bought 300 NVDA $510 calls on OpEx Friday morning after NVDA beat earnings by 7% and rallied 8%. The stock opened at $506.50 and she bought at 9:35 AM for $4.80. Why did she lose $96,000 by 3:45 PM despite excellent earnings?

A) NVDA earnings were actually bad once analysts dug into the details
B) The semiconductor sector collapsed due to macro news
C) She should have bought the $515 strike instead of $510
D) Market makers had $17.6B in NVDA hedges (35M shares) they HAD to unwind by 4 PM OpEx, creating mechanical selling that overwhelmed bullish fundamentals
Correct: D — with the explanation corrected. D is the right answer because it is the only option that identifies the expiration, and the expiration is what killed the trade. But the version of D printed on the card overstates the mechanism, so read the accurate one. Dealers were not required to liquidate 35 million shares by 4 PM: in-the-money calls are exercised and the hedge is delivered, out-of-the-money calls see their hedge run off as delta decays. What is true is that on expiration day, near a heavily traded strike, gamma is enormous and price is unusually well anchored to that strike — so a call bought $6 above it needed the stock to hold a level it was being pulled away from, on the one day when there was no tomorrow to be right in. Everything else is confirmation of the same point: the earnings thesis was correct, NVDA traded $512 the following Monday, and her calls were worthless before that happened. The lesson is about the deadline, not about a villain.
Part 8: The Complete OpEx Trading Playbook

Actionable Strategies: How to Trade Every OpEx Scenario

Now that you understand the mechanics, here's your complete playbook for trading around dealer positioning.

OpEx Trading Strategies:

1. Weekly expirations (Fridays): note where max pain sits relative to price, and treat a large gap as a reason to be sceptical of a breakout away from it rather than as an entry. No example trade with a profit attached is given here, and that is deliberate: an example chosen because it worked tells you what the author wanted to show, not what the setup does. Before you size anything off this, mark the max pain level every Friday for two months and record where price actually closed. That record is worth more than any of the four strategies below, because it will be yours.

2. Monthly expirations (3rd Friday): far more contracts, far more gamma, and a much stronger tendency for price to sit near a big strike. The previous version of this lesson told you to sell an at-the-money straddle at 3 PM and quoted a 65% win rate for it. That advice has been removed and it is worth saying why, since you will meet it elsewhere. A short at-the-money straddle on expiration day has no defined maximum loss and collects a premium that is small precisely because the market agrees price will not move much. It also carries assignment risk in exactly the scenario the trade is built on: if price settles a cent either side of the strike you can end up short or long stock over the weekend without intending to. The high win rate is not a reason to take it. It is the mechanism by which the losing trade, when it comes, is larger than everything before it.

3. Quarterly expirations (quad witching): index options, index futures, single-stock options and futures all expire together, volumes are extreme and the day is genuinely hard to read. The lesson previously suggested buying a straddle into it. Note what that costs: everyone knows the day is volatile, so the straddle is priced for it, and you need a move bigger than the one already in the price. "Unpredictable" is not the same as "profitable", and a day nobody can read is a day to have a smaller position, not a cleverer one.

4. Zero-day options: SPX has expirations every session, so the gamma concentration this lesson describes now happens daily rather than monthly. Two constructed winning trades and a 68% win rate used to sit here; both are gone. What is worth keeping is the shape of the day — gamma at the nearest strikes grows through the session, so an option is least sensitive to direction in the morning and most violent in the last hour. Which cuts the way you would not expect: the last hour is when the instrument is least forgiving of being slightly wrong, not when it is most rewarding. If you trade this at all, the sizing question is not "what is my win rate" but "what fraction of the account am I prepared to lose in an afternoon", because on a zero-day option that fraction is 100% of what you put in, routinely, and the routine is the point.

OpEx Checklist: Pre-market: Check max pain, gamma exposure, highest OI strikes, earnings. Open: Compare price to max pain (>$3 = opportunity), set gamma level alerts. Throughout: 11 AM reassess, 2 PM prepare final hour, 3:15 PM close positions. Post-close: Review SPY vs max pain, note deviations.

Part 9: Tools & Resources for Tracking Dealer Positioning

How to Monitor Real-Time Dealer Flows

Essential Tools:

1. Commercial gamma-exposure services (SpotGamma and similar): they publish a modelled dealer gamma profile, a gamma flip level where the sign of the hedging changes, and max pain. No price is quoted here because subscription prices change and quoting one reads as a recommendation. What matters more than which service you pick: every one of them is running a MODEL, because dealer positioning is not published. They infer it from where trades printed relative to the bid and ask. Two services can and do disagree about the sign on the same day.

2. DIX and GEX (SqueezeMetrics): DIX is the buy-side share of off-exchange volume, GEX the modelled gamma. The bands usually quoted — above about 45% as heavy buying, below about 40% as heavy selling — are readings of the historical distribution rather than fixed thresholds, so recalibrate them against a few years of the series before you lean on them. Again no price is quoted; check what the service currently offers.

3. Free Tools: CBOE P/C Ratio (CBOE.com): >1.0 bearish, <0.7 bullish. TradingView OI Indicator: Chart overlay for gamma walls. Maximum-Pain.com: Free max pain calculator, updates throughout day.

🚨 Don't Overtrade Based on Dealer Positioning

Tools show probabilities, not certainties. Use dealer positioning as ONE input, not the only input.

Best use: Filter out bad trades (e.g., don't buy calls 3:30 PM on OpEx when massive unwind pending)

Worst use: Force trades just because max pain exists (sometimes it doesn't materialize)

🔧 Put This To Work This Week (Click to expand)

Start with these 3 actions:

  1. Check max pain for SPY on Friday, using the calculation in Part 3 or any free calculator. Note the strike with the highest open interest. Then track where SPY actually closes. No hit rate is given here — the "60-70% within $2" that used to be printed was invented, and the whole value of this exercise is that the number you produce is yours. Ten expirations is not enough to settle it either; treat it as learning what to look at.
  2. Watch the last hour of an expiration Friday without trading it. What you are looking for is how price behaves near the big strikes: whether it sticks to one, gets pushed off one, and how much wider the option spreads get. What you are NOT looking for is a signal to fade — a move in the last hour of expiration is not evidence that it will reverse, and the fact that it has no news attached does not make it wrong.
  3. Paper trade ONE expiration setup and record every instance, not the ones that worked. Thursday: note max pain. Friday: if SPY opens more than a few dollars above it, log what a short would have done by 3:30 PM — including the days it would have been stopped out. Three instances tell you nothing; twenty start to. No example result is given here, because a single worked winner is what convinces people to skip exactly this step.
Quick Knowledge Check

📝 Knowledge Check

Test your understanding of options market microstructure:

SPY is at $455 on Friday morning. Max pain is at $450. 100,000 call contracts at $450 strike expire today. What's likely to happen by 3 PM?

A) SPY drifts down toward $450 as dealers unwind call hedges
B) SPY rallies higher due to momentum
C) SPY stays flat at $455 with no directional bias
Correct: A — but for a different reason than you were probably given. A drift back toward a heavily traded strike into expiration is a real, documented tendency, and it is worth knowing what actually causes it and what it requires. First, the mechanism that does NOT apply here: the $450 calls are $5 in the money, so they will be exercised, and a dealer short them delivers the hedge to the holders rather than selling it. Hedges are not liquidated into the close simply because the option is expiring. What does apply is gamma. At a strike with 100,000 contracts on expiration day, tiny price moves change the required hedge enormously, so the hedging flow near $450 dwarfs everything else on the tape. Whether that pins price to the strike or repels it from it depends on which side of that gamma the dealers are on: research on expiration-day pinning finds the clustering happens when market makers are net LONG the options at that strike (they sell each tick up and buy each tick down), and finds prices pushed AWAY from the strike when they are net short. So the honest answer is A with a condition attached, and the condition is the part the open interest table cannot tell you. No hit rate is quoted — the "60-65% of monthly expirations" printed here previously was invented.

It's 3:00 PM on OpEx Friday. You see SPY at $520 with max pain at $515. Large call open interest at $520 strike is about to expire. You have a bullish thesis on SPY for next week. What's the best move?

A) Buy SPY calls expiring today at 3:00 PM (cheap premium, might rally)
B) Wait until 4:05 PM after OpEx unwind completes, then buy Monday calls or shares
C) Buy SPY shares now at $520 to ride momentum into close
Correct: B. And notice that you do not need any dealer story to get there. Your thesis is about next week. Option A buys an instrument that ceases to exist in one hour, which cannot express a next-week view no matter how right the view is — that is the mistake both case studies in this lesson made, in both directions. Option C buys shares into the single most gamma-distorted hour of the month, when price is being moved by hedging rather than by anything about SPY. B waits for the distortion to pass and then takes the position that matches the horizon of the idea. No bounce figure is given and no example is quoted: "price often bounces 0.5-1.5% afterwards" was an invented regularity, and if it were reliable it would be a trade in itself, which it is not. The reason to wait is that your thesis has a one-week horizon and there is no hurry, not that a rebound is owed to you.

You're analyzing GME during a potential gamma squeeze. Open interest: 200K call contracts at $40 strike (GME currently $38). Delta = 0.60. If GME rallies to $42, what happens to market maker hedging?

A) Market makers sell stock to reduce their hedge (they're over-hedged)
B) Market makers don't need to adjust hedges (delta is stable)
C) Market makers buy more stock as delta increases toward 1.0, amplifying the rally
Correct: C, on the assumption stated in the question — that dealers are short those calls, which is the usual configuration when retail is buying calls in size, though open interest alone never establishes it. Given that: GME $38, $40 calls at 0.60 delta, dealers hedged with 12M shares (200K × 100 × 0.60). GME rallies $42, calls deep ITM, delta → 0.95. Dealers need 19M shares (200K × 100 × 0.95). Must BUY 7M more. Buying pushes GME higher → delta increases → more buying → feedback loop = gamma squeeze. Jan 2021 GME: $20 → $483. High call OI + small float + rapid move = exponential acceleration. Reverse: GME drops $36, delta 0.30, dealers dump 6M shares. Gamma squeezes are mechanical, not fundamental.

Expiration is not random, and it is not a conspiracy either. Dealers hedge because they have to, gamma decides how violently, and the sign of their position decides whether a big strike attracts price or repels it. Knowing that will mostly tell you which days to leave alone.

⚡ Quick Wins for Tomorrow (Click to expand)

Don’t overwhelm yourself. Start with these 3 actions:

  1. Pull the open interest by strike for one ticker you trade — find where it clusters and mark those strikes on your chart. Watch how price behaves near them into Friday.
  2. Calculate max pain once, by hand — doing the arithmetic yourself is what turns it from a number someone quotes into something you can sanity-check.
  3. Track one expiry week end to end — note where price started, where the big strikes sat, and where it settled. One week proves nothing; it teaches you what to look at.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Dealer hedging is mechanical, not discretionary — they hedge because they must, and that flow moves the underlying. It does not follow that they are obliged to liquidate anything at the close: in-the-money options are exercised and the hedge is delivered
  • Gamma matters more than delta at the extremes — it governs how fast the hedge has to change
  • Price often settles near high-open-interest strikes into expiry — but the pull becomes a push when dealers are short the gamma at that strike, and max pain alone cannot tell you which it is
  • Charm drifts delta as time passes — the hedge changes even when price does not
  • Options flow is a cause of equity movement, not just a reflection of it
