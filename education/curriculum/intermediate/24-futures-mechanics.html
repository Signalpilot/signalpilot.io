Futures Mechanics: Roll, Tick and Margin

Reading time ~13 min • Contract Mechanics •

A futures contract cannot be cut in half. That single fact changes the arithmetic of everything in the previous lesson, and it is why futures deserve a lesson of their own rather than a row in a table.

With shares you scale down until the risk fits the account. With futures the smallest unit is fixed, so the account has to fit the unit. Traders who miss this do not discover it gradually; they discover it in one afternoon.

💸 The number nobody runs first

A ten-point stop on an E-mini S&P contract is $500. At 1% risk that requires a $50,000 account for a single contract. The same trade on the Micro E-mini requires $5,000. Most accounts that blow up on futures were never large enough to hold one contract at a sensible stop, and the trader closed that gap by tightening the stop.

🚨 Real Talk

Brokers advertise day-trade margins of a few hundred dollars a contract. That number tells you what you must post to open the position. It tells you nothing about what the position can lose, and the two are not related. Treating the margin requirement as a risk measure is the most expensive misreading in this market.

🎯 What You’ll Master

By the end of this lesson, you’ll understand:

  • How to compute the minimum account a futures stop actually requires, before you open anything
  • Why margin and risk are unrelated numbers, and what margin genuinely constrains
  • What the quarterly roll costs you, and why the chart you backtested is not the contract you traded
  • Why the micro contracts changed which strategies are available to a small account

Part 1: Tick, Point, and the Account They Imply

Every contract has a point value — what a one-point move is worth — and a tick, the smallest increment it trades in. From those two and your stop, the minimum account falls out with no judgement required:

risk per contract  =  stop distance in points  x  point value
minimum account    =  risk per contract  /  your risk fraction

Worked across the contracts retail traders actually use, at a ten-point stop:

Contract Per point Per tick 10-pt stop Account at 1% at 2%
ES — E-mini S&P 500$50$12.50$500$50,000$25,000
NQ — E-mini Nasdaq$20$5.00$200$20,000$10,000
MES — Micro E-mini S&P$5$1.25$50$5,000$2,500
MNQ — Micro Nasdaq$2$0.50$20$2,000$1,000

💡 The row that matters most

A ten-point stop on ES needs fifty thousand dollars behind it to be a 1% risk. A trader with $10,000 who takes that trade is risking 5% on one position, whatever their plan said. The micro contract is not a lesser version of the same instrument — it is the same instrument at a tenth of the unit, and it is what makes the arithmetic work for an ordinary account.

Run the table with your stop, not a ten-point one. If your method uses a twenty-point stop on NQ, the account it demands doubles.

Part 2: Margin Is Not Risk

Your broker may let you open one ES contract for a few hundred dollars of day-trade margin. That figure is a performance bond: the deposit the clearing house wants while you hold the position. It is not a cap on your loss, and it is not related to your stop.

The contract controls a notional value in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Post $500 against that and you are running leverage that would be unavailable to you anywhere else in retail finance. The position can lose more than the margin, and if it does, the shortfall is a debt.

📊 What margin actually constrains

Margin decides how many contracts you may open. Your stop decides how much you lose per contract. Conflating them produces the classic failure: a trader sizes to what margin allows rather than to what the stop costs, and the account is gone in a single session that the strategy would have survived at correct size.

Day-trade margin also evaporates at a fixed time each afternoon. A position held past that point is subject to the full overnight requirement, and a broker that finds you short of it will close the position for you — the mechanism from Lesson 13, on a same-day clock.

Part 3: The Roll, and the Chart That Never Existed

Futures expire. Index contracts run on a quarterly cycle, and volume migrates from the front contract to the next one over a few days near expiry. To stay in the market you close one and open the other. That is the roll, and it happens about four times a year.

Two consequences, one obvious and one not:

  • It costs. Each roll is a round trip: a spread out and a spread in, on both contracts. Four rolls a year is eight crossings. Small per event, and it is a fixed annual charge on being in the market at all, so it belongs in the friction arithmetic from Lesson 23.
  • Your chart is a construction. The continuous futures chart you backtest on is several contracts stitched together, and the prices at each join have been adjusted so the series does not jump. That means the historical prices on your chart are not prices anything ever traded at. Levels drawn across a roll boundary refer to a number that never existed. Lesson 36 develops the backtesting consequence; the practical rule here is simply not to draw support and resistance through a join.
Part 4: Where Signal Pilot Fits

The arithmetic in Parts 1 and 2 is yours and needs no tool. Where the indicators earn their place is the roll period, which is genuinely hard to read: for several days two contracts are live and the volume is split between them.

  • Volume Oracle shows where the participation actually is. During a roll that answers the only question that matters — which contract is the real market today — and it answers it from the tape rather than from a calendar rule.
  • Janus Atlas marks structure on the contract you are actually trading, which matters because structure carried over from the expiring contract is drawn on prices that no longer apply.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: Your method uses a 12-point stop on NQ, whose point value is $20. You want to risk 1% per trade. What account does one contract require?

A) Whatever the broker's day-trade margin is, typically a few hundred dollars
B) $24,000 — the stop risks $240, and $240 is 1% of $24,000
C) $12,000 — twelve points at $20 a point, times fifty
D) It cannot be determined without knowing the entry price

Correct: B. 12 × $20 = $240 of risk, and $240 ÷ 0.01 = $24,000. A is the answer the platform encourages and it is the central error of this lesson: margin is the deposit to open, not the loss to survive. D is worth rejecting because the entry price genuinely does not enter the calculation — only the stop distance does, which is why you can run this before you have a trade.

Q2: You have $8,000 and your method needs a 10-point stop on the S&P. What does the arithmetic support?

A) One ES contract, since margin allows it
B) One MES contract at roughly 0.6% risk, or two at 1.2%
C) One ES contract with the stop tightened to 3 points
D) Nothing — $8,000 is below the futures minimum

Correct: B. MES risks $50 on that stop, which is 0.6% of $8,000. A is 6.25% risk on one trade regardless of what the margin permits. C is the seductive one and it is worse than it looks: a three-point stop on the S&P sits inside ordinary noise, so it converts a sizing problem into a strategy that loses on trades it would have won. The micro contract exists precisely so that D is false.

Q3: Why should you avoid drawing a support level across a roll boundary on a continuous futures chart?

A) Because volume is lower during the roll, so the level is less reliable
B) Because the historical side has been price-adjusted, so it refers to a price nothing traded at
C) Because the contract specification changes at each expiry
D) Because exchanges reset the order book at expiry

Correct: B. A continuous chart splices contracts and shifts the older data so the series is smooth, which means older prices are offset from what actually printed. A level drawn across that join is anchored to a number no participant ever transacted at, so no one remembers it and nothing defends it. A is true but minor and is not the reason. C is false — the specification is identical across expiries, which is what makes the splice possible in the first place.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Minimum account = stop in points x point value, divided by your risk fraction. A 10-point ES stop needs $50,000 to be a 1% risk; the same trade on MES needs $5,000.
  • Margin is a deposit, not a loss limit. It constrains how many contracts you may open and says nothing about what one contract can cost you.
  • Sizing to margin instead of to the stop is the standard way a futures account ends. The strategy is usually fine; the unit was too big for the account.
  • Micro contracts are a tenth of the unit, and they are what make correct sizing available to an ordinary account rather than a compromise.
  • The roll is a fixed annual cost — about four round trips a year — and belongs in the friction ratio from Lesson 23.
  • A continuous chart's history is adjusted, not real. Do not draw levels across a roll boundary; nothing traded there.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Size One Contract Honestly

Objective: Establish, before your next futures trade, whether your account can hold a single contract at the stop your method actually uses.

Part 1: Your real stop. From your last twenty futures trades, record the stop distance in points you actually used — not the one in the plan. Take the median.

Part 2: The account it implies. Multiply by the point value, then divide by the risk fraction you claim to run. Compare with your actual balance. If the implied account is larger than yours, you have been running above your stated risk on every one of those trades, and you can now say by how much.

Part 3: The micro alternative. Repeat with the micro version. In most cases this is where the arithmetic starts working, and the only thing you give up is the size you could not afford anyway.

Part 4: Cost the roll. Estimate your round-trip cost on one contract, multiply by four, and express it as a percentage of your account. That is your annual fee for being in this market at all, and it belongs next to the friction ratio you built last lesson.
