What Should You Actually Trade?

Reading time ~14 min • Instrument Selection •

Eighty-odd lessons on how to trade, and none so far on what to trade. That is the wrong order, and this lesson fixes it — but it had to come after the last one, because you cannot choose an instrument until you can price what it costs you to be in it.

Most people never make this choice at all. They trade whatever they first heard about, or whatever moves most, and then spend years trying to make a method work on a market that was never going to pay for it. The choice is worth more than the method, and unlike the method it can be settled in an evening with a spreadsheet.

💸 The number that decides it

Every instrument has a daily range — the opportunity — and a round-trip cost. What matters is not either one but the ratio. On a mega-cap stock your costs eat about 1% of the average daily range. On a $1.50 micro-cap they eat 73%. Identical strategy, identical skill: one is a business and the other is arithmetically impossible.

🚨 Real Talk

“Trade what moves” is the single most expensive piece of advice a beginner receives. The instruments that move most are almost always the ones that charge most to enter and leave, and the charge scales with the movement. Volatility is not opportunity; volatility minus friction is opportunity, and the second term is the one nobody measures.

🎯 What You’ll Master

By the end of this lesson, you’ll understand:

  • How to compute friction as a share of the daily range, and rank any set of instruments by it in one spreadsheet
  • Why the cheapest instrument is not automatically the right one, and what the second filter is
  • How much capital an instrument actually demands before your stop can sit where the chart says
  • Why trading hours are a strategy constraint rather than a lifestyle preference

Part 1: Friction as a Share of the Opportunity

An instrument offers you a daily range and charges you a round trip. Write the range in basis points — one basis point is 0.01% — so that instruments of different prices are comparable, and the whole question reduces to one fraction:

friction ratio  =  round-trip cost (bps)  /  average daily range (bps)

round-trip cost =  spread x 2  +  commission x 2   (+ impact, if your size warrants it)
average daily range = mean of (high - low) / close, over the last 60 sessions

That fraction is the share of a typical day’s entire movement that you hand over simply for the right to participate. Here it is across the range of what retail traders actually choose:

Instrument Spread (bps) Daily range (bps) Round trip Share of the range
Mega-cap tech1.01502.01.3%
S&P 500 ETF1.7903.43.8%
Mid-cap, $40 stock12.52502510.0%
Small cap, $5 stock10050020040.0%
Micro-cap, $1.50 stock33090066073.3%

Spreads and ranges are representative order-of-magnitude figures for each category, not quotes for any particular security. The point of the exercise is that you fill this table in with your own instruments, from your own broker's quotes, which takes about twenty minutes.

💡 What the bottom row means

At a friction ratio of 73%, you must be right about roughly three quarters of the day’s entire movement before you have covered the cost of showing up. No edge in this curriculum survives that. The micro-cap is not a harder version of the same game — it is a different game, and the house edge in it is enormous.

And notice the trap it is built on. The micro-cap has a 900 basis point daily range, six times the mega-cap. It genuinely does move more. It is the instrument that looks most attractive on a chart and is worst on the arithmetic, and those two facts have the same cause.

Part 2: The Second Filter — Can Your Method Even Run Here?

Lowest friction does not settle it, or everyone would trade one index ETF and stop. The second question is whether the instrument produces the behaviour your method needs, and that is a different property.

  • A mean-reversion method needs an instrument that overshoots and comes back. Index products do this; a single stock in a news cycle does not, because the move is information rather than imbalance.
  • A liquidity-sweep method needs visible stop clusters and enough participants to run them. That requires a crowded, well-charted instrument — the opposite of an obscure one.
  • A trend method needs an instrument capable of sustained direction, which usually means one attached to a slow-moving macro force rather than to a single company’s news flow.

So the choice is a conjunction, not a ranking: lowest friction among the instruments that actually exhibit the behaviour you trade. Rank on Part 1, then eliminate anything that fails this test, and the field is usually down to two or three.

Part 3: What the Instrument Demands in Capital

The third constraint is the one that silently forces bad sizing. Every instrument has a smallest sensible increment — one share, one contract — and a stop distance the chart, not you, decides. Together they set a minimum account:

minimum account  =  (stop distance x value per point x smallest size)  /  your risk fraction

A $600 index ETF with a $6 stop risks $6 per share; at 1% risk a 100-share lot needs a $60,000 account, though you can hold fewer shares. A futures contract cannot be subdivided at all, which is why the next lesson is devoted to it. The failure mode is always the same: a trader whose account cannot fund one unit at a sensible stop moves the stop rather than the instrument, and then reports that the method does not work.

📊 The fourth constraint, briefly: hours

An instrument trades when it trades. If the volume in your market concentrates in a two-hour window you cannot be at a screen for, then whatever you are doing is not that method — it is a different, thinner method that happens to use the same rules. Lesson 19 covers session structure; the point here is only that hours are a hard filter, applied before the others, and no amount of discipline substitutes for being awake.

Part 4: Where Signal Pilot Fits

The friction ratio comes from your broker’s quotes and sixty days of highs and lows — no indicator required, and any tool claiming to rank instruments for you is hiding the assumption that matters. What the tools help with is the second filter, which is the genuinely hard one.

  • Harmonic Oscillator classifies an instrument’s current regime as trending or mean-reverting. Run it across your candidate list and you can see which of them behave the way your method needs, rather than assuming it.
  • Volume Oracle shows whether the order flow is thick enough for the structure you rely on to form. A setup that needs absorption cannot appear where there is nothing to absorb.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: Instrument A has a 90 bps daily range and a 1.7 bps spread. Instrument B has a 500 bps daily range and a 100 bps spread. Which is the better candidate, and why?

A) B — it moves five times as much, so there is more to capture
B) A — friction is 3.8% of its range against 40% for B
C) B — the wider spread is compensated by the wider range
D) They are equivalent, since cost and range both scale up

Correct: B. Round trips are 3.4 bps and 200 bps; against their ranges that is 3.8% and 40%. D is the interesting wrong answer because it sounds like a principle — costs and ranges do both rise as you go down the liquidity scale, but they do not rise together. Spreads widen faster than ranges, which is exactly why the ratio is the thing to rank on and neither number alone will do.

Q2: Your friction table says the S&P 500 ETF is your cheapest instrument. Does that make it the right one to trade?

A) Yes — lowest friction is the whole point of the exercise
B) Only if it also produces the behaviour your method needs, and trades when you can
C) No — index products are too efficient for retail to have an edge in
D) Yes, provided your account can fund one share

Correct: B. Friction ranks candidates; it does not choose among them. An instrument you cannot trade during its active hours, or that never exhibits the behaviour your method exploits, is not a candidate at any price. C is worth rejecting explicitly: efficiency is not the same as impossibility, and the friction table is precisely what tells you an efficient instrument leaves you 96% of its range to work with rather than 27%.

Q3: You want to trade an instrument whose sensible stop is $6 per share, at 1% account risk. Your account is $12,000. What does the arithmetic say?

A) Trade it with a $2 stop instead, to fit the account
B) Twenty shares is the whole position — if that is too small to be worth doing, the instrument is wrong for this account
C) Raise risk to 3% so the position is meaningful
D) Use margin to reach a normal position size

Correct: B. 1% of $12,000 is $120, and $120 ÷ $6 is twenty shares. That is the honest answer, and if twenty shares feels pointless the conclusion is about the instrument, not about the sizing. A and C are the two standard escapes and both convert a capital problem into a risk problem: the tighter stop gets hit by noise the chart always contained, and the higher risk changes the drawdown distribution rather than the edge. D adds the machinery from Lesson 13 to a position that was already too large.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Rank instruments by friction as a share of their daily range, not by spread and not by volatility. Round-trip cost divided by average daily range, in basis points, is the whole calculation.
  • The instruments that move most usually charge most, and charge disproportionately. A mega-cap gives up about 1% of its range to friction; a $1.50 micro-cap gives up 73%.
  • Cheapest is a ranking, not a decision. The instrument must also produce the behaviour your method exploits — mean reversion, sweeps, sustained trend — or the low friction buys you nothing.
  • Every instrument has a minimum account, set by its stop distance and its smallest tradeable unit. Below that, traders shrink the stop instead of changing instrument, and then blame the method.
  • Trading hours are a hard filter applied first. A method run outside its instrument's active window is a different and thinner method.
  • This choice outranks your entry rules. It is settled in one evening and it constrains every trade you will take for years.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Rank Your Own Candidates

Objective: Turn “what should I trade” from a preference into a ranked list you produced from data. One spreadsheet, one evening.

Part 1: Build the list. Write down every instrument you have traded in the last year, plus any you have been tempted by. Aim for eight to twelve.

Part 2: Two columns. For each one, record the typical bid-ask spread in basis points during the hours you would actually trade — watch it live, do not take it from a summary page — and the average daily range in basis points over the last sixty sessions. Both are free from any charting platform.

Part 3: Rank. Compute round-trip cost divided by daily range for each. Sort ascending. Draw a line at 15%: below it you have something workable, above it you are fighting the instrument as well as the market.

Part 4: Apply the second filter. For each survivor, write one sentence on whether it produces what your method needs, and one on whether it is active when you are. Anything failing either is out regardless of its ranking.

Part 5: Compare with what you actually trade. Where does your current instrument sit on your own list? For a lot of readers this is the uncomfortable part of the exercise, and it is the reason the exercise is worth doing.

You now have a way to choose an instrument rather than inherit one. The next lesson takes the single instrument class where that choice is most often made blind — futures — and works through the mechanics that make its capital requirement different in kind from everything above.
