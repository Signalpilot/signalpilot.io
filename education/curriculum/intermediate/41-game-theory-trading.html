Trading isn't you vs the market. It's you vs every other trader trying to take your money.

Game theory is the mathematics of strategic decision-making when your outcome depends on others' decisions. In trading, most of what you make on a short horizon is made at another trader's expense. That is worth stating precisely, because the loose version is wrong. Equities as a whole are not zero-sum: shareholders as a class are paid by the companies they own, which is why an index has gone up over a century while nobody had to lose for it to. Futures are zero-sum before costs and negative-sum after them, because the exchange, the broker and the spread are paid before anybody's profit is. What is close to zero-sum is precisely the thing this lesson is about — short-horizon trading against other short-horizon traders — and there game theory is the right tool, because it explains why stop clusters get run, why the obvious level is the dangerous one, and why a strategy that only works while nobody knows you use it is not a strategy.

Part 1: Nash Equilibrium in Markets

Nash Equilibrium: A stable state where no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy unilaterally (without others changing).

Named after: John Nash (mathematician, Nobel Prize in Economics 1994). He proved that every finite game has at least one equilibrium — though possibly only in mixed strategies, meaning the equilibrium play is a randomised mix rather than a single fixed choice. Hold on to that qualifier: it is exactly why the randomised behaviour in Part 3 is an equilibrium rather than a trick.

Market Auction as a Game

Players:

  • Buyers: Want lowest price possible (maximize value for money)
  • Sellers: Want highest price possible (maximize profit)
  • Market makers: Want widest spread possible while providing liquidity (profit from bid-ask spread)
  • Arbitrageurs: Want to exploit price differences across venues/instruments

Nash Equilibrium = Current Market Price

At any moment, the current price represents equilibrium because:

  • Buyers willing to pay MORE have already bought (removed demand from market)
  • Sellers willing to accept LESS have already sold (removed supply from market)
  • Remaining buyers/sellers are waiting for better prices (holding out for more favorable equilibrium)
  • No single player can profitably change their strategy without new information

What breaks equilibrium? New information (news, earnings, macro data, order flow signals) changes players' valuations → new equilibrium sought → price moves to find new balance.

Applying Nash Equilibrium to Trading

Example: Support Level Game

Scenario: SPY at $450 (daily support level, previously held 3 times)

Player 1 (Retail trader - 10,000 players):

  • Strategy A: Buy at $450.00, stop at $449.50 (textbook support trading)
  • Strategy B: Wait for sweep to $449.20, then buy (strategic, expects stop hunt)

Player 2 (Institution - 5 large players):

  • Strategy A: Buy at $450.00 (compete with retail for liquidity)
  • Strategy B: Sweep to $449.20, grab stops, THEN buy (optimal - more liquidity + better price)

Payoff Matrix:

Retail Strategy Institution Strategy Retail Outcome Institution Outcome
A (Buy at $450) A (Buy at $450) +1 (compete for fills) +1 (compete for fills)
A (Buy at $450) B (Sweep stops) -5 (stopped out, miss rally) +8 (grab liquidity + better entry)
B (Wait for sweep) A (Buy at $450) 0 (wait, no entry) +1 (buy without competition)
B (Wait for sweep) B (Sweep stops) +6 (enter at $449.20, survive sweep) +3 (sweep but retail adapted)

Nash Equilibrium:

  • Institution chooses Strategy B (sweep stops = dominant strategy, always optimal regardless of retail choice)
  • Retail SHOULD choose Strategy B (wait for sweep) to maximize outcome
  • Reality: retail overwhelmingly chooses Strategy A, and its predictability is the edge institutions are pricing

Lesson: once you accept that the level will probably be traded through before it holds, the equilibrium play is to wait for that rather than to stand in front of it. Two things about the table, though. Its payoffs are illustrative ranks — they say B beats A, not by how much — and no win rate is attached to either strategy, because none has been measured. What the matrix earns you is the ordering, and the ordering is the part you can act on.

Worked Example: A Support Sweep in Index Futures

🔍 Setup:

  • Level: ES (S&P 500 futures) at 5250 (strong daily support, held 4 times in past 2 weeks)
  • Time: 2:00 AM ET — a thin session, which matters only because it takes less size to move price when the book is thin
  • What you can actually see: the level has held four times, so anyone long from those tests has a stop somewhere below it. You cannot see how many, or exactly where — nobody can, because a stop is not in the book until it triggers — but 5245-5248 is where the textbook puts them, and that is enough to plan around.
  • What a large buyer needs: to be filled where supply is, not where it is scarce. Below the level, not at it.

📉 Price Action:

  • 2:05 AM: Price at 5250, holding steady
  • 2:10 AM: Sudden 15-point sell pressure → 5250 to 5235 in 3 minutes (swept all retail stops 5245-5248)
  • 2:13 AM: Immediate reversal → 5235 to 5260 in 8 minutes (+25 points from sweep low)
  • 2:30 AM: Price stabilizes at 5258 (back above original support)

📊 Outcomes:

  • Retail (Strategy A - stop at 5245): Stopped out at 5245, watched price rally +13 points without them. Loss: -5 points ($250 per contract)
  • Retail (Strategy B - waited for sweep): Entered long at 5238 during sweep, exited at 5258. Profit: +20 points ($1,000 per contract)
  • The large buyer: filled between 5235 and 5240 instead of at 5250, then held while price came back to 5258. No total is given here: multiplying a per-contract figure by a contract count nobody can observe produces a headline, not a result.

✅ What this actually shows: nobody had to hunt anybody. Two facts do all the work. Stops cluster below an obvious level, because that is where every course tells you to put them. And a buyer who needs size wants to be filled where the supply is, which is exactly there. Put those together and the level gets traded through before it holds, with no coordination at all. That is the version to plan around — and it is worth being precise about the alternative, because the lesson used to blur them: deliberately pressing price with the intent of triggering stops rather than trading at those prices is manipulation, it is prosecuted, and treating it as the routine explanation will train you to look for a conspiracy where a resting order book is enough. The advantage in the fill is real either way: 5235 against 5250 is 15 points, which on ES is $750 a contract. The numbers above are constructed to make the mechanic legible, which is also why no date is attached to them; a date would make this a claim about a particular session rather than an illustration.

Part 2: Prisoner's Dilemma in Liquidity Provision

Prisoner's Dilemma: Two players can cooperate for mutual benefit, but rational self-interest leads to suboptimal outcomes for both.

Classic setup: Two prisoners interrogated separately. If both stay silent (cooperate), both get 1 year. If one confesses (defects) and other stays silent, confessor goes free, silent one gets 10 years. If both confess, both get 5 years. Rational choice: Both confess (Nash equilibrium), even though mutual silence is better for both.

One correction before we use it: stop placement is not a prisoner's dilemma, and that is worth saying plainly in a lesson about game theory. A prisoner's dilemma needs both players to have a cooperative option they jointly prefer and individually abandon. You and a large buyer have no such option — there is nothing for the two of you to cooperate on. What you have is a matrix game against an opponent who does not know you specifically but does know what traders like you typically do, which is a different structure and, for your purposes, a more useful one. The genuine prisoner's-dilemma shape in markets is liquidity provision, and the quiz comes back to it.

Payoff Matrix for Stop Placement

Your Strategy vs Institution's Strategy

Scenario: You're long AAPL at $180.00, support at $179.00

Your Stop Institution Action Price Path Your Outcome
$178.50 (textbook: $0.50 below support) Sweep to $178.20 $180 → $178.20 → $183 ❌ Stopped at $178.50 (-$1.50), missed +$3 rally
Net: -$1.50 per share

Net: +$3.00 per share
$177.00 (wider) Real breakdown (no sweep) $180 → $175 (legit breakdown) ❌ Stopped at $177 (-$3.00) vs -$1.50 if stopped at $178.50
Net: -$3.00 per share (extra -$1.50 loss from wider stop)
$178.50 (tight) Real breakdown (no sweep) $180 → $175 ✓ Stopped at $178.50 (-$1.50), avoided further -$3.50 drop to $175
Net: -$1.50 per share (protected from -$5 total loss)

Optimal strategy: stop wide enough to survive a run through the level, but not so wide that a genuine breakdown hurts badly. No frequency is quoted for how often reversals are preceded by one, because the answer depends entirely on the instrument, the session and where you draw the line on what counts — and every number in circulation was produced by someone who chose that line after looking at the data. Use ATR-based stops (1.5-2x ATR beyond structure) + reduce position size to compensate for wider stop.

Math: If ATR = $2.00, stop 1.5×ATR = $3.00 below entry. If normal position size = 100 shares, reduce to 50 shares. Same dollar risk ($300), but 50% fewer stop-outs from sweeps.

Game Theory Stop Placement Framework

Level 1 (Retail thinking): "Stop below support at $178.50 (textbook says $0.50 below support)"

Issue: predictable — this is the logic every retail course teaches, which makes it an obvious sweep target

Level 2 (Aware of sweeps): "Stop below likely sweep zone at $178.00 (account for $0.50 sweep below retail stops)"

Issue: Still anchored to support level, not robust if market structure changes

Level 3 (Game theory - optimal): "Stop based on thesis invalidation + ATR buffer, regardless of support levels"

Why it works: Logic-based (not pattern-based), adapts to volatility, less predictable to institutions

Level 3 Example (TSLA long trade):

  • Entry: $245.00 (daily order block mitigation at $243-$247)
  • Thesis: Order block mitigation (institutions accumulated $243-$247 previously, expect support)
  • Invalidation: If price closes BELOW $243, thesis invalid (order block broke, no longer support)
  • ATR (20-day): $8.50
  • Buffer: 1.5×ATR = $12.75
  • Stop calculation: $243 (invalidation) - $12.75 (buffer) = $230.25
  • Stop: $230.00 (rounded for simplicity)

Why this works: Stop is based on LOGIC (thesis invalidation + volatility buffer), not arbitrary support levels. If TSLA sweeps to $240 (above $230 stop), thesis still valid (order block holding). If TSLA breaks $230, thesis truly invalid (order block failed + exceeded normal volatility).

💸 Composite Example: Sarah and a $17,250 stop-hunt lesson

Sarah Kim (composite example)

March-May 2024: Sarah was stopped out 23 times in 45 trades (49% win rate). Pattern: She bought ES futures at support ($5200, $5180, $5250) with stops $10-20 below. Every time, price swept $15-30 below support, hit her stop, then rallied +40-60 points without her.

Cumulative losses from stops: 23 stops × $750 average = $17,250 in false stop-outs over 3 months.

June 2024: Sarah studied game theory. Realized: "Institutions KNOW where my stops are. They're playing a game, and I'm the predictable opponent."

New strategy (game theory-based):

  • Stop placement: Thesis invalidation + 1.5×ATR (not support-based)
  • Entry timing: Wait for sweep confirmation, then enter with institutions
  • Position size: Reduced 50% to compensate for wider stops (same risk)

What changed: her stop stopped being a function of the support level and became a function of what would make her wrong. Over the following weeks she sat through runs below the level that would previously have taken her out. No result is reported for that stretch: two months of trades cannot produce a rate, and a figure attached to it would be describing those particular weeks rather than the change she made.

The part of her three months worth keeping is not the loss total. It is that all 23 stop-outs shared one feature: the stop had been placed by reading the chart rather than by asking what would make the trade wrong. That is a repeatable mistake, which is the only kind that has a repeatable fix.

🚨 Real Talk

When you place a stop below support, you are placing it where thousands of other people placed theirs. Nobody knows where your particular stop is, and nobody needs to. What is visible is the pocket, and a pocket of resting sell orders is worth trading into whether or not anybody set out to take yours.

The structural reality: retail stops cluster at the obvious technical levels — round numbers, recent lows, moving averages — because that is what every chart and every course points at. Price runs through those levels often enough that you should plan for it. No percentage is given, for the reason above. And the mechanism does not require anyone to be hunting you personally: the orders are sitting there, they are sell orders, and a buyer with size to fill goes where the sell orders are.

Part 3: How Institutions Use Game Theory

Strategy #1: The Dominant Action — Fill Where The Supply Is

Insight: trading into a stop cluster rather than at the level is the better of the two available actions when all three of these hold. Note the word "better" rather than "free" — pushing price down through the pocket means selling into a thin book first, and that selling costs something that the improved fill has to earn back:

  • Condition 1: Liquidity gained from stops > cost of moving price temporarily
  • Condition 2: The reversal has to be likely enough to cover the cost of getting there. Not "more than half the time" — run the arithmetic below and the breakeven on those inputs is about 37.5%, because the worst case still leaves a better fill than buying at the level. Where your own breakeven sits depends on your own numbers, and it is the only version of this condition worth using
  • Condition 3: Retail stop clustering is high (many stops at same level = large liquidity pool)

Payoff analysis:

  • Best case: filled at 5235, price reverses to 5260 = +25 points, which on ES is $1,250 a contract. No total is given: the contract count is unobservable, and multiplying by it turns a per-unit figure into a headline.
  • Worst case: Price continues through sweep to $5220 (-15 points from sweep entry), but institution got better entry than not sweeping (would've bought at $5250, so sweep saved them 15 points = -$750 per contract vs -$1,500 without sweep)
  • Expected value: assume — purely as an input, to see how the arithmetic behaves — a 65% chance of reversal. EV = 0.65×$1,250 + 0.35×(-$750) = $812.50 - $262.50 = $550 a contract. The 65% is not a measured figure; it is the number you should replace with your own before any of this means anything. Run it at 50% and the edge is $250. At 40% it is $50. Below 37.5% it is gone.

Retail counter: Assume sweeps will happen at obvious levels (support/resistance, round numbers, previous day's low/high). Position size smaller, stops wider (1.5-2×ATR), or wait for sweep before entering.

Strategy #2: Mixed Strategy (Randomize Timing to Prevent Adaptation)

Insight: Here is where most treatments of this go wrong, including the earlier version of this page. The story usually told is that institutions deliberately vary when they hunt stops so that retail cannot adapt — which requires a single coordinating actor deciding a schedule, exactly the thing Part 1 spent a page explaining you do not need. The randomness is real; the coordinator is not. It comes from the structure: different firms with different mandates, different sizes to fill, on different clocks, and no reason to consult each other. Sometimes several want the same level on the same night and the run through it is violent. Sometimes nobody does and the level simply holds. That produces a mixed strategy nobody chose — and, as Part 1 noted about Nash, a mixed strategy is a perfectly good equilibrium.

Randomization examples:

  • Timing varies because whoever needs the fill is on their own clock — a London desk, a US afternoon rebalance, an Asia-session hedge. No two of them are waiting for the same hour
  • Depth varies because it is set by how much has to be filled against how thin the book is, and both change every session
  • Frequency varies, and no percentage is given for it. Every figure you have seen quoted here — 70%, 80%, whatever — was produced by someone who chose after the fact what counted as a sweep. Measure it on the one instrument you actually trade, with your own definition written down first, or do not use a number at all

Game theory principle: A mixed strategy cannot be exploited the way a fixed one can, and that holds whether the mixing is chosen or merely emergent. The practical consequence is the same either way, and it is the useful half: you cannot forecast when the level gets run, so stop trying. Build for the fact that it can happen on any given approach.

Retail counter: Don't predict WHEN or IF sweeps happen. Just assume they CAN happen and protect accordingly (wider stops, smaller size, limit orders in sweep zones).

Strategy #3: Exploit Coordination Failure

Insight: the clustering needs no conspiracy — and the version of this claim you will meet elsewhere is wrong twice over. Retail traders agreeing among themselves where to put stops would not be illegal; it is simply impossible to arrange, because there are millions of them and no channel reaches them all. And institutions do not need to coordinate either. Everybody independently reading the same chart produces the clustering a cartel would have had to organise. Coordination failure is the right idea. The illegality and the cartel are not part of it.

Example:

  • Retail: 10,000 traders all independently analyze SPY support at $450 → all place stops at $449.50 (same textbook logic, no coordination)
  • If retail could coordinate: "Let's all place stops at different levels ($449.50, $448.80, $447.20, $445.00) to avoid clustering and prevent institutions from targeting us"
  • Reality: retail does not coordinate, and does not need to in order to end up in the same place → the great majority put stops just under $449.50, because that is what the same textbook told all of them → the pocket exists, and anyone who wants to buy size trades into it. No share is quoted for how many; the clustering is visible in how price behaves at the level, not in a statistic anybody publishes.

How a large participant infers where the liquidity sits — no coordination required:

  • The visible book: resting limit orders are public. Stops are not, but the levels people defend with limits and the levels they abandon are, and they move together.
  • The level's own history: a level that has held four times has four cohorts of longs behind it, each with a stop below. That is inference from the chart everyone can see, not from anybody's client file.
  • Aggregate flow they legitimately see: a wholesaler that internalises a large share of retail orders knows the shape of retail demand in aggregate. Passing a specific client's order information to a trading desk is what information barriers exist to prevent, and dark pools are dark by construction — you cannot see other participants' resting orders in one, which is the entire point of using it.

Retail counter: Be contrarian. When everyone places stops in one spot (social media shows "everyone's stop is at $449.50"), place yours elsewhere (wider, based on ATR, or don't enter until after sweep). You can't coordinate with other retail traders, but you can avoid being part of the predictable herd.

Part 4: Building Robust Adversarial Strategies

Adversarial Thinking Framework

Core principle: Assume your opponent (institutions) knows your strategy and will exploit it. Design strategies that remain profitable even when opponent adapts.

Steps:

  1. Identify your strategy: "I buy support with stops below support"
  2. Opponent's counter: "I'll sweep stops below support, trigger your stop, then price rallies"
  3. Your counter-counter: "I'll wait for sweep, enter after stops are grabbed"
  4. Opponent's counter-counter-counter: "I'll randomize sweeps (sometimes sweep, sometimes don't) so you can't predict"
  5. Robust strategy: "I'll use limit orders in sweep zones + stop based on thesis invalidation (not support levels) + reduce size to compensate for wider stops"

Result: Your strategy works whether opponent sweeps or not (limit orders in sweep zone = better entry if sweep happens, stop based on thesis invalidation = protected if no sweep and real breakdown occurs).

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1: Nash Equilibrium Analysis

Scenario: NQ (Nasdaq futures) at 18,500 (major support, tested 5 times). You're considering going long. You know:

  • Assume 15,000 long contracts with stops at 18,480-18,490. Take that as a premise of the exercise, not as something you could look up: stops are not in the book until they trigger, as Part 1 said. What a real participant infers is that a level tested five times has five cohorts of longs behind it, and that their stops are in the band the textbook points at
  • Current time: 2:30 AM ET (low liquidity)
  • Institutions need to accumulate 5,000 contracts

Question: What's the Nash equilibrium strategy for institutions? What's your optimal response?

Show Analysis

Answer: Institutions' Nash equilibrium = Sweep stops (dominant strategy). Sweeping to 18,475 gives them 15,000 contracts of liquidity at better price than buying at 18,500. Even when the reversal fails — and the exercise gives you no frequency for that, so do not assume one — they are filled better than they would have been at 18,500.

Your optimal response: Wait for sweep. Don't enter at 18,500. Place limit buy order at 18,478 (in sweep zone). If sweep happens → you enter with institutions at better price. If no sweep → you don't enter (re-evaluate thesis). Stop: 18,450 (thesis invalidation = below support - 1.5×ATR).

Exercise 2: Mixed Strategy Defense

Scenario: On your instrument, some approaches to support run through it first and some do not, and you cannot tell in advance which this one is. Assume nothing about the split — that is the point of the exercise. Support sits at $100.00 and the band below it where resting orders would be is roughly $99.20-99.60.

Question: How do you design a strategy that profits in both scenarios?

Show Analysis

Answer: Use split position sizing:

  • Half the position: Limit buy AT support ($100.00). This is the half that gets filled when the level simply holds.
  • Half the position: Limit buy in the band below ($99.20-99.60). This is the half that gets filled when it is traded through first.
  • Stop: $98.50 (below the band, thesis invalidation). Applies to both halves.

Expected outcome: Be precise about what a split buys and what it costs, because "you always catch the move" is not true and is the sentence that used to be here. If the level holds, only the first half fills and you are in at $100.00 with half the size you wanted. If it is traded through, both halves fill and your average is 0.5×$100.00 + 0.5×$99.40 = $99.70. So a split does not give you the best of both cases; it gives you a smaller position in the good case in exchange for never missing the move entirely. Whether that trade is worth making is a question about your own tolerance for missing entries, and the honest answer is that it depends on how often the level is run on your instrument — which is the thing the scenario deliberately refused to tell you, because in live trading nobody tells you either. Split 50/50 until you have measured it yourself.

Exercise 3: Adversarial Stop Placement

Scenario: Long NVDA at $875, daily support at $870. ATR (20-day) = $15.00. Institutions know retail places stops at $869 (just below support).

Question: Where should you place your stop using game theory principles?

Show Analysis

Answer: Don't anchor to support level ($870). Use thesis invalidation + ATR buffer:

  • Thesis: Daily support at $870 (previous accumulation zone)
  • Invalidation: If price closes below $870, support broken → thesis invalid
  • ATR buffer: 1.5×ATR = 1.5×$15 = $22.50
  • Stop: $870 - $22.50 = $847.50

Rationale: Institutions will sweep to ~$867 (grabbing retail stops at $869). Your stop at $847.50 survives sweep. If NVDA breaks through $847.50, thesis truly invalid (support broken + exceeded normal volatility = real breakdown, not sweep).

Position sizing: Stop is $27.50 away (vs retail's $6 stop). Reduce position size by 4.5× to maintain same dollar risk. If retail trades 100 shares with $6 stop ($600 risk), you trade 22 shares with $27.50 stop ($605 risk). Same risk, and the stop now sits outside the band where the resting orders are. No improvement figure is quoted for that, because it depends entirely on how often the instrument you actually trade runs that band.

Don't overwhelm yourself. Start with these 3 actions:

  1. Wait for Stop Sweeps Before Entering — Institutions sweep obvious retail stops $5-20 below support/above resistance before reversals. Sarah Kim lost $17,250 in 3 months (23 false stop-outs, 49% win rate) buying ES at support with stops $10-20 below—price swept $15-30 past her stops, then rallied +40-60 points without her.   3. Practice "Level 3 Thinking" Before Every Trade — Level 1: What will price do? (technical analysis). Level 2: What will retail do? (where are stops/entries?). Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: What is a dominant strategy, and why is sweeping stops a dominant strategy for institutions?

Show Answer

Answer: A dominant strategy is one that yields the best outcome regardless of what opponents do. Sweeping stops is dominant for institutions because: (1) Best case: Grab liquidity + price reverses = huge profit, (2) Worst case: Price continues through, but they got better entry than not sweeping = less loss. Since sweeping is optimal in both scenarios, it's a dominant strategy (always execute it).

Q2: Explain the prisoner's dilemma in market liquidity provision. Why do market makers exist?

Show Answer

Answer: The shape is a free-rider problem — the many-player generalisation of the prisoner's dilemma — rather than the two-prisoner version. Posting a limit order provides liquidity to everybody, but it carries adverse selection: the orders that trade against yours are disproportionately the ones that turn out to have been right. Taking liquidity with a market order avoids that risk by handing it to whoever posted. If everyone reasons that way, nobody posts and the spread widens for all. Market makers do not solve this by promising to quote regardless — they widen and step back when the risk rises, which is exactly why spreads blow out in a panic. They solve it by being paid to carry the risk, through the spread and through exchange rebates, and by turning inventory fast enough that being picked off sometimes still leaves them ahead. On some venues a designated market maker also carries a quoting obligation, which puts a floor under how far back they can step — not a guarantee that the quote is good.

Q3: You're long at $200, support at $198. Where should you place your stop using game theory?

Show Answer

Answer: Don't place stop just below support ($197.50) - too predictable. Use thesis invalidation + ATR buffer. If daily ATR = $5, stop = $198 (invalidation) - 1.5×$5 (buffer) = $190.50. This survives sweeps (institutions typically sweep $1-2 below retail stops at $197.50, so ~$196-197 sweep zone). If price hits $190.50, thesis truly invalid (support broken + exceeded volatility).

Q4: Runs through a level happen at unpredictable times and depths. What actually produces that unpredictability — and why does the answer change what you should do about it?

Show Answer

Answer: Not a schedule anybody sets. Different firms, different mandates, different amounts to fill, different clocks, and no channel between them — so the timing and the depth come out varied without anyone choosing variety. That is a mixed strategy arrived at structurally rather than strategically, and it is unexploitable for the same reason a chosen one would be. Why the distinction changes what you do: if a coordinator were setting the schedule, the sensible response would be to work out the schedule. There is no schedule to work out. So the response is not prediction but construction — a stop placed where your thesis breaks rather than where the chart does, sized so that surviving a run through the level costs you no more than the tight version would have.

Q5: How can retail traders defend against stop hunts when they can't coordinate?

Show Answer

Answer: Be contrarian individually. Don't follow the herd (textbook stops just below support). Use game theory: (1) Stops based on thesis invalidation + ATR (not support levels), (2) Limit orders in sweep zones (enter with institutions), (3) Reduce position size to compensate for wider stops (same risk, fewer false stops), (4) Avoid entering at obvious levels (wait for sweep or don't enter).

  • ✓ Calculate sweep zone: $0.50-$2.00 below retail stop clusters (where institutions will sweep)
  • ✓ Stop placement: Use thesis invalidation + 1.5-2×ATR buffer (not support-based stops)
  • ✓ Entry strategy: Limit orders in sweep zone OR wait for sweep confirmation before entering
  • ✓ Position sizing: Reduce size if using wider stops (maintain same dollar risk)
  • ✓ Mixed strategy defense: Split position (30% at support, 70% in sweep zone) if uncertain
  • ✓ Avoid predictability: Don't use textbook levels (institutions exploit textbook traders)
  • ✓ Check timing: Low liquidity sessions (2-5 AM ET, lunch 12-1 PM) = higher sweep probability

Key Takeaways

  • Nash equilibrium = stable state where no player improves alone (current price = equilibrium)
  • Sweeping stops is dominant strategy for institutions (always optimal, execute 60-70% of time)
  • Prisoner's dilemma explains why liquidity provision fails (adverse selection risk) → market makers profit
  • Strategic stop placement = thesis invalidation + ATR buffer (not arbitrary support levels)
  • Assume sweeps WILL happen at obvious levels (support, resistance, round numbers)
  • Mixed strategies = the timing and depth of runs through a level vary because uncoordinated participants are on different clocks, not because anyone randomises on purpose — unexploitable either way, which is why the answer is construction rather than prediction
  • Coordination failure = retail can't coordinate, institutions can → retail clustering exploited
  • Robust strategy = works whether opponent sweeps or not (limit orders in sweep zone + thesis-based stops)

If you made it this far, you now think like a game theorist. You understand WHY markets behave the way they do—and how to design strategies that remain profitable even when institutions adapt and exploit predictable retail behavior.

Markets are adversarial. Think like your opponent, anticipate traps, avoid predictable stops. Game theory turns you from prey to predator.
