Almost every market order you place is filled by a wholesaler rather than on an exchange. They do not profit from every transaction — Part 2 is about the orders that cost them money, and the whole reason spreads exist is that some flow is informed. What is true, and is enough, is that they profit on average, and the average is paid out of the difference between what you got and what you could have got.

When you hit "Buy Market Order," someone is on the other side: Citadel, Virtu, Jane Street. These firms make billions by being the counterparty to retail trades—whether markets go up, down, or sideways.

🚨 The Harsh Reality

Citadel Securities made $7.5 billion in revenue in 2022. They don't make money by hoping stocks go up. They make money on every single trade they facilitate.

How? Spread capture, adverse selection management, inventory management — the word is management, and Part 3 is about hedging and quote skewing rather than anything improper — and Payment for Order Flow (PFOF).

Part 1: Spread Capture — The Core Business Model

Buy the Bid, Sell the Ask, Repeat

The market maker's core profit strategy is spread capture: buy at the bid price, sell at the ask price, pocket the difference.

Example:

  • Bid: $100.00 (price buyers are willing to pay)
  • Ask: $100.05 (price sellers are asking)
  • Spread: $0.05

How It Works:

  1. Citadel posts bid: $100.00 (willing to buy 10,000 shares)
  2. Citadel posts ask: $100.05 (willing to sell 10,000 shares)
  3. You place market sell order for 500 shares → filled at $100.00
  4. Another trader places market buy → filled at $100.05
  5. Citadel's profit: $25, in seconds (they carry the inventory only between the two fills)

Scale: a wholesaler of this size handles something like a quarter of all US equity share volume. But be careful with the arithmetic you will see everywhere, including in the earlier version of this paragraph: multiplying a full penny by every share gives about $6 billion a year from US equities alone, which is more than the firm reports across every asset class it trades. What is actually captured per share is a fraction of the spread, because much of it is given back as price improvement, and a further part is lost to the informed orders discussed in Part 2. The business is real and enormous. It is not "the whole spread, every time".

Why the Spread Exists

The spread compensates market makers for two risks:

  • Inventory risk: Holding shares for seconds/minutes before finding a counterparty
  • Adverse selection risk: Trading with informed traders who know something the MM doesn't

High Liquidity = Narrow Spreads

Example: AAPL (Apple)

  • Bid: $175.00, Ask: $175.01
  • Spread: $0.01 (0.006%)

Why so tight? Millions of shares traded per minute → easy to find counterparties. MMs compete on price.

MM Strategy: tiny margins, massive volume. Note that the penny spread is the theoretical maximum per share and not the realised capture — in a name this liquid, competing quotes and price improvement give much of it back, and adverse selection takes more.

Low Liquidity = Wide Spreads

Example: Small Cap Stock

  • Bid: $10.00, Ask: $10.50
  • Spread: $0.50 (5%)

Why so wide? Low volume → hard to find counterparties. High inventory risk → MMs may hold shares for hours/days.

⚠️ Retail Trap: Market Orders on Illiquid Stocks

You see a hot penny stock pumped on Reddit. You place a market buy for 1,000 shares. You get filled at the ask ($10.50), but the midpoint is $10.25. You just paid 2.4% above fair value.

Lesson: Always use limit orders on illiquid stocks.

Part 2: Adverse Selection

When Smart Money Trades, Market Makers Lose

Adverse selection happens when informed traders—hedge funds, prop traders, insiders—trade with market makers because they have information the MM doesn't.

Example:

  • Hedge fund knows Apple is about to beat earnings
  • They aggressively buy AAPL at the ask ($175.01) seconds before announcement
  • Market makers fill the orders (they're posting liquidity)
  • Earnings announced: AAPL jumps to $180
  • Market makers are now short AAPL at $175, bleeding losses

⚠️ Why MMs Hate Informed Flow

The spread they earned ($0.01) is wiped out by the adverse move ($5.00). They're picking up pennies in front of a steamroller.

Solution: MMs use algorithms to detect informed flow and widen spreads or pull liquidity when they sense danger.

How Market Makers Detect Informed Traders

Signal #1: Order Size

Retail traders buy 10-100 shares. Institutions buy 10,000-100,000 shares. When a 50,000 share market buy hits, the MM's algo:

  • Widens the spread (ask jumps from $100.05 → $100.10)
  • Fills part of the order at higher prices (slippage)
  • Hedges immediately (buys futures to offset risk)

Signal #2: Order Velocity

If buy orders spike suddenly, someone knows something. Example: AAPL normally trades 100,000 shares/minute. Suddenly, 500,000 shares bought in 10 seconds.

MM Response: Algo detects "toxicity" → pulls liquidity → reprices higher.

Signal #3: Time of Day

Retail traders sleep. Heavy trading at 6:00 AM = institutions reacting to overnight news.

MM Response: Spreads are wider in pre-market. Example: AAPL spread during market hours: $0.01. At 7:00 AM: $0.10 (10x wider).

Part 3: Inventory Management

Market Makers Don't Want Directional Risk

Market makers are not investors. They want to be delta-neutral—making money on the spread, not on price movements.

Problem: Order flow is random. Sometimes more buyers (MM goes short). Sometimes more sellers (MM goes long).

Solution: Market makers use inventory management algorithms to quickly offload unwanted positions.

How MMs Rebalance Inventory

When MM is Long (Too Much Inventory):

  • Tactic #1: Skew quotes (lower ask to encourage buying)
  • Tactic #2: Cross with dark pools (offload to institutions)
  • Tactic #3: Hedge with futures (short SPY futures to neutralize)

When MM is Short (Negative Inventory):

  • Tactic #1: Skew quotes (raise bid to encourage selling)
  • Tactic #2: Internalize with other retail orders (match internally)
  • Tactic #3: Buy from dark pools

The Dark Side of Market Making

Order-book manipulation is real, prosecuted, and worth understanding. Read this section with one correction in mind, though, because the previous version of this lesson got it wrong in a way that matters: the enforcement record for spoofing and layering is overwhelmingly against proprietary trading firms, individual traders and bank trading desks, not against the registered wholesalers that fill retail orders. Those firms are among the most heavily surveilled participants in the market, and an accusation that they routinely spoof is a serious one to make without evidence. What follows describes the techniques and who has actually been prosecuted for them.

Tactic #1: Spoofing

What it is: Placing large fake orders to create the illusion of supply or demand, then canceling before they execute.

Example:

  1. A trader posts a large sell order they do not intend to execute: 50,000 shares at $100.10
  2. Retail sees massive sell wall → thinks "resistance ahead"
  3. Retail sells their shares at $100.00
  4. They cancel the 50,000 share sell order before it can trade
  5. They buy the shares that were sold into $100.00
  6. Price recovers to $100.20
  7. Profit: bought at $100, sold later at $100.20

Legality: illegal, and prosecuted. Dodd-Frank made spoofing an explicit criminal offence in 2010, and there have been convictions and nine-figure settlements since — against bank metals and treasuries desks and against individual proprietary traders. What makes it hard is the intent requirement: cancelling an order is legal and normal, so the government has to show the order was entered with no intention that it trade, which usually means reconstructing a pattern across thousands of orders rather than pointing at one.

Tactic #2: Layering

What it is: Placing multiple orders on one side of the book to create fake pressure, then trading on the other side.

Example: a trader posts ten buy orders at $99.90, $99.85 and $99.80 that they do not intend to have filled, creating the appearance of support. Others read "strong buying interest" and buy at $100.05. The trader sells into that, then cancels the buy orders.

⚠️ Real Case: Navinder Singh Sarao (2010 Flash Crash)

Who: Solo trader from his parents' house in London

Tactic: Used layering and spoofing to manipulate S&P 500 futures

Impact: Contributed to May 6, 2010 Flash Crash (Dow fell 1,000 points in minutes)

Punishment: Arrested 2015, sentenced to 1 year home confinement

What this case does and does not show: Sarao pleaded guilty to spoofing and to wire fraud, and his layering was running that morning. But the official joint SEC-CFTC report on the crash attributed the initial move to a large automated sell programme in E-mini futures, not to him, and how much he contributed remains disputed among the people who have studied it. What the case does establish is that one determined individual with a modified front end could distort a major futures book for months before anyone stopped him — which says more about how hard this is to detect than about who is doing it.

Tactic #3: Quote Stuffing

What it is: Rapidly placing and canceling thousands of orders to flood the order book and slow down competitors.

Why MMs do it:

  • Creates "noise" that confuses HFT algos from competing firms
  • Slows down data feeds → gives MM's own algos a latency advantage
  • Makes order book harder to read → retail hesitates → MM gets better fills

The scale of it: the overwhelming majority of orders entered in US equities are cancelled rather than executed, and have been since at least 2010. Most of that is ordinary market making — quotes updated as the market moves. Quote stuffing is a deliberate subset of it, and separating the two from public data is not something a retail trader can do.

Part 4: Latency Arbitrage & HFT Speed Advantages

The Speed Game

Market makers who are faster by even 1 millisecond (0.001 seconds) can see order flow before competitors and profit from the information advantage.

What Is Latency Arbitrage?

⚡ Latency Arbitrage Explained

Scenario: Stock XYZ trades on multiple exchanges (NYSE, Nasdaq, BATS, etc.)

  1. NYSE shows XYZ at $100.00 bid / $100.05 ask
  2. Big seller hits NYSE with market order → XYZ drops to $99.95
  3. HFT MM sees this in 50 microseconds (0.00005 seconds)
  4. HFT immediately sells on Nasdaq (still showing $100.00) before the price update arrives
  5. Buys back on NYSE at $99.95 → locks in $0.05 profit per share
  6. Repeats this thousands of times per day

Result: HFT MM makes millions from being faster than retail and slower institutions.

How Fast Are We Talking?

Speed Comparison Time (Milliseconds) Notes
Human eye blink 300-400 ms Slowest reference point
Retail trader clicks "Buy" 150-300 ms Average human reaction time
Standard broker routing 10-50 ms Robinhood, TD Ameritrade
Institutional algo (slow) 1-5 ms Hedge funds, mutual funds
HFT market maker 0.05-0.5 ms Citadel, Virtu, Jump Trading
Fastest HFT (co-located) 0.01-0.03 ms Servers physically next to exchange

Translation: By the time you click "Buy," HFT MMs have already seen the order, analyzed it, and executed 10-20 trades ahead of you.

Co-Location: The Ultimate Advantage

Co-location = Renting server space inside the exchange's data center (literally feet away from the exchange's matching engine).

💰 The Cost of Speed

Co-location fees (NYSE):

  • Cabinet space and power: tens of thousands of dollars a month for a full high-draw cabinet, less for a partial one
  • Data feed access: $10,000-$30,000/month
  • Network connectivity: $5,000-$15,000/month

Total: $480,000 - $1.14 million/year just for the infrastructure

Only institutional MMs can afford this. Retail traders competing against this = bringing a knife to a gunfight.

Microwave Towers: Shaving Microseconds

Firms built microwave tower networks between Chicago (CME) and New Jersey to cut the Chicago-to-New-Jersey round trip from roughly 14.5 milliseconds over fibre to roughly 8.5 over microwave. The $300 million figure this lesson used to attach to the towers actually belongs to Spread Networks' dedicated FIBRE line, laid a few years earlier — which the microwave links then made obsolete almost immediately, at a fraction of the cost. That is the more interesting fact: the expensive thing was beaten by the cheap thing within about three years.

Why? Light travels about a third slower through fiber-optic cable than through open air. That 6 millisecond advantage = millions in arbitrage profits.

✅ How Retail Traders Can Compete (Spoiler: You Can't on Speed)

Accept reality: You will NEVER beat HFT MMs on speed. Don't try.

What you CAN do:

  • Trade longer timeframes: Latency doesn't matter on 4H/Daily charts
  • Avoid tight scalps: Don't compete for 1-2 tick profits (MMs win there)
  • Use limit orders at key levels: Let MMs come to you (don't chase)
  • Do NOT trade illiquid times. An earlier version of this list said pre-market and after-hours have less HFT activity, which is true and completely beside the point: they also have far wider spreads, and the spread is the cost you actually pay. Strategy #2 below says the same thing and the two used to contradict each other. Liquid hours, always.

Bottom line: Play a different game. Focus on pattern recognition, order flow analysis, and multi-day swings where speed doesn't matter.

📉 CASE STUDY: Eric's $68,000 Market Maker Death Spiral (7 weeks)

Trader: Eric Thompson (composite example), 32, software engineer turned day trader (4 years experience, $140K account), Jul-Aug 2024

Strategy: High-frequency scalping SPY/TSLA/NVDA, 60-80 trades/day, 1-3 minute holds. He had traded the same way for a year before this and considered the approach settled, which is the part worth noticing: the execution cost was there the whole time and only became visible when he raised the trade count. No prior-year profit figure is given — a good year before a bad one is not evidence about the method, and quoting it would make this a story about a streak rather than about a cost.

Fatal flaw: Never used limit orders, never checked Level 2, traded during widest spread periods (9:30-9:45 AM open, 3:50-4:00 PM close, Fed days). "Limit orders might miss the move."

Result: Win rate was 52% (profitable strategy), but slippage destroyed edge. Average slippage $0.10/share × 300 shares/trade × 60 trades/day = $1,800/day slippage cost = $63,000 in 7 weeks. Lost $68K (-48.6%). $140K → $72K.

The slippage disaster (Jul-Aug 2024): Eric's setups were solid, but every trade bled money to market makers. Examples: Jul 9 SPY market buy during 9:32 AM volatility, NBBO spread $549.50/$549.58 (8¢), filled $549.63 (+5¢ slippage) = -$86 loss. Jul 30 Fed day SPY 0DTE calls market buy, $2.50 mid, spread $2.35/$2.65 (30¢ wide), filled $2.68 (+18¢ overpay × 300 contracts = $5,400 extra). Profit was $4,500, should've been $9,900. Aug 1 NVDA earnings market buy during volatility, spread $127.50/$128.20 (70¢!), filled $128.35 (+85¢ slippage) = -$470 vs -$300 with limit. Aug 23 realization: "Win rate 52%, setups solid, but down $68,000 in 7 weeks. Execution report showed $0.10/share avg slippage. I'm trading 60×/day at 300 shares. $30/trade lost to slippage = $1,800/day = $63,000 in 7 weeks. I'm not a trader. I'm Citadel's ATM."

Recovery (Sep-Oct 2024): New execution system: (1) Limit orders only at NBBO mid (split spread with MMs), (2) Check Level 2 for fake walls/spoofing before entry, (3) Avoid 9:30-9:45 AM and 3:50-4:00 PM (widest spreads), (4) Skip if spread > 10% of stop, (5) Track slippage daily (target < $0.01/share), (6) Reduce frequency to 15-20 trades/day (higher quality). The measurable change: his own execution report showed the average gap between the quote when he clicked and the price he received falling by most of its size once he stopped using market orders in the widest windows. No account result is reported for the ten weeks that followed. Ten weeks cannot produce one, and a win-rate improvement from 52% to 67% over a few hundred trades would be describing the market he traded in, not the change he made.

The arithmetic worth taking from this, with one correction: $0.10 a share × 300 shares × 60 trades a day is $1,800 a day, and $63,000 over seven weeks. That is the number that matters and it is simple multiplication. But be careful about what the $0.10 was. On a liquid ETF in the middle of the day, the spread is a penny and the cost of crossing it is a penny; you cannot lose a dime a share in SPY at 11 AM by any amount of impatience. Where Eric's figure came from was the specific places he traded: the first fifteen minutes, the last ten, Fed days, and 0DTE options whose spreads genuinely are 20 to 30 cents wide. The lesson is therefore narrower and more useful than "market orders cost you a dime": the cost of crossing the spread is whatever the spread happens to be at that moment, so the entire question is which moments you choose to trade in. Sixty trades a day forces you into all of them.

Case Study Quiz: Eric had a 52% win rate (profitable strategy) trading 60 times per day, but still lost $68,000 in 7 weeks. His average slippage was $0.10/share on 300-share positions. What was Eric's fatal mistake?

A) His win rate was too low—he needed at least 60% to be profitable with that many trades
B) He used 100% market orders and traded during the widest spread periods (9:30-9:45 AM, 3:50-4:00 PM, Fed days), bleeding $1,800/day in slippage to market makers
C) He traded too many stocks—should have focused on just SPY instead of SPY/TSLA/NVDA
D) He held positions too long—1-3 minutes is too slow for high-frequency scalping
Correct: B. This is the brutal reality of trading execution costs. Eric's setups were PROFITABLE (52% win rate with good risk:reward), but his execution method destroyed his edge entirely. Here's the math breakdown: Eric traded 60 times/day with 300 shares per trade. His average cost per share was about $0.10 — and it is worth being exact about what that is, because the lesson used to call it "slippage" throughout and that is only half right. Most of it was simply the spread, which a market order pays by definition, and it was large because of WHEN he traded rather than because anything went wrong: opening and closing minutes, Fed days, and 0DTE options where the quoted spread is genuinely tens of cents. A small remainder was true slippage, the order filling worse than the quote when size exceeded what was displayed. Daily slippage cost: $0.10/share × 300 shares = $30 per trade × 60 trades = $1,800/day. Over 35 trading days (7 weeks): $1,800 × 35 = $63,000 in pure slippage costs. He lost $68K total, meaning $63K of it came from slippage alone—NOT from bad trades. His win rate was profitable! The three execution mistakes: (1) 100% market orders instead of limit orders. (2) Trading in the widest-spread windows. (3) Never checking Level 2 order book. He also read large resting orders as walls and traded against them, then watched them disappear. Note that this costs you whether or not anyone intended to deceive: an order that is withdrawn as price approaches has the same effect on your decision either way, which is the practical reason not to lean on one. What he changed: limit orders instead of market orders, no trades in the first fifteen or last ten minutes, and roughly a third as many trades. Note that the last of those three does most of the work. Cutting from sixty trades a day to eighteen cuts the daily cost by two thirds before you have improved a single fill. No account result is reported for the weeks that followed: the point of this case study is a cost you can calculate in advance, and attaching a recovery figure to it would replace an argument you can check with an anecdote you cannot. The lesson: a strategy with a positive expectancy before costs can have a negative one after them, and nothing about the strategy tells you which. Work out your cost per trade — spread paid, times shares, times trades per day — before you work out anything else. If that number is a meaningful fraction of your average winner, the strategy does not work at that frequency, and no amount of being right about direction will fix it.

Part 5: Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)

Why "Free" Trading Isn't Free

Payment for Order Flow (PFOF) is the practice where brokers (Robinhood, Webull) sell your order to market makers (Citadel, Virtu) instead of routing it to public exchanges.

The Deal:

  • You place a trade on Robinhood (commission-free)
  • The broker routes your order to a wholesaler and is paid for it. For equities that payment is small — typically a small fraction of a cent per share, not the $0.002 this lesson used to print, which was roughly ten times too high. Options are where the money is: per-contract rebates are far larger, and options are most of the total.
  • Citadel fills your order and pockets the spread

How Robinhood Makes Money

Revenue Source: transaction-based revenue — order-flow rebates on equities, options and crypto — was roughly three quarters of Robinhood's total revenue at its 2021 peak. Options and crypto were the bulk of it; equity rebates were the smallest piece.

The Math:

  • Robinhood has 15 million users
  • Each user trades 100 shares/month on average
  • Total: 1.5 billion shares/month
  • The wholesaler pays a small fraction of a cent per share for equities
  • The equity piece is the small one. No total is calculated here, because the per-share rate is not published in a form you can multiply, and the figures that circulate are usually the options rate applied to share counts. What matters for the argument is the direction, not the total: the broker is paid more when you trade more, which is why the app is built the way it is.

Why Robinhood loves PFOF: They get paid more when you trade more (confetti animations, push notifications, "free stock" promotions).

How Citadel Makes Money

Why Citadel pays for order flow: Retail order flow is uninformed (low adverse selection risk), making it highly profitable.

The Math:

  • Citadel buys 1.5 billion shares/month from Robinhood
  • Average spread captured: $0.01/share
  • Not calculable from these numbers. Capturing a full penny per share is the ceiling, not the outcome — price improvement gives much of it back, and that is the wholesaler's main selling point to brokers. The honest statement is that retail flow is profitable because it is uninformed, not that it yields the whole spread.
  • The economics that do hold: uninformed flow can be quoted more tightly than anonymous exchange flow, because the person on the other side is very unlikely to know something you do not. That is the whole reason it is worth paying for.

How You Lose Money

The Hidden Cost: You don't pay commissions, but you pay via price improvement failure.

Example:

  • NBBO: Bid $100.00, Ask $100.02
  • You place market buy on Robinhood
  • Robinhood routes to Citadel (doesn't send to public exchange)
  • Citadel fills you at $100.02 (the ask)
  • But: On the public exchange, there was a seller at $100.01
  • You overpaid by $0.01/share

Scale: 1,000 shares = overpaid $10. Over 100 trades/year = $1,000/year in hidden costs.

⚠️ What the evidence on PFOF actually says

The "2020 SEC study finding $1.5 billion a year in costs" that this lesson used to cite does not exist, and it has been removed. Here is what does. Academic work that sent identical orders through several retail brokers at the same moment found large and persistent differences in the prices they came back with — differences far bigger than the commissions the industry had just abolished. Brokers publish execution-quality reports under SEC rules, and those reports show most marketable retail orders receiving some price improvement over the quoted spread. Both things are true at once, and the real argument is about the gap between the improvement you get and the improvement an order-by-order auction might have produced. That is an argument about how much better it could be, not about whether you are being filled outside the quote.

What to do with that: the practical conclusion is unchanged and does not need a headline number. Your broker publishes an execution-quality report. Read the one for your broker rather than an aggregate for the industry, and compare it against another. That comparison is specific to you and is the only version of this question you can actually settle.

Latency Arbitrage via PFOF

A claim you will meet constantly, and which the previous version of this lesson repeated: that the wholesaler sees your order first and trades ahead of it. Be precise here, because the true part and the false part are close together. Trading ahead of a customer order is prohibited, and a firm that bought stock in front of your buy and then sold it to you would be committing a straightforward violation, not exploiting a loophole. What is true is subtler and still worth knowing.

Example:

  1. You buy 1,000 AAPL shares at market on Robinhood
  2. Robinhood sends order to Citadel (not NYSE)
  3. The wholesaler sees your order before the wider market does — it is the one filling it
  4. Citadel's algo sees your 1,000 share buy coming
  5. Citadel buys 1,000 AAPL on NYSE at $175.00 (the bid)
  6. Citadel then sells to you at $175.02 (the ask)
  7. Citadel profit: $20, with the position held for milliseconds

Why it's legal: Citadel claims they "provided liquidity" and you paid the NBBO ask (not worse). But they knew your order was coming—information asymmetry.

Part 6: How to Trade Against Market Makers

Defense Strategies for Retail Traders

You can't eliminate market maker edge, but you can minimize it.

Strategy #1: Always Use Limit Orders

Rule: Never use market orders unless execution speed matters more than price.

Why: Limit orders let you set the price, not the market maker.

Example:

  • NBBO: Bid $100.00, Ask $100.05
  • Bad: Market buy → filled at $100.05
  • Good: Limit buy at $100.02 → wait for seller, save $0.03/share

💡 Pro Tip: Use Midpoint Limit Orders

Tactic: Place limit orders at the midpoint of the bid-ask spread.

Bid $100.00, Ask $100.04 → Midpoint = $100.02. Place limit buy at $100.02.

Benefit: You split the spread with the MM instead of giving them the entire edge.

Strategy #2: Trade During Liquid Hours

Rule: Trade between 10:00 AM - 3:00 PM ET (avoid first/last hour and after-hours).

Why: spreads are tightest when liquidity is deepest and volatility lowest, because both of the risks a spread is paid for — inventory and adverse selection — are smallest then. The open and the close are wide for a reason rather than out of opportunism: overnight news is being priced, volumes are lumpy, and the chance that whoever is trading against you knows something is at its highest.

Example:

  • AAPL spread at 10:00 AM: $0.01
  • AAPL spread in the first minutes: wider, though how much varies day to day. Note that this lesson used to quote 5x here and 8x further down, from the same stock on the same morning — if two numbers in one lesson disagree, neither was measured. Check your own instrument at 9:31 and at 11:00 for a week; that is a ten-minute exercise and it will give you a figure you can trust.
  • AAPL spread at 6:00 PM (after-hours): $0.15 (15x wider)

Strategy #3: Avoid PFOF Brokers (Or Route to IEX)

Rule: know whether your broker accepts payment for your order flow, and on which products. The blanket list this lesson used to print — "Fidelity, Vanguard, Interactive Brokers don't" — is wrong about all three in the details. Some brokers decline it on equities but accept it on options. Some run two tiers, one that accepts it and one that does not, and you have to be on the right tier. Check the current disclosure for your own account rather than trusting a list in any lesson, including this one, which will be out of date before you read it.

Why it matters: not because of a latency advantage — the wholesaler is filling you, not racing you. It matters because routing decided by who pays for the flow is a different decision from routing decided by where the order fills best, and those two can point at the same venue or at different ones. When they diverge, you are the one who pays the difference, and you will never see it as a line item.

Alternative: If stuck with Robinhood, route orders to IEX (Investors Exchange)—a public exchange designed to eliminate latency arbitrage.

How to Route to IEX on Interactive Brokers:

  1. Open order entry ticket
  2. Click "Route" dropdown
  3. Select "IEX" (Investors Exchange)
  4. Place limit order

What the speed bump actually does: IEX delays incoming messages by 350 microseconds, which stops a resting order on IEX from being picked off on a quote that has already moved somewhere else. That is a genuine protection, and it is a protection for LIQUIDITY PROVIDERS — for your limit orders sitting on the book. It does nothing for a market order you send there, and describing it as preventing front-running of retail is an overstatement you will see everywhere.

Strategy #4: Don't Chase—Let the Market Come to You

Rule: If your limit order doesn't fill immediately, don't chase by raising your limit price.

Why: Market makers want you to chase. They intentionally pull liquidity to force you to pay more.

Example:

  • You place limit buy at $100.02
  • Ask jumps to $100.10 (MM pulled liquidity)
  • Bad move: Cancel and rebuy at $100.10 (you got played)
  • Good move: Wait 30 seconds. Ask will likely return to $100.05

Don't overwhelm yourself. Start with these 3 actions:

  1. Switch to Limit Orders on Next 10 Trades (100% Rule) — Tomorrow commit: "Next 10 trades, ONLY limit orders, even if I miss move." When buying, look at bid/ask. Bid $100.00, Ask $100.05? Place limit at $100.02 (midpoint). Wait 10 sec. If no fill, adjust $0.01 if necessary. Track fills: how much better than ask? Example: $50.00/$50.05, limit at $50.02, filled $50.02 = saved $0.03/share vs market order. 200 shares = $6 saved. 10 trades = $60. 100 trades/year = $600. 1,000 trades = $6,000. Measure slippage savings: "Fill price minus ask." Never go back to market orders.
  2. Check Level 2 Order Book Before Next Entry (Identify Fake Walls) — Open broker's Level 2 window ("Market Depth" or "Order Book"). Before entry, look at buy/sell orders. See HUGE order dwarfing others? Example: 50,000 shares to sell at $100.10, but other levels only 500-2,000. Likely FAKE wall (spoofing/layering). Market makers use to scare you. Watch: within 30 seconds, 50,000 share order disappears or gets pulled. Never real. Then read the correction in the quiz explanation before you draw conclusions from it: most orders in US equities are cancelled rather than filled, so a large order vanishing is the ordinary case and not evidence of anything. The usable version of this exercise is narrower and still worth doing — watch how often a big resting order is still there when price actually reaches it. Learning that you cannot lean on one is the point.
  3. Avoid Trading First 15 Min (9:30-9:45 AM) and Last 10 Min (3:50-4:00 PM) for One Week — Biggest behavior change separating profitable traders from losers. Spreads are widest in these windows, for a real reason: volatility is highest and the chance of being on the wrong side of informed flow is greatest, so quoting is more expensive. Eric Thompson lost $68,000 (-48.6%) in 7 weeks—had 52% win rate (profitable strategy) but slippage destroyed edge. Avg slippage $0.10/share × 300 shares × 60 trades/day = $1,800/day = $63,000 in 7 weeks using market orders during market maker windows. Measure this yourself rather than taking a figure from a lesson: note the spread on your instrument at 9:32 and again at 11:00, every day for a week. The point is not the multiple, it is that you will see the cost with your own eyes and stop arguing with it. One week rule: no trades until 9:45am, close all by 3:50pm. No improvement figure is promised. What you will have after one week is a record of what the first fifteen minutes were costing you, which is the thing that changes behaviour.
Quiz: Test Your Understanding

📝 Knowledge Check

Test your understanding of market maker algorithms:

AAPL bid/ask is $175.00 / $175.05. You place a market sell order for 500 shares. What price do you get?

A) $175.00 (the bid)
B) $175.05 (the ask)
C) $175.025 (the midpoint)
Correct: A is the answer to expect, though "always" is too strong and the exceptions matter. A marketable order can be filled BETWEEN the quotes: price improvement on retail marketable orders is common, and it is the main thing wholesalers advertise to the brokers who route to them, so a fill at $175.01 or $175.02 is a normal outcome rather than a surprise. In the other direction, an order larger than the displayed size at the bid walks down the book and averages worse than $175.00. So: the bid is what you are accepting, and it is the right answer among these three, but treat it as the reference price rather than a guarantee. Here's the mechanic: Market makers post two quotes simultaneously—bid (price they'll buy at) and ask (price they'll sell at). When you place a market sell, you're accepting their bid offer. They buy your shares at $175.00, then turn around and sell them to the next buyer at $175.05, capturing the $0.05 spread ($25 profit on your 500 shares, in milliseconds — they hold the inventory only until the next buyer arrives). This is called "spread capture," and it's the market maker's core business model. Why you should care: If you had placed a limit sell at $175.03 instead, you might have been filled there, saving $0.03 a share, or $15 on this trade. Whether that scales to $1,500 over a hundred trades depends entirely on how many of those limit orders never fill and what the missed moves cost you — which is the trade-off the whole strategy turns on, and the reason the figure is not quoted as a saving here. The bid-ask spread is the market maker's profit margin—and your cost. Market orders guarantee you pay the maximum cost. Limit orders at midpoint split the difference. Always check the spread size before trading: spread > 0.1% of stock price = use limit orders only.

You see a huge sell order (50,000 shares) appear at $100.10 on Level 2, but it disappears within 20 seconds before anyone can fill it. What just happened?

A) A large institution changed their mind about selling
B) Spoofing—a market maker posted a fake order to manipulate price perception
C) High-frequency trader testing liquidity conditions
Correct: B is what the question is testing, and the sequence below is what spoofing looks like. But read the caveat at the end before you start applying it, because the detection rule this card used to give was wrong and would have you seeing manipulation everywhere. Here's the full sequence: (1) A trader wants to buy shares at $100.00, the current bid. (2) They post a sell order for 50,000 shares at $100.10 that they do not intend to have filled, creating the appearance of heavy supply. (3) Others read the order book and think: "huge seller at $100.10, this cannot break higher, I should sell at $100.00 before it drops." (4) Those shares are sold into $100.00 and the spoofer buys them. (5) The 50,000 share order is cancelled before it can trade. (6) Price rallies to $100.20, because the supply was never there, and the spoofer sells into it. Why it's illegal: Dodd-Frank Act 2010 banned spoofing, but it's nearly impossible to prove intent. Regulators must show the trader "never intended to execute" the order. Defense: "I changed my mind" or "market conditions shifted." How to spot it — and here is the correction: you cannot, from a Level 2 screen. The great majority of orders entered in US equities are cancelled rather than executed, and have been for over a decade. That is not manipulation; it is ordinary quoting, updated continuously as the market moves, plus algorithms that post and pull as they work parent orders. A large order that appears and disappears without filling is the normal case, not an 80% signal of anything. The "track ten and if eight vanish you have confirmed it" test that used to sit here confirms nothing, because eight of ten vanishing is what you would see in a market with no spoofing in it at all. What you can usefully take from this section: do not treat a large resting order as a wall you can lean on. It may be withdrawn the moment price approaches it, whatever the intent behind it.

You trade on Robinhood (PFOF broker). NBBO is $100.00 / $100.02. You place a market buy for 1,000 shares. Citadel fills you at $100.02. Did you get a fair price?

A) Yes—you paid the NBBO ask, so it's fair by regulation
B) Yes—Robinhood is commission-free, so you saved money overall
C) No—there might have been better prices ($100.01) on public exchanges that you didn't access
Correct: C. This is the hidden cost of Payment for Order Flow (PFOF). Here's what really happened behind the scenes: When you hit "buy" on Robinhood, your order did NOT go to NYSE or Nasdaq (public exchanges). Instead, Robinhood sold your order to Citadel for $0.002/share ($2 payment for your 1,000 share order). Citadel filled you at $100.02 (the NBBO ask), which is LEGAL—they're required to match or beat NBBO. The version of this explanation printed here previously does not hold together, and it is worth seeing why. It said there was a seller resting at $100.01 on NYSE that you were denied. If that were true, $100.01 would BE the national best offer, and filling you at $100.02 would be a trade-through — a violation, not a loophole. A displayed better price cannot be ignored; that is what the order protection rule exists to enforce. The real answer to the question is subtler. You were filled at the quoted ask, which is legal and is what you were promised. What you did not get is the price improvement the same order might have earned somewhere else: in the wholesaler model, how much of the spread comes back to you is set by that firm's pricing, and no one else got the chance to bid for the right to fill you. That is the actual complaint, and the reason exchanges and regulators have argued about order-by-order competition for years. Note also what the commission-free argument does and does not settle. Zero commission is real money saved. Whether the fills you get make up for it is a question you can answer with your own broker's execution-quality report, and it will not be the same for every broker or every order size, which is why no dollar figure is given here. Old-school brokers (Fidelity, Schwab) charge $0 commissions now AND route to best execution. What actually helps: use a broker that lets you direct your routing, and use limit orders, which take the question of where you sit in the spread out of anyone else's hands. Be careful with the IEX claim in particular. Its 350-microsecond delay protects orders RESTING on IEX from being picked off on a stale quote; it does nothing about a marketable order you send there, and it does not "prevent front-running" of a retail buy. No improvement figure is quoted, because none has been measured for you specifically.
Key Takeaways

💡 Understanding Market Makers = Better Fills

  • Spread capture is the MM's core profit: buy bid, sell ask, repeat billions of times.
  • Adverse selection = informed traders cost MMs money, so MMs widen spreads when they detect "toxic" flow.
  • Inventory management: MMs skew quotes, use dark pools, hedge with futures to stay delta-neutral.
  • Spoofing and layering are illegal and prosecuted — and the enforcement record is against proprietary traders and bank desks rather than the wholesalers filling your orders. You cannot identify either from a Level 2 screen: most orders in US equities are cancelled rather than filled, so a vanishing order proves nothing.
  • PFOF = "free" trading isn't free. You pay via worse fills and latency arbitrage.
  • Defense: Use limit orders, trade during liquid hours, avoid PFOF brokers, route to IEX.
