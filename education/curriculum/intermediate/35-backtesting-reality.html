Backtesting Reality: That Perfect Expectancy Is Lying to You

16 min read • Strategy Validation •
Signal Pilot

Your backtest: 8R expectancy. 3.5 profit factor. $50,000 profit on $10,000 account.

You go live. First week: -2R expectancy. 0.4 profit factor. Account down 12%.

What happened? Your backtest lied.

The four assumptions a backtest makes for you

Unless you told it otherwise, it filled every order at the price you asked for, paid no spread, paid no commission, and never missed a fill. Each of those is a small lie, and they all point the same way.

The other failure is different in kind: a strategy fitted to one stretch of history describes that stretch. Only out-of-sample data can tell you which of the two you have.

In this lesson, you'll learn:

  • Why a backtest can be arithmetically correct and still describe a strategy that cannot be traded
  • The 4 deadly sins: overfitting, look-ahead bias, unrealistic costs, survivorship bias
  • How to model slippage and spreads that actually reflect reality
  • In-sample vs. out-of-sample testing (the only validation that matters)

Part 1: The Four Ways Backtests Lie
📖 Deep dive: Why Backtesting Results Are Worthless (And What to Do Instead) →

Why Your Perfect Strategy Will Fail Live

A strategy optimised against a stretch of history will fit that history. That is what optimisation does, and it happens whether or not there was an edge there to find.

Curve-Fitting to Random Noise

What it is: Optimizing parameters until backtest looks perfect

Example:

  • You test 100 different EMA periods (10, 11, 12... 110)
  • EMA(47) gives 4.2R expectancy in backtest
  • You think you found the "magic number"

Reality: EMA(47) just happened to fit that specific data set. Forward test? -0.8R expectancy. You learned the noise, not the signal.

Fix: Limit optimization. Use standard parameters. Test on unseen data.

Using Future Data (Impossible in Live Trading)

What it is: Code that "peeks" into future candles to make current decisions

Example:

if close[0] > high[5]:  # Uses FUTURE 5 candles
    enter_long()

The problem: In backtesting, you have future data. In live trading, you don't.

Reality: This code literally cannot execute live. Your backtest is testing a strategy that doesn't exist.

Fix: Only use data available at the moment of decision.

Assuming Perfect Fills at Perfect Prices

Backtest assumption: "I buy at $100.00 exactly"

Reality: Market order fills at $100.12 (spread + slippage)

Impact over 100 trades:

  • Backtest profit: $5,000
  • Spread cost ($0.10/trade): -$1,000
  • Slippage ($0.05/trade): -$500
  • Commissions ($2/trade): -$200

Reality: Real profit = $3,300 (34% less than backtest)

Testing Only on Survivors

What it is: Backtesting on current S&P 500 stocks only

The problem: the index is not the same 500 companies it was ten years ago. Names leave it constantly — most through acquisition or falling out of the size band, some by going to zero — and the ones that went to zero are precisely the ones a mean-reversion rule would have kept buying

Example:

  • Backtest on 2024 S&P 500 list: 1.8R expectancy, 2.1 profit factor
  • But across that window you'd have been long Hertz (bankrupt 2020), SVB Financial (collapsed 2023), and every other name that went to zero.

Reality: Your expectancy excludes catastrophic losses from delistings. Real expectancy: -0.2R (losing system).

💡 The Aha Moment

A backtest isn't reality. It's a simulation. And if your simulation includes impossible assumptions, your live results will be impossible too.

Part 2: Modeling Realistic Costs

The Hidden Costs That Kill Strategies

Here's what most backtests ignore. How much it costs you depends on your instrument, size and holding period, so the only honest answer is the one you compute below from your own numbers:

Step 1: Model Bid-Ask Spread

What it is: The difference between buy price and sell price

Example:

  • SPY bid: $450.00 / ask: $450.01 → Spread = $0.01
  • You buy: Pay $450.01 (half spread = $0.005)
  • You sell: Receive $450.00 (half spread = $0.005)

Cost per round trip: $0.01

For 1,000 shares: $0.01 × 1,000 = $10 per trade

100 trades: $1,000 in spread costs

Step 2: Model Slippage

What it is: Difference between intended price and actual fill

Realistic slippage by condition:

  • Normal liquidity: 0.01-0.02%
  • Volatile markets: 0.05-0.10%
  • Thin liquidity: 0.10-0.30%
  • Large position size: 0.20-0.50%

Conservative assumption: 0.05% per trade

$10,000 position × 0.05% = $5 slippage per side = $10 round trip

Step 3: Model Commissions

Example: Interactive Brokers

  • $0.005 per share, $1 minimum
  • 200 shares × $0.005 = $1 per side
  • Round trip = $2

100 trades: $200 in commissions

Step 4: Total Realistic Cost

Per trade (round trip):

  • Spread: $10
  • Slippage: $10
  • Commission: $2

Total: $22 per trade

If your strategy makes $50/trade gross → $28 net (44% reduction!)

Backtest Adjustment Example

Before vs. After Realistic Costs

Strategy: 100 trades, $50 avg profit per trade

Backtest (fantasy):

100 trades × $50 = $5,000 profit

After realistic costs:

  • Gross: $5,000
  • Spread: -$1,000
  • Slippage: -$1,000
  • Commissions: -$200

Net profit: $2,800 (44% reduction)

The lesson: Chris's backtest was a fantasy. Testing 15,000 parameter combinations guarantees you'll find one that fits historical noise perfectly. The "perfect" 1.3R expectancy was curve-fit garbage. Out-of-sample testing would have revealed this before losing $8,400. Backtesting isn't about finding the best historical performance—it's about finding what will work going forward. And that requires unseen data, realistic costs, and zero look-ahead bias.

Part 3: In-Sample vs. Out-of-Sample

The Only Validation That Matters

Here's the professional way to validate a strategy:

Split your data into two periods.

  1. In-Sample (60-70%): Develop and optimize your strategy here
  2. Out-of-Sample (30-40%): Validate on UNSEEN data

🎯 Example: 6 Years of Data (2018-2024)

In-Sample: 2018-2022 (optimize here)

  • Test different parameters
  • Find best-performing rules
  • Refine entry/exit logic

Out-of-Sample: 2023-2024 (validate here)

  • Run strategy with ZERO changes
  • Compare performance to in-sample
  • If similar → Not overfit!
  • If much worse → Overfit to in-sample noise

Red Flags of Overfitting

🚩 Warning Signs Your Strategy Is Curve-Fit

  • Sharpe > 3.0: Too good to be true
  • Expectancy above about +0.5R per trade: rare enough that it needs an explanation. Above +1R, an execution assumption is almost certainly wrong — note that Chris's "holy grail" backtest above was +1.3R
  • Only 20-30 trades: Sample size too small (statistically meaningless)
  • Perfect equity curve: Smooth line with no drawdowns (impossible)
  • Tested on 1 asset only: Likely fit to that specific regime
  • 10+ optimized parameters: You fit the noise, not the signal
Part 4: Walk-Forward Analysis

The Professional Validation Method

In-sample/out-of-sample is good. Walk-forward is better.

How it works:

📊 Walk-Forward Framework

Period 1: Train on 2018-2019 → Test on 2020

Period 2: Train on 2019-2020 → Test on 2021

Period 3: Train on 2020-2021 → Test on 2022

Period 4: Train on 2021-2022 → Test on 2023

If consistent across all periods: Robust strategy

If performance degrades: Overfit or regime-dependent

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Model realistic costs: Spread, slippage, commissions (20-40% profit reduction)
  • Avoid overfitting: Limit parameters, test across multiple markets
  • In-sample vs. out-of-sample: Develop on 60-70%, validate on 30-40%
  • Walk-forward testing: Verify consistency across time periods
  • Red flags: Sharpe > 3.0, expectancy > 5R, < 30 trades
  • Forward test before live: Paper trade 3-6 months minimum
Practice Exercise

🎯 Backtest Validation Practice

Exercise: Validate Your Strategy Using the Red Flag Detector

Take your current strategy (or a strategy you're considering) and run it through validation:

  1. Backtest on 60% of your data (in-sample). Record: trades, expectancy, Sharpe, max DD
  2. Identify parameters used (EMAs, thresholds, etc.). Did you optimize these by testing multiple values?
  3. Test on remaining 40% of data (out-of-sample). Compare results to in-sample.
  4. Score the strategy on the eight checks below, 0 to 5 each (40 points maximum)
  5. Add it up: 32 or more = proceed to paper trading, 24-31 = revise and re-test, under 24 = scrap it
  6. If you scored 32 or more: paper trade for 30-60 trades before going live

The eight checks (0 = not done at all, 5 = done properly)

  1. Data split. Was a genuine out-of-sample period held back and left untouched during development? Score 0 if you optimised on everything you had.
  2. Optimisation restraint. How many parameter combinations did you try? Under 10 scores 5; thousands scores 0, whatever the result looked like.
  3. Cost modelling. Are spread, slippage and commission in the equity curve at values you could defend to a broker? Zero costs scores 0.
  4. Look-ahead check. Did you re-read the entry logic asking specifically whether each input existed at the moment of the decision? Signal on this candle, execution on the next, scores 5.
  5. Out-of-sample result. How much did performance degrade on the untouched data? Under 30% degradation scores 5; over 70%, as in Chris's case, scores 0.
  6. Walk-forward consistency. Did you roll the optimisation window forward and test the next period, several times? One period scores 1; four consistent periods score 5.
  7. Sample size. Fewer than 30 trades scores 0 no matter how good they look; 100 or more scores 5.
  8. Breadth. Does it hold on more than one instrument, or across more than one market regime? One asset in one regime scores 1.

Run Chris's strategy through this and it scores 2 out of 40 — a point for sample size and a point for having a walk-forward period at all. The scorecard would have caught it in an afternoon, for free.

Goal: Catch curve-fitting, look-ahead bias, and unrealistic assumptions BEFORE they destroy your live account. Better to scrap a bad strategy in backtesting than lose real money.

Test Your Understanding

🎮 Quick Check

Q: You backtest a strategy: 5.2R expectancy, 4.2 Sharpe ratio, tested on 25 trades, using EMA(47) because it gave the best results after testing 100 different periods. What's wrong?

A) Nothing—great strategy!
B) Just the expectancy is too high
C) All of it: Sample size too small, Sharpe & expectancy too high, EMA(47) is curve-fit
D) Just the sample size is too small
Correct! Three major red flags: (1) Only 25 trades = statistically insignificant, (2) Sharpe 4.2 = suspiciously high (likely overfit), (3) EMA(47) found by testing 100 periods = curve-fit to noise. Need: 100+ trades, standard parameters, and a Sharpe you would not be embarrassed to defend — above 2 is exceptional even for professional systematic funds, and a retail backtest showing 4.2 is describing an execution assumption, not an edge.

You backtest a mean-reversion strategy on SPY from 2010-2024. Results: 2.8R expectancy, 68% win rate, $120K profit. Data includes only current S&P 500 components (stocks in the index today). You go live in 2025—it fails. What went wrong?

A) Nothing—2025 market conditions changed, unforeseeable
B) Expectancy too high—2.8R is unrealistic for mean reversion
C) Sample size too small—need more than 14 years of data
D) Survivorship bias—the backtest only contains companies that were still in the index in 2024, so every name that left it between 2010 and 2024 is missing
Correct! Classic survivorship bias trap. Testing on "current S&P 500 components" means you only tested WINNERS—stocks that survived 14 years and made it into today's index. Excluded from the test: every company that was acquired, dropped out of the index, or went to zero over those fourteen years. The ones that went to zero are the ones that matter here, because a mean-reversion rule buys weakness — it would have bought them all the way down and never been paid. How much this inflates a given backtest depends entirely on the universe and the rule, so no percentage is quoted; what is certain is the direction, and that it is never in your favour. Solution: use point-in-time data that includes ALL stocks that existed at each date, including the ones that later delisted.

You walk-forward test your strategy: Train 2018-2020, test 2021 (pass). Train 2018-2021, test 2022 (pass). Train 2018-2022, test 2023 (pass). You didn't model slippage or commissions. Should you trade this strategy live?

A) No—must add realistic costs (0.2-0.4% slippage + commissions) and retest. If still profitable, then yes.
B) Yes—passed all walk-forward tests, robust strategy
C) Yes—walk-forward validation is sufficient, costs don't matter much
D) No—need at least 10 walk-forward periods to validate
Correct! Walk-forward tests prove robustness (not overfit to single period), but you MUST model realistic costs before trading live. Slippage (0.1-0.2% per side = 0.2-0.4% round-trip) plus commissions ($2-5/trade) come off the top of every trade, and they come off in R terms too: on a trade risking $500, $150 of costs is 0.3R gone before the market does anything. A backtest at +0.5R can land near zero once that is real. Chris's case above is this exact failure — a +1.3R backtest that came in at -0.45R live once the costs were real. Add costs LAST (after walk-forward), then ask whether the expectancy is still positive by a margin you could defend, and whether you can say what produces it. A small, explicable edge that survives costs beats a large one that does not.

In backtesting, perfection is suspicion. Realism is edge. Model costs, avoid overfitting, forward test—or fail live.
