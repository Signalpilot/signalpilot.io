Cross-Market Correlation Analysis: Bonds, Dollar, Commodities

Stocks don't trade in a vacuum. When the 10-year yield spikes 30 basis points, SPX will react. When the dollar breaks support, gold will rally. Professional traders monitor these relationships constantly—because the move that hits equities is often visible in another market first.

Retail traders stare at SPY charts all day, wondering why it suddenly dropped 2%. Meanwhile, the bond market was screaming "risk-off" for three hours. High-yield credit spreads were widening. The dollar was spiking. Copper was tanking. All the warnings were there—just not on the SPY chart.

Professional traders watch bonds, dollar, commodities, credit spreads, and volatility alongside equities. These markets are interconnected through capital flows, correlation arbitrage, and systematic strategies. When correlations break—when stocks rally but bonds refuse to sell off—that's a divergence signal worth trading.

Part 1: Core Intermarket Correlations

Understanding Asset Class Relationships

Markets don't move in isolation. Capital flows between asset classes based on correlations that persist across decades. Understanding these relationships gives you an edge: when one market moves, you can predict what happens next in another.

The Four Critical Correlations

1. Stocks vs Bonds (the correlation whose sign changes)

Start here, because almost every version of this you will read gets it wrong. The stock/bond correlation does not have a fixed sign. It has a sign that depends on which kind of shock is driving the market, and it has spent long stretches of history in each state.

When growth is the dominant worry, the correlation is negative. Bad growth news hurts earnings and helps bonds, because slower growth means lower policy rates. Stocks down, bonds up. This is the state that held for most of 2000 to 2020, which is why a generation of writing describes it as "normal", and it is the state that makes a 60/40 portfolio work.

When inflation and rates are the dominant worry, the correlation flips positive. A hot inflation print raises the discount rate applied to equities AND the yield demanded on bonds. Both fall together. This is what happened through 2022, when the S&P and long Treasuries fell in the same year for the first time in decades and 60/40 had its worst year in a century. It is also what happened in the case study below, in the autumn of 2023.

So the practical question is never "are they inversely correlated?" It is "what is the market frightened of this week?" If the headline risk is a recession, expect the inverse relationship and treat a break from it as a warning. If the headline risk is inflation, rates, or a fiscal shock, expect them to move together and do not read that as an emergency.

Trading signal, stated conditionally:

  • In a growth-shock regime: stocks falling while yields rise is the anomaly, and usually means forced selling (March 2020)
  • In a rates-shock regime: stocks falling while yields rise is the ordinary case, and the yield move is the thing driving the equity move rather than a divergence from it

Key tickers: SPY (stocks) vs TLT (20Y bonds) or ^TNX (10Y yield). Rather than memorising a sign, plot the rolling 60-day correlation between SPY and TLT once and look at it every few weeks. You will see it cross zero. Knowing which side of zero you are on is worth more than any rule in this lesson.

2. Stocks vs Dollar (Inverse Correlation -0.5 to -0.8)

Normal relationship: When dollar strengthens (DXY up), stocks fall. When dollar weakens (DXY down), stocks rise.

Why:

  • Strong dollar = U.S. multinationals earn less overseas (currency headwind)
  • Strong dollar = emerging markets debt crisis (EM borrowing in USD becomes expensive)
  • Strong dollar = risk-off (flight to safety, sell equities)

What to take from this, and what not to:

  • The mechanism above is the part to keep: a stronger dollar really does compress foreign earnings translated back into dollars, and really does tighten conditions for anyone borrowing in dollars
  • Fixed levels do not survive contact with the record. DXY sat above 105 for long stretches of 2023 and 2024 while the S&P made new highs, so "above 105 = bearish" would have kept you out of two good years. The level is not the signal
  • What is worth watching is the rate of change, and whether equities are disagreeing with it. A fast dollar move that equities ignore is an observation; a dollar level is not

Example: September 2022. DXY broke 110 (multi-decade high). SPY crashed -8% in 4 weeks. Dollar strength = stocks weakness.

3. Dollar vs Gold (Inverse Correlation -0.7 to -0.9)

Normal relationship: When dollar rises, gold falls. When dollar falls, gold rises.

Why: Gold is priced in USD. Stronger dollar = gold becomes more expensive for foreign buyers = demand falls. Weaker dollar = gold cheaper for foreigners = demand rises.

Trading signal:

  • DXY and GLD both rising = UNUSUAL → inflation hedge demand (stagflation fears)
  • DXY and GLD both falling = risk-on (sell safety, buy equities)

Worth noticing: when DXY and gold both rally, the usual inverse has broken, and the two things normally bid for opposite reasons are being bid together. That is an observation about what the market is frightened of, not a forecast for equities.

4. Stocks vs Commodities (Copper, Oil)

Copper vs Stocks (+0.6 to +0.8 correlation, LEADING indicator):

Copper = "Dr. Copper" because it predicts economic growth. It's used in construction, manufacturing, electronics. High copper demand = strong economy = stocks follow UP. Low copper demand = weak economy = stocks follow DOWN.

Lead time, honestly: copper often turns before equities, and the lead is anything from a few days to several months. It is not 2-4 weeks; that number gets repeated because it sounds tradeable. In 2024 copper peaked on 20 May and the S&P went on to make new highs into mid-July before it broke — a two-month lead, during which selling on the copper signal would have cost you an 8% rally. Treat a copper breakdown as a reason to stop adding and to check your other indicators, not as a date stamp.

Oil vs Stocks (complex relationship):

  • Moderate oil rise (+10-20%) = BULLISH for stocks (economic growth signal)
  • Oil spike (+50%+) = BEARISH for stocks (inflation threat, consumer spending hit)
  • Oil crash (-30%+) = BEARISH for stocks (recession signal, demand destruction)

What to do with it: a copper breakdown is a reason to stop adding and to look at your other readings. It is not a date, because the lead is not a fixed length — see the paragraph above, where a two-month lead would have cost you an 8% rally.

Part 2: Correlation Regimes—When Diversification Fails

Understanding Correlation Regime Shifts

Correlations aren't static—they change based on market regime. In normal markets, correlations behave predictably. In crisis, everything moves together (high correlation). Understanding these regime shifts is critical for risk management.

High Correlation = Systemic Risk

Definition: When all asset classes move together (correlation → +1 or -1).

When It Happens:

  • Market crashes / forced liquidations (March 2020, October 2008)
  • Systematic strategies unwinding (volatility targeting funds, and risk parity funds, which size by volatility rather than by dollars)
  • Margin calls / deleveraging events

What Happens:

  • Stocks fall, bonds fall, gold falls, crypto falls, commodities fall
  • Only USD cash rallies (flight to ultimate liquidity)
  • Diversification FAILS (60/40 portfolios get crushed)

Example: March 2020 (COVID Crash)

March 9-18, 2020:
- SPY: -12%
- TLT (bonds): -8%
- GLD (gold): -13%
- BTC (crypto): -33%
- Oil: -35%

Correlation to SPY: Everything +0.9 correlation (all falling together)

Only winner: USD (DXY +7%)

Cause: Forced liquidation. Funds selling everything to raise cash.

Trading Rule for High Correlation Regimes — with the qualification that makes it usable:

  • Signal: SPY and TLT both down more than 1% on the same day, and the move is accompanied by the other signs of a liquidation: the dollar bid, gold down, credit gapping wider, everything moving at once.
  • Action: cut risk hard. A genuine forced-liquidation day is not a place to be leveraged.
  • Re-entry: when the cross-asset moves stop being uniform — when some things go up on a down day again.

Why the qualification matters more than the rule. The rule as usually stated is simple: SPY and TLT both down 1% means exit everything and go to cash until the correlation normalises, usually within one to four weeks. Applied to 2022 that instruction would have put you in cash for most of two years and the correlation would never have "normalised", because nothing was broken. Stocks and bonds fell together all year for a straightforward reason: the discount rate was rising. That is a regime, not a liquidation. The two look identical on a single day and are told apart by what else is happening — in a liquidation, the dollar and cash are the only things bid and the moves are indiscriminate; in a rates repricing, the selling is orderly, credit behaves, and gold often holds.

Low Correlation = Alpha Opportunity

Definition: When assets trade on individual fundamentals (correlation → 0).

When It Happens:

  • Normal market environments (60-70% of time)
  • Low volatility regimes (VIX <18)
  • Stock-picker markets (2017, 2019, H2 2023)

What Happens:

  • Individual stocks trade on earnings, fundamentals, sector trends
  • Some stocks up +10%, others down -10% (dispersion high)
  • Active managers can generate alpha (outperform index)

Trading Strategy:

  • Low correlation: your positions are closer to being separate bets, so the same number of them carries less concentrated risk than it does in a high-correlation regime
  • High correlation = reduce position sizing: Everything moving together = no edge. Go defensive, wait for regime shift.

📉 CASE STUDY: Sarah's $89K Cross-Market Blindness (Sept-Oct 2023)

Trader: Sarah Martinez (composite example), 6-year equity trader from Chicago ($180,000 account)

Markets: Short-dated SPY and QQQ call options (she expressed every dip-buy as leveraged premium, which is why a -7.7% move in SPY cost her half the account)

Fatal flaw: Only looked at SPY charts, completely ignored bond yields, dollar, and cross-market warnings

Disaster period: September 1 - October 27, 2023 (8 weeks of yield spike)

Result: Lost $89,000 (-49.4%) buying SPY dips while 10Y yields screamed "SELL"

What changed: a daily cross-market checklist, run before looking at a SPY chart. No result is quoted for the months that followed; four months of one account cannot show that a checklist works

The Disaster Timeline: Ignoring the Bond Market's Warning

Sarah's Pattern (12 trades over 8 weeks, 1 of them a winner, -$89K)

The approach: Sarah was a pure equity trader. She only watched SPY and QQQ charts. She didn't track bonds, yields, the dollar, or credit spreads. If the information wasn't on her TradingView charts, she didn't see it.

What was actually happening (Sept-Oct 2023):

  • 10Y Treasury yield spiked from 4.10% to 4.95% (+85 bps in 8 weeks — a large move, though 2022 produced several larger ones)
  • Over this stretch the yield move was what equities were pricing off — which Part 1 is careful to call a property of a rates-driven regime rather than a law
  • TLT (bond ETF) fell -8.2% (bonds selling off hard)
  • DXY (dollar) rose +4.1% (strengthening, risk-off)
  • Copper down -7.3% (growth concerns)
  • Every one of those readings was public, daily and free. None of them were on her charts

Examples of cross-market blindness:

  • Sept 7 (SPY $447): Sarah put $40K of premium into three-week SPY $450 calls ("RSI oversold, great dip!"). What she missed: 10Y yield jumped 4.10% → 4.28% (+18 bps in 1 week), TLT down -2.1%, dollar up +1.3%. SPY fell to $438 and the calls went from near-the-money to 2.7% out. She sold at 60 cents on the dollar. Loss: -$16,000.
  • Sept 15 (SPY $438): Sarah put $35K into SPY $440 calls ("Support holding"). What she missed: 10Y yield at 4.35%, TLT down -1.8%. SPY fell to $428. Loss: -$14,000.
  • Sept 22 (SPY $428): Sarah doubled down with $45K of SPY $430 calls ("It has to bounce here!"). What she missed: 10Y yield at 4.48% (historic spike), TLT down -2.4%, copper down -3.1%. SPY fell to $420. Loss: -$18,900.
  • Sept 29 (SPY $420): Sarah went all-in with $50K of SPY $422 calls ("Max oversold!"). What she missed: 10Y yield at 4.68%, DXY up +2.1%, credit spreads widening. SPY fell to $413. Loss: -$21,500.
  • Oct 6 (SPY $423): Sarah closed what was left. The other eight trades — smaller sizes, and one of them her single winner — had cost another $18,600 between them. Account: $91,000 (down -49.4% from $180K). What she missed the entire time: 10Y yield at 4.88% (near 5% = psychological resistance for equities).

Result: 12 trades, 1 winner, -$89K (-49.4%). Sarah kept "buying dips" based on SPY RSI while 10Y yields spiked +85 bps - the real driver of the selloff.

The breaking point (Oct 6): "I just closed everything. I lost $89K in 8 weeks buying SPY dips with calls. Every dip kept dipping. I don't understand—RSI was oversold EVERY time. My friend asked, 'Did you check bond yields?' I said, 'Why would I? I trade stocks, not bonds.' He showed me a chart: 10Y yield went from 4.1% to 4.9% during my 8-week losing streak. He said, 'Right now, rising yields are what is moving stocks. You were fighting the bond market.' I had no idea yields mattered this much."

The Realization and Recovery

What Sarah Learned (Oct 2023)

The realization: Sarah stopped trading for 2 weeks after she closed the account down. She studied cross-market correlations, and what she came back with was not a list of relationships but a way of sizing them:

  • Give each reading a size: divide the move in the asset by the move in the 10-year, in percentage points, and you have its sensitivity. If your hedge's number matches the number for what you are hedging, the hedge is a second copy of the position.

🧮 Give the bond market's warning a size

Everything above says the bond market was screaming. Screaming how loudly? The episode has the numbers to answer that, and the answer is a single division that turns the warning into a quantity you can put next to a position.

Over the eight weeks the 10-year went from 4.10% to 4.95%, a rise of 0.85 percentage points, and SPY fell 7.7%. Divide: 7.7 / 0.85 = 9.1. The S&P moved about 9% for every one percentage point of yield, or a little under 1% for every 10 basis points. That is the sensitivity, and it is the number Sarah needed and did not have.

Check it against her first trade. On 7 September the 10-year jumped 18 basis points in a week. Multiply: 1.8 x 0.9 is about 1.6%, so the arithmetic said SPY should fall roughly a percent and a half. It fell from $447 to $438, which is 2.0%. Not exact, and it does not need to be — it needed only to be enough to tell her that buying calls struck above the money was betting against the one variable that was actually driving price.

Now the part that explains why nothing in her book helped. Run the same division on the hedge. TLT fell 8.2% over the same 85 basis points: 8.2 / 0.85 = 9.6. The bond ETF had an effective duration of about 9.6 and the S&P was behaving like 9.1. They were the same trade.

10-year yield        4.10%  ->  4.95%     +0.85 points

SPY   -7.7%   /  0.85  =  9.1     sensitivity to yields
TLT   -8.2%   /  0.85  =  9.6     sensitivity to yields

That is what a positive stock/bond correlation means once you put a number on it: not that diversification is weaker, but that the two holdings have become the same exposure with different tickers. So do this division whenever the regime is in doubt. Take any recent stretch, divide each holding's percentage move by the yield move in percentage points, and compare the answers. If your hedge's number is close to the number for the thing it is supposed to hedge, you are not hedged — you are doubled.

What she took from it: look at the other markets before the SPY chart, not after. When several of them disagree with the trade, the equity setup is the only thing in the room arguing for it — which is a reason to stand aside, not a reason to take the other side.

Recovery (Nov 2023 - Feb 2024)

What changed: Sarah built a daily cross-market dashboard. She tracked 10Y yields, TLT, DXY, copper, HYG, VIX every morning BEFORE looking at SPY charts.

What the checklist actually said over the following weeks, which you can verify yourself:

  • Third week of October 2023: the 10-year yield touched 5% and turned. TLT stopped falling. For the first time in eight weeks the rates line was pointing the other way.
  • Late November: the 10-year was back to about 4.4%, TLT had rallied hard, DXY was falling, copper had turned up. Every item on the list agreed for the first time all autumn.
  • December 13: the Fed's dot plot showed cuts for 2024. The 10-year fell through 4% and finished the year near 3.88%.

What happened next, and why no return figure is reported for it

Before (ignoring cross-market, Sept-Oct 2023): 12 trades, 1 winner, -$89K, $180K → $91K (-49%)

After: she stopped trading premium and traded shares, and she was long through the stretch from late October 2023 into the spring. That stretch was one of the strongest four-month runs in the index's recent history — the S&P gained roughly a fifth of its value. Almost any long position held through it made money, which is exactly why no percentage is quoted here: a four-month return earned inside that rally measures the rally, not the checklist. What the checklist can be credited with is the timing of the switch from short-dated calls to shares, and the fact that she was not still buying dips in September.

✅ Sarah's Core Lesson

Sarah's advice: "I lost $89K in 8 weeks because I only looked at SPY charts. I ignored the bond market, which was screaming 'SELL STOCKS' the entire time. 10Y yields spiked from 4.1% to 4.9% (+85 bps) and I didn't even know it was happening. I kept buying SPY dips based on RSI, not understanding that the yield move WAS the selloff. I'd have told you at the time that bonds and stocks are inversely correlated, which is what everyone says. In the autumn of 2023 they were not. They were falling together, because the same thing — the discount rate — was hurting both.

What saved me: Building a daily cross-market checklist. Every morning BEFORE looking at SPY, I check: 10Y yields, TLT, DXY, copper, HYG, VIX. If 3+ are bearish, I don't buy dips - I go defensive or short. I made some of it back over the following four months, in a market that went almost straight up, so I try not to give myself much credit for that part.

The lesson? You can't trade SPY in a vacuum. Stocks don't move because of RSI or moving averages - they move because of MACRO forces: yields, dollar, commodities, credit spreads. Learn to read the cross-market signals. They'll save your account."

Case Study Quiz: Sarah lost $89K (-49.4%) in 8 weeks buying SPY dips while 10Y yields spiked from 4.10% to 4.95% (+85 bps). She had an 8% win rate (1 out of 12 trades). What was her fatal mistake?

A) She used too much leverage—should have traded with smaller positions
B) Her technical analysis was flawed—RSI oversold is never a reliable buy signal
C) She only looked at SPY charts and completely ignored cross-market signals (10Y yields spiking, TLT falling, DXY rising, copper falling) that screamed "SELL STOCKS"
D) She should have diversified into other stocks instead of only trading SPY
Correct: C. The selloff had a driver, and it was not on her chart. Over those eight weeks the 10-year went from 4.10% to 4.95%; SPY fell 7.7%. Dividing one by the other gives the sensitivity the box above derives, and it is the number that told her what her calls were really a bet on. A is wrong because the sizing was consistent with her account — it was the instrument that magnified it. B is wrong because RSI was not lying: it was measuring something that had stopped being the thing that mattered.
Part 3: Divergences and Lead-Lag Relationships

Spotting Intermarket Warnings Before the Crowd

The most profitable signals come from divergences—when one market contradicts another. These breakdowns in normal correlations signal regime shifts, and they occur BEFORE major moves in equities.

Critical Divergence Signals

⚠️ Bearish Divergence

Signal: SPY making new highs, but HYG (high-yield bonds) making lower highs

Translation: Credit market sees risk that equity market doesn't. Institutions are reducing credit exposure.

Action: Reduce long exposure, buy puts, tighten stops

A caution about this signal, using the example that is usually offered for it: people often say credit warned before the COVID crash. It did not. High-yield spreads were near their cycle tights in January 2020, and HYG turned over within a day of SPY in the third week of February. Credit and equities broke together. Use the divergence when you can see it in the data in front of you; do not go looking for it in the past, because you will find it every time.

✅ Bullish Divergence

Signal: SPY making lower lows, but HYG holding support (credit spreads stable)

Translation: Credit market doesn't see systemic risk. Equity selloff is panic, not fundamental.

Action: Buy dips aggressively, credit is confirming bottom

Example: October 2023. SPY down about 10% from its July high. HYG fell too — roughly 5% — but that is the point: high-yield spreads widened only modestly while the 10-year yield moved 85 basis points. Credit was pricing a discount-rate problem, not a default problem. SPY bottomed in late October and rallied hard into year end. The signal was not "credit is flat"; it was "credit is falling far less than rates alone would explain."

Lead-Lag Relationships: Which Markets Move First

Some markets LEAD equity moves by days or weeks. If you monitor these leading indicators, you can position BEFORE SPY confirms the move.

Leading Indicator Lead Time What It Signals
Copper (HG_F) days to months Economic growth / contraction
High-Yield Bonds (HYG) 1-3 weeks Credit risk / default concerns
Semiconductors (SMH) 1-2 weeks Tech sector health / cyclical demand
Transportation (IYT) 1-2 weeks Economic activity / shipping demand
Financials (XLF) Concurrent Economic confidence / lending conditions

Trading application: if copper breaks down 5% while SPY is flat, treat it as a reason to stop adding exposure and to look harder at the rest of the list — not as a countdown. The lead times in the table above are typical ranges from the episodes people write about, which is not the same as a distribution you can size against. If you want to use them, count the last twenty instances on your own charts first and see how many resolved inside the stated window. Most people who quote these numbers have never done that.

Part 4: Two Worked Examples on Real Market Data

Putting It All Together

The two setups below are reconstructions on real prices, not a record of trades anyone placed. Every level and date in them can be checked against a chart, which is the point: the argument is that the cross-market signals were visible before the equity move, and you should be able to verify that claim yourself rather than take it on trust. What they cannot show you is the trades where the same signals lined up and the market went the other way, so read them as illustrations of the reasoning, not as evidence of a hit rate.

Trade #1: Shorting SPX on Dollar Breakout (Sept 2022)

Setup (Sept 13, 2022):

  • DXY holding above 110 (multi-decade resistance broken the week before)
  • 10Y yield breaks 3.5% (rising rapidly, +15 bps/week)
  • SPX at 3950 (the rally off the August lows has just failed on the August CPI print)

Intermarket Signals:

  • Dollar strength: DXY +4% in a month (tightening financial conditions)
  • Yield spike: 10Y from 2.5% → 3.5% in 6 weeks (repricing equity valuations lower)
  • Credit weakness: HYG making new lows (not confirming SPX rally attempt)

Trade:

  • Entry: Short SPX at 3950 (Sept 13)
  • Stop: 4150 (above the Sept 12 high of 4119)
  • Target 1: 3750 (previous low)
  • Target 2: 3600 (measured move from DXY breakout)

Outcome:

Sept 23: SPX breaks 3750 (Target 1 hit)
Sept 30: SPX hits 3585 (Target 2 hit)

What the reconstruction shows: the dollar breakout and the
yield move were both visible on 13 September, before the index broke.
Whether a position taken on them would have been held to either target
is not something this page can tell you.

Trade #2: Buying Stocks on Credit Spread Compression (Nov 2023)

Setup (Nov 1, 2023):

  • SPX at 4200 (down -9% from the July high of 4607)
  • HYG (high-yield bonds) bottoming, spreads compressing
  • DXY breaking support (dollar weakness = bullish for equities)
  • 10Y yield peaked at 5%, now falling to 4.5%

Intermarket Signals:

  • Credit improving: HYG +2% while SPX flat (credit leading equities higher)
  • Dollar weakness: DXY -3% in 2 weeks (bullish for stocks)
  • Yield relief: 10Y falling -50 bps (discount rate tailwind for equities)

Trade:

  • Entry: Long SPX at 4225 (Nov 1)
  • Stop: 4100 (below recent low)
  • Target: 4500 (previous resistance)

Outcome:

Nov 30: SPX at 4550 (Target hit)
Dec 31: SPX at 4770 (extended rally)

What the reconstruction shows: credit, the dollar and the
yield were all pointing the same way on 1 November, before the index
turned. It does not show you the times they pointed that way and it
did not turn.

Part 5: Building Your Daily Intermarket Checklist

Daily Monitoring Routine (10-15 minutes)

Step 1: Check Core Correlations

  • SPY vs TLT: Moving inversely (normal) or together (stress)?
  • DXY: Breaking resistance (bearish SPX) or support (bullish SPX)?
  • 10Y yield: Rate of change >20bps in week? (Volatility warning)

Step 2: Check Credit Market

  • HYG vs SPY: Aligned or diverging?
  • HYG/SPY ratio: Making new 3-month low = bearish divergence

Step 3: Check Commodities

  • Copper (HG or COPX): Falling while stocks rally = bearish
  • Gold: Following dollar inverse correlation?
  • Oil: Supply shock overriding fundamentals?

Step 4: Check Volatility

  • VIX: Elevated despite stock rally = fade rally
  • CBOE Skew: >150 = extreme fear (reversal)

Step 5: Use Signal Pilot Tools

  • Janus Atlas: Overlay SPY, TLT, DXY, GLD on one chart → visual divergence detection
  • Pentarch Pilot Line: Check institutional order flow in bonds vs stocks
  • Plutus Flow: Unusual options activity in TLT, GLD, DXY (hedging signals)

Key Takeaways

  • Stocks and bonds falling together is an inflation-and-rates regime far more often than it is a systemic event. It ran for the whole of 2022 and again in the autumn of 2023, which is the period this lesson's own case study covers. Read it as "my hedge is not hedging", not as an exit signal
  • Dollar strength is a headwind, not a level — DXY sat above 105 through 2023 and 2024 while the S&P made new highs, so read the rate of change and whether equities are disagreeing with it, not the number
  • Credit is worth watching and is not an oracle — Part 3 works through the COVID example everyone cites for it, where credit and equities in fact broke together
  • Copper turns before equities and does not tell you when — in 2024 it led by two months, and the index gained 8% in between
  • Yield rate of change matters—10Y spiking >20 bps/week triggers SPX volatility
  • When everything moves together, your diversification is not doing what you think it is — which is an argument for smaller size, not for cash. A reader who went to cash on this rule in early 2022 and waited for the correlation to normalise sat out two years

🎯 Practice Exercises

  2. HYG/SPY Ratio Study: Calculate HYG/SPY ratio daily for last year. Mark when ratio made new 3-month low. Did SPX correct within next 4 weeks? Calculate hit rate.
  3. Dollar Breakout Backtest: Find last 5 times DXY broke multi-month resistance (+3%). How did SPX perform over next 30 days? What was average drawdown?
  4. Credit Divergence Hunt: Review 2022 bear market. When did HYG start falling relative to SPX? How many weeks did credit lead equities lower?
  5. Build Your Checklist: Create daily spreadsheet tracking: SPY, TLT, DXY, HYG, Copper, VIX. Note when divergences appear. Track how long until divergence resolves.

📝 Knowledge Check

Test your understanding of cross-market correlation analysis:

SPY is rallying +2% on the day, approaching all-time highs. You check your cross-market dashboard: TLT (20Y Treasury ETF) is ALSO up +1.5% (yields falling sharply). DXY (dollar) is flat. What does this signal?

A) Warning divergence—stocks and bonds both up is unusual, suggests flight to safety or Fed pivot fears, stay cautious
B) Extreme bullish confirmation—both stocks and bonds rallying means huge risk-on, buy more
C) Irrelevant—bonds and stocks can both rally anytime, no signal here
Correct: A. Stocks and bonds rallying together is a DIVERGENCE that signals regime uncertainty. Here's why: Normal regime: Stocks UP = Bonds DOWN (investors sell safe assets, buy risk assets, yields rise). Normal inverse correlation = -0.5 to -0.7. When BOTH rally simultaneously, it means one of two things: (1) Fed pivot expectations (market pricing rate cuts → bonds rally on lower yields, stocks rally on easier financial conditions), OR (2) Flight to quality starting (institutions buying BOTH safe havens, SPY rally is late-cycle blow-off top). Historical examples: (1) November 2023: SPY rallied +8.9% on the month and TLT rallied +9.5% alongside it. Why? The market priced in the end of the hiking cycle (dovish pivot). This WAS bullish—confirmed by the rally continuing through year-end. (2) January 2020 (pre-COVID): SPY made new highs, TLT also rallied. Why? Institutions hedging tail risk while staying long equities. Within 6 weeks, COVID crash began. What it is worth: the two explanations above cannot be told apart on the day, which is a reason to be smaller and to watch which one resolves. If the bond move reverses, it was the pivot; if equities stall while bonds keep rallying, it was the hedge.

You're watching copper futures (HG). Copper drops -8% over 2 weeks (from $4.50 to $4.14/lb), breaking below key support at $4.20. Meanwhile, SPY is consolidating near highs at $560, only down -1%. What's the trade?

A) Ignore copper—commodities don't predict stocks, stay fully long SPY
B) Reduce SPY exposure by 30-50%—copper is a leading indicator, SPY will likely follow lower within 2-4 weeks
C) Buy copper futures—it's oversold and will bounce, ignoring the SPY setup
Correct: B. Copper breaking key support while SPY holds near highs is a classic LEADING INDICATOR divergence. Copper is called "Dr. Copper" because it tends to turn before equities do. The lead is genuinely variable — days to months — so read it as sequence, not as schedule. Here's the mechanic: Copper demand = global manufacturing + construction + infrastructure. When copper drops sharply, it signals: (1) Weakening industrial demand (China slowdown, U.S. manufacturing contraction), (2) Reduced capital expenditures by corporations, (3) Economic growth deceleration. Corporate earnings follow 4-8 weeks later. Historical examples: (1) 2024: copper peaked at about $5.20 on 20 May and fell to roughly $4.10 by early August (-21%). SPY peaked at about $565 on 16 July and fell to about $520 by 5 August (-8%). Copper led, but by two months — and the index gained 8% between the copper top and its own. That is the honest version of this example, and it is the reason the answer above is "reduce and hedge" rather than "go flat": a signal that is right about direction and unreliable about timing has to be traded with something left on the table. (2) Feb-March 2020: Copper started falling in late January (COVID supply chain fears). Dropped from $2.80 in mid-January to $2.55 by mid-February, and on to $2.10 by mid-March. SPY didn't crash until late Feb. Copper gave a 3-week head start. Why the sequence is worth watching: copper is a bet on industrial demand being placed now, whereas an earnings report describes a quarter that has already ended. That is a real informational advantage and it is why the sequence is worth watching. What it is not is a clock. Copper told you first; it did not tell you when.

DXY (U.S. Dollar Index) breaks out above 106 (multi-month resistance) and rallies to 108 in 5 days (+2%). SPY is at $550. 10-year yield is at 4.3% (stable). What's the likely impact on SPY over the next 2-4 weeks?

A) Bullish for SPY—strong dollar means strong U.S. economy, buy the breakout
B) Neutral—dollar and SPY are uncorrelated, no impact
C) Bearish for SPY—dollar strength hurts multinational earnings and signals risk-off, expect SPY to drop 3-5%
Correct: C. DXY breakout above major resistance is BEARISH for SPY. Here's the correlation: Normal relationship: DXY UP = SPY DOWN (historical correlation: -0.6). Why? (1) Stronger dollar hurts S&P 500 multinational earnings (foreign sales are roughly 30% of S&P 500 revenue — the 40% figure you will see quoted everywhere is out of date). When USD rises, foreign revenue converts to fewer dollars → earnings miss. (2) Dollar strength = risk-off flows. Investors sell risk assets (stocks, emerging markets) and buy safe haven (USD, Treasuries). (3) Stronger dollar = tighter financial conditions (harder for emerging markets to service USD-denominated debt → global growth slows). Historical examples: (1) 2022: DXY rallied from about 95 to 99 (+4%) over the first quarter as the Fed prepared to hike, and kept going to 105 by May. SPY fell from about $480 at the start of January to about $410 by mid-May, roughly -15% over those four and a half months — not inside the quarter, which is worth noting because the dollar signal was early rather than coincident. Dollar strength was a CLEAR warning. (2) March 2020 COVID crash: DXY spiked from 95 → 103 (+8%) in 2 weeks during global liquidation. SPY crashed from $340 → $220 (-35%). When DXY breaks out sharply, it's a flight-to-safety signal. What to check next: HYG. If credit is falling while the dollar rallies, two independent readings agree and the case is stronger. If credit is steady, the dollar move is doing less work than it looks like it is.

Correlations reveal market structure. When they break, opportunities emerge. Trade the divergence, not the relationship.
