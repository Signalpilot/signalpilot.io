Trading Around Scheduled Events

Reading time ~13 min • Module 5: Context •

Some moves are scheduled. Earnings, central bank decisions, inflation prints — the market knows the date and the hour in advance, and prices the uncertainty into options before the event arrives.

That last part is what most traders miss. If a stock is going to move violently on Thursday morning and everybody knows it, the movement is not free. It has a price, that price is quoted, and you can read it off the screen before you take the trade.

💸 The bar you actually have to clear

A stock at $100 with a $6 straddle into earnings is telling you the market expects roughly a 6% move. Buy that straddle and the stock must finish beyond $94 or $106 before you make a cent. A 5% move — a huge day by any ordinary standard — loses money. “I was right about the direction and still lost” is not bad luck here; it is the contract working exactly as priced.

What the calendar is worth

There is no edge in knowing a company reports on Thursday. The date is on their website. Whatever edge exists lives in the gap between what the market has priced and what actually happens — and to have any view on that gap you first have to read what has been priced, which almost nobody does before clicking.

This lesson builds on concepts from:

  • Lesson 44: Fed Policy & Liquidity Cycles — the macro events on this calendar

Part 1: Reading the Priced Move

The at-the-money straddle — one call and one put at the strike nearest the current price, expiring just after the event — is the market’s quote for how far the stock is likely to travel, in either direction, by that expiry:

implied move  =  (ATM call + ATM put)  /  current price

break-even range  =  price x (1 - implied move)   to   price x (1 + implied move)

That is the whole calculation. It takes ten seconds on any option chain and it reframes the trade completely:

Price Straddle Implied move The event has to beat
$100$6.006.00%below $94.00 or above $106.00
$50$4.258.50%below $45.75 or above $54.25
$250$11.004.40%below $239.00 or above $261.00

💡 What this makes visible

The middle row is a stock the market expects to move 8.5% on a single announcement. Any directional option trade there is not a bet that the stock rises — it is a bet that it rises more than 8.5%. Those are wildly different propositions and the option chain has been telling you so all along.

It also explains the most common complaint in retail options trading. The stock rose 4%, the call expired worthless, and the trader concluded the market was rigged. The market was not rigged; it was quoted, and 4% was inside the quote.

Part 2: Why the Premium Collapses Afterwards

Option prices carry the uncertainty of everything between now and expiry. A scheduled event is a large, dated lump of that uncertainty. Once the announcement is out, the lump is gone — whatever the news was — and the options reprice down hard, immediately, at the open.

This is why a directional option bought the day before an event faces two forces at once: it needs the move to exceed what was priced, and it is fighting the collapse in premium that happens regardless of direction. Being right on direction and wrong on magnitude loses. Being right on both, but only slightly, can still lose.

📊 The honest framing of the trade

Buying options into an event is a bet that realised movement exceeds implied movement. Selling them is the opposite bet. Neither is inherently the good side, and anyone telling you one of them is a reliable income stream is selling something — the seller collects a premium most of the time and pays for it on the occasions the move is enormous, which is a payoff shape, not an edge.

What makes it an edge, if anything does, is a repeatable view on that gap for instruments you know well. That view is built from a record, which is Part 4.

Part 3: Scheduled Versus Surprise

Everything above applies to events with a date. A genuine surprise — an unexpected announcement, a headline mid-session — behaves differently in one crucial way: there was no priced expectation to beat, so the move is not competing against anything, and the repricing happens in the trade rather than before it.

That difference is worth holding onto because it cuts against intuition:

  • Scheduled events are the ones you can prepare for, and the ones where preparation is worth least, because everyone else prepared too and it is in the price.
  • Surprises are the ones you cannot prepare for, and where the biggest dislocations happen — but they arrive with halts, spreads several times normal, and the execution problems from Lesson 13.

The practical consequence for most traders is not a strategy but a decision: whether to hold through scheduled events at all. Holding through means accepting a gap that ignores your stop, in exchange for a move that has already been priced. Standing aside costs you the days on the calendar and nothing else. Both are defensible; drifting into the first because you did not check the calendar is not.

Part 4: Where Signal Pilot Fits

The implied move comes from the option chain and needs no indicator. Where the tools help is the part that surrounds the event rather than the event itself.

  • Harmonic Oscillator classifies the regime you are entering the event in. The same earnings reaction behaves differently in a trending market than in a mean-reverting one, and that context is not in the straddle price.
  • Volume Oracle shows the order flow into the reopening after a release. Whether the first print is a real market or one order clearing a thin book decides whether the level it sets means anything.

Neither tells you what an announcement will say, and no tool does. What they address is whether the market you are trading into afterwards is one you can execute in.

Quiz: Test Your Understanding

Q1: A stock trades at $80. The ATM call is $3.20 and the ATM put is $3.60 for the expiry just after earnings. What is the implied move?

A) 4.0% — the average of the two option prices over the stock price
B) 8.5% — the two premiums sum to $6.80, which is 8.5% of $80
C) 4.5% — the put is the larger premium, so it sets the expectation
D) It cannot be read from option prices

Correct: B. ($3.20 + $3.60) ÷ $80 = 8.5%. A is the most common error and it halves the number, which flatters every directional trade you are considering by exactly a factor of two. The straddle is the sum because you are pricing a move in either direction. The slight excess in the put over the call is ordinary skew, not a directional forecast.

Q2: You buy a call into earnings. The stock rises 3% on the announcement, and the call loses money. What happened?

A) The trade was executed badly
B) 3% was inside the implied move, and the event premium collapsed once the news was out
C) Market makers repriced the option against you deliberately
D) The rise must have happened after the option expired

Correct: B. Both forces went the wrong way at once: the move failed to exceed what was priced, and the uncertainty premium that had been supporting the option disappeared the moment the announcement landed. This is the ordinary outcome of a correct directional call that was not large enough, and it is fully visible in advance from the straddle. C is worth rejecting because it attributes to intent something that is mechanical: the premium falls because the uncertainty it was pricing no longer exists.

Q3: What is the main practical difference between a scheduled event and a genuine surprise?

A) Scheduled events produce larger moves
B) A scheduled event has a priced expectation to beat; a surprise does not, and reprices during the trade
C) Surprises can be hedged, scheduled events cannot
D) There is no meaningful difference in how they trade

Correct: B. The expectation is the whole distinction. With a date on the calendar, the uncertainty is priced ahead of time and you are competing against that price. With a surprise there is nothing to beat, and the repricing happens while you are in the position — alongside halts and widened spreads. Note the counter-intuitive consequence: the events you can prepare for are the ones where preparation is worth least, because everyone else prepared as well.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Read the implied move before every event trade. Straddle divided by price, in ten seconds. It is the bar the event has to clear before you make anything.
  • A correct directional call can still lose. If the move is inside the implied range, being right about direction is not enough, and the option chain said so in advance.
  • Event premium collapses the moment the news is out, whatever the news is, because the dated uncertainty it was pricing has ceased to exist.
  • Buying is a bet that realised exceeds implied; selling is the reverse. Neither side is inherently good, and a strategy that wins most of the time is a payoff shape, not an edge.
  • Scheduled events are priced; surprises are not. The ones you can prepare for are the ones where preparation is worth least.
  • The real decision for most traders is whether to hold through events at all. Standing aside costs only the days on the calendar. Drifting into an event unchecked costs whatever the gap decides.

🎯 Practice Exercise: Ten Events, Implied Against Realised

Objective: Find out whether events are worth trading for you — with evidence rather than recollection. No money required, and the record is worth more than any single trade.

Part 1: Pick five instruments you follow and find their next scheduled events — earnings dates, or the release calendar if you trade index products.

Part 2: Record the implied move the day before. Straddle divided by price, plus the two break-even prices. Write them down before the event. This is the part that must happen in advance to be worth anything.

Part 3: Record what actually happened. The next day, write the realised move as a percentage, and whether it landed inside or outside the range.

Part 4: After ten events, count. How often did the realised move exceed the implied one? If it is roughly half, you have learned that the pricing is fair on your instruments and that no simple directional approach will beat it. If it leans hard one way, you have found something worth investigating — and Lesson 55 tells you how many observations you would need before believing it.

Part 5: The decision. Write one sentence on whether you hold through scheduled events. Then put the next four dates for your instruments in your calendar, so that the sentence gets applied rather than remembered.

You can now price what an event has to beat before you take a position into it. The next lesson turns to volatility itself — not as a scheduled lump but as a regime, and as something that can be traded directly rather than survived.
